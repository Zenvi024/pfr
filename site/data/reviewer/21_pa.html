Parental Authority

Title IX, Family Code, Arts. 209–233 (syllabus XVI), with NCC 2180, Rule 130, Sec. 25 and P.D. 603. Who exercises parental authority, substitute and special parental authority and the liability that comes with it, the filial privilege, authority over the child's person and property, and suspension, termination and revival. Cases: Hontiveros, Unson, Exconde, Palisoc, Amadora, Salvosa, St. Mary's, Medina, Luna, Lindain, Chua v. Cabangbang; from the class reviewer, Eslao, Santos Sr., Briones, Vancil, Cuadra, Tamargo, Lee.

Concept; cannot be renounced (Arts. 209–210)

Art. 209, FC. Pursuant to the natural right and duty of parents over the person and property of their unemancipated children, parental authority and responsibility shall include the caring for and rearing them for civic consciousness and efficiency and the development of their moral, mental and physical character and well-being. (n)
Art. 210, FC. Parental authority and responsibility may not be renounced or transferred except in the cases authorized by law. (313a)
Eslao v. CA (G.R. No. 116773, January 16, 1997; class reviewer)

After her husband Reynaldo died, Maria Paz Cordero-Ouye left her daughter Angelica with her mother-in-law, Teresita Sagala-Eslao, who begged to keep the child to ease her grief. Maria Paz later married an American and returned to take Angelica with her; Teresita refused, claiming abandonment. Held: custody goes to the mother. Leaving the child with her grandmother was temporary and was not a renunciation. "Parental authority and responsibility are inalienable and may not be transferred or renounced except in cases authorized by law. The right attached to parental authority, being purely personal, the law allows a waiver of parental authority only in cases of adoption, guardianship and surrender to a children's home or an orphan institution."

Who exercises it (Arts. 211–213)

Art. 211, FC. The father and the mother shall jointly exercise parental authority over the persons of their common children. In case of disagreement, the father's decision shall prevail, unless there is a judicial order to the contrary.
Children shall always observe respect and reverence towards their parents and are obliged to obey them as long as the children are under parental authority. (311a)
Art. 212, FC. In case of absence or death of either parent, the parent present shall continue exercising parental authority. The remarriage of the surviving parent shall not affect the parental authority over the children, unless the court appoints another person to be the guardian of the person or property of the children. (n)
Art. 213, FC. In case of separation of the parents, parental authority shall be exercised by the parent designated by the Court. The Court shall take into account all relevant considerations, especially the choice of the child over seven years of age, unless the parent chosen is unfit.
No child under seven years of age shall be separated from the mother unless the court finds compelling reasons to order otherwise. (n)
Art. 176, FC (as amended by R.A. 9255, first sentence). Illegitimate children shall use the surname and shall be under the parental authority of their mother, and shall be entitled to support in conformity with this Code. …
Bar traps (custody): A mother's lesbian relationship (2024 Bar) or her work as an entertainer in a men's club (2023 Bar) is not, by itself, a compelling reason: there must be proof that it harms the child. The illegitimate child stays with the mother (Art. 176), and the tender-age rule adds to it. A biological father's offer to support mother and child if they live with him does not give him custody. Contrast Unson, where the mother's situation was an "obviously unwholesome, not to say immoral influence" on a girl at an impressionable age.
Unson v. Navarro (101 SCRA 183, 1980)

Edgar Unson and Edita Araneta separated; their daughter Maria Teresa stayed with her mother, who was then living with Agustin Uy, the husband of her own sister, by whom she had children. The father sought custody when the girl reached school age. Held: custody to the father; the mother gets visitorial rights only. "In all controversies regarding the custody of minors, the sole and foremost consideration is the physical, education, social and moral welfare of the child concerned, taking into account the respective resources and social and moral situations of the contending parents." The girl should be "freed from the obviously unwholesome, not to say immoral influence, that the situation in which private respondent has placed herself … might create in the moral and social outlook of Teresa who is now in her formative and most impressionable stage." "Decisions even of this Supreme Court on the custody of minor children are always open to adjustment as the circumstances relevant to the matter may demand."

Hontiveros v. IAC (132 SCRA 745, 1984)

A father contested the mother's custody of their minor child, claiming before the Supreme Court that she was unfit, after his own custody case below had been dismissed for his lack of interest. Held: the mother keeps custody. Unfitness is a question of fact that cannot be raised for the first time in a Rule 45 petition; "it is just too bad that the case for custody was dismissed for lack of interest on the part of the petitioner. Clearly, the private respondent has a clear legal right under Article 17 of P.D. 603 to the custody of her minor child, there being no compelling reason to the contrary."

Briones v. Miguel (G.R. No. 156343, October 18, 2004; class reviewer)

Joey Briones and Loreta Miguel, never married, had a son, Michael. Loreta worked in Japan and married a Japanese national; Joey sought custody by habeas corpus, arguing that he had recognized the child and could support him. Held: custody to the mother, visitation for the father. Under Art. 176, illegitimate children are under the parental authority of their mother; "the recognition of an illegitimate child by the father could be a ground for ordering the latter to give support to, but not custody of, the child." Rule 99, Sec. 6 (choice of a child over seven between separated parents) applies only to married parents, so it was deleted from the CA's ruling.

Tolentino (Arts. 211–213): Art. 211 formalized Filipino practice. The mother's authority is "subordinated, although not subsidiary" to the father's: in private, internal matters (education, moral guidance) they share equally, but in a conflict the father's decision prevails subject to a court order; in external, juridical matters the law itself prefers the father (administration of the child's property, Art. 225; liability for quasi-delicts under NCC 2180). The duty of obedience lasts only while the child is under parental authority; the duty of respect and reverence lasts for life, though after emancipation it has no sanction except, in grave cases, disinheritance. Other ascendants have no authority while the parents live and have not lost it; an aunt, by that relationship alone, is not entitled to custody (Ortiz v. Del Villar). Parental authority follows the parent abroad (national law). On separation, the welfare of the child controls (Perkins v. Perkins, 57 Phil. 217: custody of a girl over ten awarded to the father despite her preference for the mother, who had poisoned her mind against him and kept her in court hearings).
"Choice of the child over seven": Art. 213 says "over seven years of age"; Art. 102(6) and 129(9) (adjudication of the family dwelling) say "children below the age of seven years are deemed to have chosen the mother". A child of exactly seven is outside both the tender-age presumption and, on a strict reading, the "over seven" choice. On a quiz, quote the text.

Substitute parental authority (Arts. 214, 216, 217, 233)

Art. 214, FC. In case of death, absence or unsuitability of the parents, substitute parental authority shall be exercised by the surviving grandparent. In case several survive, the one designated by the court, taking into account the same consideration mentioned in the preceding article, shall exercise the authority. (355a)
Art. 216, FC. In default of parents or a judicially appointed guardian, the following persons shall exercise substitute parental authority over the child in the order indicated:
(1) The surviving grandparent, as provided in Art. 214;
(2) The oldest brother or sister, over twenty-one years of age, unless unfit or disqualified; and
(3) The child's actual custodian, over twenty-one years of age, unless unfit or disqualified.
Whenever the appointment of a judicial guardian over the property of the child becomes necessary, the same order of preference shall be observed. (349a, 351a, 354a)
Art. 217, FC. In case of foundlings, abandoned, neglected or abused children and other children similarly situated, parental authority shall be entrusted in summary judicial proceedings to heads of children's homes, orphanages and similar institutions duly accredited by the proper government agency. (314a)
Art. 233, FC. The person exercising substitute parental authority shall have the same authority over the person of the child as the parents.
In no case shall the school administrator, teacher or individual engaged in child care exercising special parental authority inflict corporal punishment upon the child. (n)
Uribe (CivRev Quiz 2, item 6; PFR Quiz 3, item 20; Samplex T/F 10): "In case of death, absence or unsuitability of the parents, special parental authority shall be exercised by the surviving grandparent." False: that is substitute parental authority (Arts. 214, 216). Special parental authority belongs to "teachers, administrators, the school" (Art. 218). A second version: "… substitute parental authority shall be exercised by the surviving grandparents." Also false: the law says "the surviving grandparent", and if several survive, the one designated by the court. Watch every letter.
Vancil v. Belmes (G.R. No. 132223, June 19, 2001; class reviewer)

Bonifacia Vancil, the paternal grandmother and an American citizen in Colorado, sought guardianship of her grandson Vincent against his mother, Helen Belmes, whom she called unfit because Vincent's sister had been abused by Helen's live-in partner. Held: the mother prevails. "The natural parents of the child have preferential natural and legal rights to the custody of the child"; substitute parental authority of the grandparent under Art. 214 arises only if the mother is first proved dead, absent or unsuitable, which was not shown. The grandmother's age, foreign residence and a prior conviction also made her an impractical guardian.

Santos, Sr. v. CA (G.R. No. 113054, March 16, 1995; class reviewer)

The maternal grandparents, who had cared for the boy since birth, resisted the father's claim to custody. Held: custody to the father. "Parental authority is primarily and exclusively vested in the parents, and substitute parental authority of grandparents may arise only when statutory conditions are met, namely, the death, absence, incapacity, or unfitness of both parents." The grandparents' greater means and affection are not enough.

Tolentino (Arts. 216–217): including grandparents and the oldest sibling conforms to Filipino custom. The Art. 216 order is mandatory unless special circumstances require otherwise: if the child's welfare is better served with the maternal grandparents, they prevail over the paternal grandparents. An "abandoned" child is one with no parental care or guardianship, or whose parents or guardians have deserted him for at least six months. Teachers have no power of corporal punishment; it belonged only to parents.
"Over twenty-one" in Art. 216: R.A. 6809 lowered the age of majority to 18 but did not amend Art. 216, which still says "over twenty-one years of age" for the oldest sibling and the actual custodian. Quote 21.

Special parental authority and liability for the minor's acts (Arts. 218–219, 221; NCC 2180)

Art. 218, FC. The school, its administrators and teachers, or the individual, entity or institution engaged in child care shall have special parental authority and responsibility over the minor child while under their supervision, instruction or custody.
Authority and responsibility shall apply to all authorized activities whether inside or outside the premises of the school, entity or institution. (349a)
Art. 219, FC. Those given the authority and responsibility under the preceding Article shall be principally and solidarily liable for damages caused by the acts or omissions of the unemancipated minor. The parents, judicial guardians or the persons exercising substitute parental authority over said minor shall be subsidiarily liable.
The respective liabilities of those referred to in the preceding paragraph shall not apply if it is proved that they exercised the proper diligence required under the particular circumstances.
All other cases not covered by this and the preceding articles shall be governed by the provisions of the Civil Code on quasi-delicts. (n)
Art. 221, FC. Parents and other persons exercising parental authority shall be civilly liable for the injuries and damages caused by the acts or omissions of their unemancipated children living in their company and under their parental authority subject to the appropriate defenses provided by law. (2180(2)a and (4)a)
Art. 2180, NCC (pars. 2, 3, 7, 8). The father and, in case of his death or incapacity, the mother, are responsible for the damages caused by the minor children who live in their company. Guardians are liable for damages caused by the minors or incapacitated persons who are under their authority and live in their company. … Lastly, teachers or heads of establishments of arts and trades shall be liable for damages caused by their pupils and students or apprentices, so long as they remain in their custody. The responsibility treated of in this article shall cease when the persons herein mentioned prove that they observed all the diligence of a good father of a family to prevent damage.
Exconde v. Capuno (101 Phil. 843, 1957)

Dante Capuno, 15, a student of Balintawak Elementary School, joined a school-ordered parade in honor of Dr. Jose Rizal. On the way he boarded a jeep, took the wheel, and the jeep turned over, killing two passengers, including Isidoro Caperiña. His father, Delfin Capuno, was sued with him. Held: the father is solidarily liable with his son; the school is not, because NCC 2180 then applied only to teachers or heads of "establishments of arts and trades", not academic schools. "The civil liability which the law imposes upon the father, and, in case of his death or incapacity, the mother, for any damages that may be caused by the minor children who live with them, is obvious. This is a necessary consequence of the parental authority they exercise over them…. The only way by which they can relieve themselves of this liability is if they prove that they exercised all the diligence of a good father of a family to prevent the damage", which Delfin failed to prove.

Palisoc v. Brillantes (41 SCRA 548, 1971)

Dominador Palisoc, 16, a student of the Manila Technical Institute, was fatally punched in the school laboratory by a classmate, Virgilio Daffon, during a quarrel, while their instructor was nearby. Held: the head of the school (Valenton), the instructor (Quibulue) and Daffon are solidarily liable. They "could have avoided the unfortunate death … had said defendants but complied with their duty of providing adequate supervision over the activities of the students in the school premises to protect their students from harm, whether at the hands of fellow students or other parties." "There is nothing in the law which prescribes that a student must be living and boarding with his teacher or in the school before heads and teachers of the school may be held liable for the tortious acts of their students." (Abandoning the contrary dictum in Mercado v. CA.)

Amadora v. CA (160 SCRA 315, 1988)

Alfredo Amadora, 17, a graduating student of Colegio de San Jose-Recoletos, went to the school auditorium a few days before graduation to submit his physics report, though classes had formally ended. A classmate, Pablito Daffon, shot and killed him there. Held: NCC 2180 now applies to all schools, academic or not; the teacher-in-charge of an academic school, and the head of a school of arts and trades, are liable. Alfredo "was in the custody of the authorities of the school notwithstanding classes had formally ended when the incident happened. It was immaterial if he was in the school auditorium to finish his physics requirement. What was important is that he was there for a legitimate purpose." But the rector, principal and dean of boys were not liable, "because none of them was the teacher-in-charge … Each was exercising only a general authority over the students and not direct control and influence," and no teacher-in-charge was shown negligent. The school itself is not directly liable under NCC 2180.

Salvosa v. IAC (166 SCRA 274, 1988)

Jimmy Abon, a commerce student and ROTC armorer of Baguio Colleges Foundation (a school of arts and trades), shot Napoleon Castro on the campus with a rifle he had taken from the armory, after school hours, when he was supposed to be at home. Held: the school and its officers are not liable. The rationale of NCC 2180 is that a student in the teacher's custody is one over whom the teacher "stands, to a certain extent, in loco parentis." "So long as (the students) remain in their custody" means "the protective and supervisory custody that the school and its heads and teachers exercise over the pupils and students for as long as they are at attendance in the school, including recess time." Abon "cannot be considered to have been 'at attendance in the school,' or in the custody of BCF, when he shot Napoleon Castro."

St. Mary's Academy v. Carpitanos (G.R. No. 143363, February 6, 2002)

During an enrollment drive organized by St. Mary's Academy, student Sherwin Carpitanos rode a jeep driven by another minor student, James Daniel II, 15; the jeep turned over and Sherwin died. The trial court held the school liable under Arts. 218–219. Held: the school is not liable. "Considering that the negligence of the minor driver or the detachment of the steering wheel guide of the jeep owned by respondent Villanueva was an event over which petitioner St. Mary's Academy had no control, and which was the proximate cause of the accident, petitioner may not be held liable for the death resulting from such accident." Liability under Art. 219 still requires that the school's negligence be the proximate cause of the injury.

Cuadra v. Monfort (35 SCRA 160, 1970; class reviewer)

Two Grade 6 girls were weeding a school garden as instructed by their teacher. Maria Teresa Monfort, in jest, tossed a piece of headband she said was a worm; it hit Maria Teresa Cuadra in the eye, which she rubbed, and she lost the sight of her right eye. Her parents sued Monfort's father. Held: complaint dismissed. The parents' responsibility under NCC 2180 ceases on proof of the diligence of a good father of a family; the act happened in school, outside the father's company, and was an innocent prank whose consequences no parent could anticipate.

Tamargo v. CA (G.R. No. 85044, June 3, 1992; class reviewer)

Adelberto Bundoc, 10, shot Jennifer Tamargo with an air rifle while living with his natural parents. A petition for his adoption by the Rapisura spouses had been filed before the shooting and was granted after it. The natural parents argued that, since a decree of adoption retroacts to the filing of the petition, parental authority and liability had passed to the adopters. Held: the natural parents are liable. The retroactive effect of an adoption decree is meant to benefit the child; it cannot impose liability on adopters "accruing at a time when adopting parents had no actual or physical custody over the adopted child." Parental liability rests on those who actually exercise custody and control.

Tolentino (Arts. 218–221): liability of parents rests on a presumption that they failed in their duty of vigilance and guidance; they may rebut it by proving the diligence of a good father of a family. The old rule (Bagajo v. Marave) that only teachers, not the head of an academic school, are answerable for their students' torts was modified by Arts. 219 and 221, which have a much broader application. A head of family who keeps a car for the family's general use is liable for its negligent operation by a child he permits to drive it (Gutierrez v. Gutierrez, 56 Phil. 177).

2003 Bar: During class hours, while the teacher was chatting with other teachers in the corridor, a 7-year-old pupil stabbed another boy in the eye with a ball pen during a fight, causing permanent blindness. Who may be liable: the teacher, the school authorities, or the guilty boy's parents?

The teacher and the school authorities, principally and solidarily, because the pupil was under their special parental authority (Art. 218) and they were negligent: the teacher was out of the classroom chatting during class time (Art. 219). The boy's parents are subsidiarily liable (Art. 219, 1st par.).

2010 Bar: On May 5, 1989, Rozanno, 16, drove his car on a school field trip at his teacher's request, carrying four classmates; he made a wrong maneuver, and one classmate died. (a) Who is liable? (b) Same facts, but the accident happened in mid-1994.

(a) Rozanno was a minor on an authorized activity outside the premises, so the school, its administrators and teachers are liable under Arts. 218–219, with his parents subsidiarily liable (and principally under Art. 221 if negligent). (b) By mid-1994 Rozanno was 21; R.A. 6809 (majority at 18) took effect in December 1989. Arts. 218, 219 and 221 no longer apply; Rozanno alone is liable, unless the school or his parents were themselves negligent, in which case they answer under the general rules on quasi-delict. (Compare Art. 236, 3rd par.: parents remain responsible under NCC 2180 for children below 21; see Emancipation.)

Filial privilege (Art. 215; Rule 130, Sec. 25)

Art. 215, FC. No descendant shall be compelled, in a criminal case, to testify against his parents and grandparents, except when such testimony is indispensable in a crime against the descendant or by one parent against the other. (315a)
Rule 130, Sec. 25, Rules of Court. Parental and filial privilege. — No person may be compelled to testify against his parents, other direct ascendants, children or other direct descendants, except when such testimony is indispensable in a crime against that person or by one parent against the other.
Lee v. CA (G.R. No. 177861, July 13, 2010; class reviewer)

In a Rule 108 case over the birth records of the children of Lee Tek Sheng (registered as children of his wife, Keh Shiok Cheng, though allegedly borne by his concubine, Tiu Chuan), Tiu Chuan was called to testify. The filial privilege was invoked on the ground that she would be testifying against Emma Lee, whose stepmother she was claimed to be. Held: the privilege "only applies to 'direct' ascendants and descendants, a family tie connected by a common ancestry. A stepdaughter has no common ancestry by her stepmother." She may be compelled to testify.

Authority over the child's person (Arts. 220–224)

Art. 220, FC. The parents and those exercising parental authority shall have with respect to their unemancipated children or wards the following rights and duties:
(1) To keep them in their company, to support, educate and instruct them by right precept and good example, and to provide for their upbringing in keeping with their means;
(2) To give them love and affection, advice and counsel, companionship and understanding;
(3) To provide them with moral and spiritual guidance, inculcate in them honesty, integrity, self-discipline, self-reliance, industry and thrift, stimulate their interest in civic affairs, and inspire in them compliance with the duties of citizenship;
(4) To enhance, protect, preserve and maintain their physical and mental health at all times;
(5) To furnish them with good and wholesome educational materials, supervise their activities, recreation and association with others, protect them from bad company, and prevent them from acquiring habits detrimental to their health, studies and morals;
(6) To represent them in all matters affecting their interests;
(7) To demand from them respect and obedience;
(8) To impose discipline on them as may be required under the circumstances; and
(9) To perform such other duties as are imposed by law upon parents and guardians. (316a)
Art. 222, FC. The courts may appoint a guardian of the child's property or a guardian ad litem when the best interests of the child so requires. (317)
Arts. 223–224, FC (summary). The parents (or, in their absence or incapacity, whoever exercises parental authority) may petition the court of the child's residence for disciplinary measures; the child is entitled to counsel and a summary hearing. If the court finds the petitioner at fault, it may instead suspend or deprive him of parental authority. The measures may include commitment of the child for not more than thirty days in an accredited child-care institution; the parent may not interfere but must support the child.
Nine items, not eight: some class reviewers drop item (4) ("to enhance, protect, preserve and maintain their physical and mental health at all times") and list eight rights and duties. The official text, as Tolentino prints it, has nine.
Medina v. Makabali (27 SCRA 602, 1969)

Zenaida Medina gave birth to Joseph in Dra. Venancia Makabali's clinic and left him there. Dra. Makabali raised him as her own, had him treated for polio and sent him to school; Zenaida never visited or paid anything for over five years, then sought custody by habeas corpus. Asked whom he preferred, the boy pointed to Dra. Makabali: "She is the one rearing me." Held: custody stays with Dra. Makabali. "While our law recognizes the right of a parent to the custody of her child, Courts must not lose sight of the basic principle that 'in all questions on the care, custody, education and property of children, the latter's welfare shall be paramount' (Civil Code, Art. 363), and that for compelling reasons, even a child under seven may be ordered separated from the mother." "The right of parents to the company and custody of their children is but ancillary to the proper discharge of parental duties to provide the children with adequate support, education, moral, intellectual and civic training and development."

Luna v. IAC (137 SCRA 7, 1985; the syllabus prints 167 SCRA 7)

The natural parents of a girl, Shirley, who had been raised since infancy by the Lunas, obtained a final judgment awarding them custody. During the execution proceedings, the girl, then about ten, manifested that she would kill herself or run away if taken from the Lunas. Held: execution of the final judgment is stayed and the child stays with the Lunas. "In child custody cases, execution of final judgment of appellate courts awarding custody to child's biological parents may be stayed where during hearings on execution the child manifests that she will kill herself and escape if given to custody of her biological parents." "The best interest of the minor can override procedural rules and even the rights of parents to the custody of their children."

Tolentino (Art. 220): rights of parents: custody; representation in all matters affecting the child; respect, obedience and discipline; administration of the child's property and use of its fruits for the child and the family; consent to marriage, settlements, donations propter nuptias, adoption and employment; disinheritance for cause. Duties: support and upbringing; education, guidance, love; defense; answering for the child's damages; leaving the legitime. Parental support of an unemancipated child depends only on the parent's means, not the child's needs, and is not lost by the child's misconduct. A third person who could give a better home will not get custody if the parents' home is decent. Parents may open the child's correspondence and forbid unwholesome associations, but not his dealings with his grandparents. They may not force a child to marry (Salvaña v. Gaela: refusing consent to the child's marriage is not ground for deprivation), nor impose forced labor. They may now represent the child in court. Correction must be moderate; parents cannot confine children in "private prisons".

Authority over the child's property (Arts. 225–227)

Art. 225, FC. The father and the mother shall jointly exercise legal guardianship over the property of the unemancipated common child without the necessity of a court appointment. In case of disagreement, the father's decision shall prevail, unless there is a judicial order to the contrary.
Where the market value of the property or the annual income of the child exceeds P50,000, the parent concerned shall be required to furnish a bond in such amount as the court may determine, but not less than ten per centum (10%) of the value of the property or annual income, to guarantee the performance of the obligations prescribed for general guardians. … The ordinary rules on guardianship shall be merely suppletory except when the child is under substitute parental authority, or the guardian is a stranger, or a parent has remarried, in which case the ordinary rules on guardianship shall apply. (320a)
Art. 226, FC. The property of the unemancipated child earned or acquired with his work or industry or by onerous or gratuitous title shall belong to the child in ownership and shall be devoted exclusively to the latter's support and education, unless the title or transfer provides otherwise.
The right of the parents over the fruits and income of the child's property shall be limited primarily to the child's support and secondarily to the collective daily needs of the family. (321a, 323a)
Art. 227, FC. If the parents entrust the management or administration of any of their properties to an unemancipated child, the net proceeds of such property shall belong to the owner. The child shall be given a reasonable monthly allowance in an amount not less than that which the owner would have paid if the administrator were a stranger, unless the owner, grants the entire proceeds to the child. In any case, the proceeds thus given in whole or in part shall not be charged to the child's legitime. (322a)
Lindain v. CA (G.R. No. 95305, August 20, 1992; 212 SCRA 725)

The widowed mother of the Lindain minors, as their legal administrator, sold their land without court approval to the spouses Apostol, who knew the sale had no judicial approval. Years later the children sued for annulment and reconveyance. Held: the sale is void as to the children. "Under Art. 320 (NCC), a parent acting merely as a legal administrator of the property of his minor children does not have the power to dispose of or alienate the property of the said child without judicial approval." The powers of a legal administrator are "merely powers of possession and management"; the power to sell, mortgage, encumber or dispose "must proceed from the court". The buyers were not in good faith, and the action had not prescribed.

Tolentino (Arts. 225–227): administration covers acts of preservation and the receipt of fruits; any act of disposition, alienation or reduction of the child's patrimony exceeds it, so parents cannot dispose of the child's property without express judicial authority, nor compromise the child's rights. The legal guardianship cannot be renounced or delegated (to the child or a stranger). A minor parent cannot administer his child's property; a guardian should be appointed. The parents' right over the fruits is a limited usufruct, an attribute of parental authority for the family's benefit; it is inalienable and, Tolentino believes, cannot be renounced. A donor or testator may exclude it. The child's salary is not "fruits" but property, so it cannot be attached for the parent's debts. The usufruct ends with emancipation, the child's death, judicial loss of parental authority, consent to the child's living independently, and disinheritance or unworthiness of the parent (NCC 923, 1035). Under Art. 227, the child's allowance is compensation for his services, not support; the parents still owe support.
Hebron v. Loyola (G.R. No. 168960, July 5, 2010; class reviewer)

In a partition among the heirs of Remigia Baylon, the administrator's successor claimed that the shares of certain grandchildren had been waived or paid by their mother. Held: the grandchildren keep their shares. "The powers given to the mother by the laws as the natural guardian covers only matters of administration and cannot include the power of disposition"; she could not relinquish her children's inheritance.

Termination, suspension and revival (Arts. 228–232)

Art. 228, FC. Parental authority terminates permanently:
(1) Upon the death of the parents;
(2) Upon the death of the child; or
(3) Upon emancipation of the child. (327a)
Art. 229, FC. Unless subsequently revived by a final judgment, parental authority also terminates:
(1) Upon adoption of the child;
(2) Upon appointment of a general guardian;
(3) Upon judicial declaration of abandonment of the child in a case filed for the purpose;
(4) Upon final judgment of a competent court divesting the party concerned of parental authority; or
(5) Upon judicial declaration of absence or incapacity of the person exercising parental authority. (327a)
Art. 230, FC. Parental authority is suspended upon conviction of the parent or the person exercising the same of a crime which carries with it the penalty of civil interdiction. The authority is automatically reinstated upon service of the penalty or upon pardon or amnesty of the offender. (330a)
Art. 231, FC. The court in an action filed for the purpose or in a related case may also suspend parental authority if the parent or the person exercising the same:
(1) Treats the child with excessive harshness or cruelty;
(2) Gives the child corrupting orders, counsel or example;
(3) Compels the child to beg; or
(4) Subjects the child or allows him to be subjected to acts of lasciviousness.
The grounds enumerated above are deemed to include cases which have resulted from culpable negligence of the parent or the person exercising parental authority.
If the degree of seriousness so warrants, or the welfare of the child so demands, the court shall deprive the guilty party of parental authority or adopt such other measures as may be proper under the circumstances.
The suspension or deprivation may be revoked and the parental authority revived in a case filed for the purpose or in the same proceeding if the court finds that the cause therefor has ceased and will not be repeated. (332a)
Art. 232, FC. If the person exercising parental authority has subjected the child or allowed him to be subjected to sexual abuse, such person shall be permanently deprived by the court of such authority. (n)
Chua v. Cabangbang (27 SCRA 791, March 28, 1969)

Pacita Chua, a cabaret hostess, left her infant daughter with the Cabangbang spouses and did nothing for five years to recover her, claiming the Cabangbangs were powerful and influential. Held: the child stays with the Cabangbangs. "A mother who really loves her child would go to any extent to be reunited with her. The natural and normal reaction of the petitioner … should have been to move heaven and earth … in order to recover her. Yet she lifted not a finger." "Abandonment is one of the grounds for depriving parents of parental authority over their children." In certain cases, custody may be awarded even to strangers, as against either parent or both.

Tolentino (Arts. 228–232): except for emancipation and the child's death, termination is "not absolute": on the parents' death, substitute parental authority follows; on adoption, the adopter takes over; on abandonment, absence, incapacity or a divesting judgment, a guardian. Remarriage of a widowed parent no longer ends parental authority (as it did for widows under NCC 328), though the court may appoint a guardian for cause. The causes in the Code are not exclusive: a child maltreated by a stepmother whom the remarried father does not stop may be given a guardian (Cortes v. Castillo: a mother convicted of adultery was denied custody, which stayed with the paternal grandmother). "Sexual abuse" (Art. 232, permanent deprivation) is more serious than "acts of lasciviousness" (Art. 231, suspension), but even serious lasciviousness may justify deprivation.
Quiz traps (parental authority): "Remarriage of the surviving parent terminates parental authority" → false (Art. 212). "Parental authority may be renounced by a notarized agreement" → false (Art. 210). "In a disagreement between the parents, the court decides" → false: the father's decision prevails unless there is a judicial order to the contrary (Arts. 211, 225). "Teachers may impose reasonable corporal punishment" → false (Art. 233). "Parents need no court approval to sell the child's land as legal guardians" → false (Lindain). "The school is subsidiarily liable" → false: the school is principally and solidarily liable; the parents are subsidiarily liable (Art. 219). "Parental authority over an illegitimate child is exercised jointly if the father recognized the child" → false (Art. 176; Briones).
