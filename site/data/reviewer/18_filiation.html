Paternity and Filiation II: Proof of Filiation; Legitimation

Title VI, Chapters 2–4, Family Code, Arts. 172–182 (syllabus XIII.E–G). Covers proof of legitimate filiation (Arts. 172–173), proof of illegitimate filiation (Art. 175), compulsory recognition in criminal cases (RPC 345), and legitimated children (Arts. 177–182, as amended by R.A. No. 9858). Cases: Gotardo v. Buling, Perla v. Baring, Heirs of Roldan, Reyes, Castro, Mendoza v. IAC, Ramos, Lim v. CA, Ranas, Mendoza v. CA, Vda. de Alberto, Baluyut, Paterno, Ara v. Pizarro, Gapusan-Chua, Rafanan, Rizo, Dempsey, Barranco.

Proof of legitimate filiation (Arts. 172–173)

Art. 172, FC. The filiation of legitimate children is established by any of the following:
(1) The record of birth appearing in the civil register or a final judgment; or
(2) An admission of legitimate filiation in a public document or a private handwritten instrument and signed by the parent concerned.
In the absence of the foregoing evidence, the legitimate filiation shall be proved by:
(1) The open and continuous possession of the status of a legitimate child; or
(2) Any other means allowed by the Rules of Court and special laws. (265a, 266a, 267a)
Tolentino (Art. 172): proof of filiation is needed (a) when the husband or his heirs impugn legitimacy (they must prove the Art. 166 grounds), (b) for a child born more than 300 days after the marriage ends (no status under Art. 169), and (c) in a defense: a child whose legitimacy is impugned may refute the evidence with Art. 172 proof without filing a separate action, and the plaintiff's proof must clearly outweigh the presumption. There are two classes of proof: primary (the record of birth, or an authentic written admission) and, only in the absence of primary evidence, secondary. Secondary evidence is inadmissible when primary evidence exists. Record of birth: the declaration of the attending physician or midwife, or of either parent, suffices to register a birth (Act No. 3753, Sec. 5); civil register entries are public documents and prima facie evidence (Sec. 13). But if the alleged father did not take part in making the birth certificate, putting his name there by the mother, doctor or registrar is void; his signature is necessary (Berciles v. GSIS, 128 SCRA 53; Reyes). Admission: in a notarized document, or a private handwritten document signed by the parent; the intent to recognize must be sufficiently apparent. Baptismal certificates prove only the administration of the sacrament on the date stated, not the truth of what they say about the parents. Possession of status: the concurrence of facts showing the relation, chiefly that the child always bore the father's surname, the father treated him as his child and attended to his education, maintenance and future, and family and society constantly recognized him as such; it must be of some duration and uninterrupted. Other means: judicial and extrajudicial confessions, testimony, and Rule 130 pedigree evidence (declarations of deceased relatives, family reputation or tradition, entries in family bibles, engravings on rings, family portraits, common reputation). Family pictures, relatives' affidavits, the claimant's biodata, marriage contracts not signed by the alleged father of the bride, school records and other writings not signed by the alleged father are not evidence of filiation.

Cases: general and record of birth

Gotardo v. Buling (G.R. No. 165166, August 15, 2012)

Divina Buling sued Charles Gotardo, a co-employee at a bank branch, for compulsory recognition and support of her son, Gliffze. She testified that they had been sweethearts with regular sexual relations, and that when she became pregnant Charles proposed marriage and preparations began, but he backed out. Charles denied paternity. The RTC dismissed; the CA reversed. Held: filiation established; the child is entitled to support. Divina's testimony on the relationship and the sexual contact, together with the marriage proposal, made out a prima facie case that Charles did not overcome. The Court restated the four significant procedural aspects of a traditional paternity action: a prima facie case, affirmative defenses, the presumption of legitimacy, and physical resemblance. "Once filiation is established, whether legitimate or illegitimate, the child is entitled to recognition and support as a matter of legal obligation" (B29 reviewer's summary). (Facts from the reviewer and general knowledge; the full text was not among the sources.)

Perla v. Baring (G.R. No. 172471, November 12, 2012)

Mirasol Baring and her son Randy sued Antonio Perla for support, claiming he was Randy's father. Their evidence included a birth certificate naming Antonio as father, which he did not sign, a baptismal certificate, and testimony. Held: complaint dismissed. "An order for support must be issued only if paternity or filiation is established by clear and convincing evidence. Just like in a birth certificate, the lack of participation of the supposed father in the preparation of a baptismal certificate renders this document incompetent to prove paternity." To prove open and continuous possession of status, "there must be evidence of the manifestation of the permanent intention of the supposed father to consider the child as his, by continuous and clear manifestations of parental affection and care, which cannot be attributed to pure charity." (Doctrine from the B29 reviewer; facts from general knowledge.)

Heirs of Roldan v. Heirs of Roldan (G.R. No. 202578, September 27, 2017)

Not found in any of your sources. Sir lists it under Proof of Filiation (general). From general recollection (verify before relying on it): in a partition case between two sets of Roldan heirs, the Court held that the filiation of a party claiming to be an heir may be passed upon in the partition case itself, without a separate action to establish filiation. Read the full text if this case comes up in recitation.

Reyes v. CA (135 SCRA 439, 1985)

Irene Delgado claimed to be the recognized natural child of the late Francisco Delgado, relying on certificates of birth he had not signed. Held: not recognized. "The birth certificate, to be sufficient recognition, must be signed by the father and mother jointly, or by the mother alone if the father refuses, otherwise she may be penalized; and if the alleged father did not sign in the birth certificate, the placing of his name by the mother, or doctor or registrar, is incompetent evidence of paternity of said child." Nor can such a birth certificate be taken as recognition in a public instrument.

Castro v. CA (173 SCRA 656, 1989; the syllabus prints 658)

Benita claimed to be the natural child of Eustaquio Castro, born in 1919. Eustaquio himself had reported and registered her birth, but he did not sign the birth certificate, and the record was not preserved. Her status was contested some 60 years later. Held: the omissions were not held against her. "Strictly speaking, a birth certificate to be sufficient for purposes of recognizing a child must be signed by the father and mother jointly and if the father refuses, by the mother alone otherwise she may be penalized. It was Eustaquio himself who had the birth of Benita reported and registered. There is no indication in the records that Eustaquio should have known in 1919 that apart from reporting the birth of a child, he should also have signed the certificate and seen to it that it was preserved for 60 years." (Contrast Reyes: here the father himself caused the registration.)

Mendoza v. IAC (152 SCRA 445, 1987; caption as in the syllabus)

Modesta Gabuya claimed to have been recognized as a natural child by her mother, Nicolasa Gabuya. Her only document was a certificate of birth and baptism signed by the assistant parish priest of Pardo, Cebu City, stating that she was Nicolasa's illegitimate daughter. Held: not enough. "Recognition or acknowledgment of a natural child under said Code must be made in a record of birth, a will, a statement before a court of record, or in some other public document. … Philippine jurisprudence is consistent and uniform in ruling that the canonical certificate of baptism is not sufficient to prove recognition." (Uribe's Doctrines prints these facts under this caption; the parties' names do not match it, so the caption may be off.)

Uribe (2018 situationer): "If the only evidence presented as proof of filiation is a birth certificate with the name of the putative father. Is that a sufficient basis that the child is the child of that person?" No. "You can actually put Gokongwei's name." The father must have signed or otherwise taken part in the registration (Reyes; Vda. de Alberto; Baluyut).

Cases: final judgment, admissions, possession of status

Ramos v. Ramos (61 SCRA 284, 1974)

The plaintiffs claimed to be natural children of Martin Ramos and sued for their shares in his estate; the legitimate heirs pleaded res judicata based on the 1913 intestate proceeding (Civil Case No. 217). Held (on status): "It is true that the acknowledgment of the plaintiffs is not evidenced by a record of birth, will or other public document (Art. 131, Old Civil Code). But the record of Civil Case No. 217 … indubitably shows that the plaintiffs were treated as acknowledged natural children of Martin Ramos. The reasonable inference is that they were in the continuous possession of the status of natural children of Martin Ramos, as evidenced by his direct acts and the acts of his family." (Per the case digest, their claim to recover their shares was, however, held barred by prescription and laches; verify if asked.)

Lim v. CA (65 SCRA 160, 1975)

Felisa Lim offered her marriage contract, which named the alleged father, as a public document of recognition. Held: insufficient. "The marriage contract presented by Felisa Lim does not satisfy the requirements of solemnity prescribed by article 131 of the Civil Code of 1889. Such contract is not a written act with the intervention of a notary; it is not an instrument executed in due form before a notary and certified by him. The marriage contract is a mere declaration by the contracting parties, in the presence of the person solemnizing the marriage and of two witnesses of legal age, that they take each other as husband and wife … The marriage contract does not possess the requisites of a public document of recognition. Be it remembered that recognition, under the Civil Code of 1889, 'must be precise, express and solemn.'"

Ranas v. Ranas (cited by Tolentino as 134 SCRA 260; the syllabus prints 64 SCRA 260)

Listed under "signed and handwritten private instrument". Your sources give no facts. Tolentino cites it for two propositions: in an admission of filiation in a document, "the intent to recognize the child must be sufficiently apparent"; and family pictures, relatives' affidavits, the claimant's biodata, unsigned marriage contracts, school records and other writings not signed by the alleged father do not constitute evidence of filiation. Related modern rule (Dela Cruz v. Gracia, G.R. No. 177728, July 31, 2009): a private handwritten instrument must be signed by the parent if it is the only evidence; if other evidence corroborates it, it is enough that the parent wrote it.

Mendoza v. CA (201 SCRA 675, September 24, 1991)

Teopista Toring Tuñacao sued Casimiro Mendoza for recognition as his illegitimate daughter. She lived with her mother, not with Casimiro, although all lived in Omapad, Mandaue City; Casimiro and his wife were childless, yet another alleged illegitimate child, Vicente Toring, lived with them. Teopista did not use Casimiro's surname, and his giving of money through relatives was "off-and-on". Held: open and continuous possession was not shown. "'Continuous' does not mean that the concession of status shall continue forever but only that it shall not be of an intermittent character while it continues. The possession of such status means that the father has treated the child as his own, directly and not through others, spontaneously and without concealment though without publicity (since the relation is illegitimate). There must be a showing of the permanent intention of the supposed father to consider the child as his own, by continuous and clear manifestation of paternal affection and care." Her filiation was nonetheless upheld under "other means allowed by the Rules of Court": the testimony of Mendoza relatives on pedigree and family reputation (Rule 130).

When the action may be brought (Arts. 173, 175)

Art. 173, FC. The action to claim legitimacy may be brought by the child during his or her lifetime and shall be transmitted to the heirs should the child die during minority or in a state of insanity. In these cases, the heirs shall have a period of five years within which to institute the action.
The action already commenced by the child shall survive notwithstanding the death of either or both of the parties. (268a)
Art. 175, FC. Illegitimate children may establish their illegitimate filiation in the same way and on the same evidence as legitimate children.
The action must be brought within the same period specified in Article 173, except when the action is based on the second paragraph of Article 172, in which case the action may be brought during the lifetime of the alleged parent. (289a)
Tolentino (Arts. 173, 175): a disputed legitimacy can be resolved only in a direct action by the proper parties within the period (Rosales v. Castillo, 132 SCRA 132). The child's action does not prescribe while he lives and cannot be renounced: "rights which emanate or are derived from blood are not subject either to prescription or waiver." It passes to his heirs only if he dies during minority or insanity, and then for five years. An illegitimate child uses the same evidence, period and parties; if the evidence is secondary, the action must be brought during the alleged parent's lifetime, so that "the supposed parent is given the chance to dispute personally the evidence of the paternity attributed to him". An illegitimate child "should be allowed that status if the evidence presented satisfies the judicial mind" (Dempsey).
Ara and Garcia v. Pizarro and Rossi (G.R. No. 187273, February 15, 2017)

The petitioners claimed to be children of the late Josefa A. Ara, mother of the respondents, and sought shares in her estate. For at least one petitioner, there was no record of birth, final judgment or signed admission by Josefa; the claim rested on secondary evidence, and the action was filed after her death. Held: that claim fails. "Even without a record of birth appearing in the civil register or a final judgement, filiation may still be established after the death of a putative parent through an admission of filiation in a public document or a private handwritten instrument, signed by the parent concerned. Absent such admission, a deceased person will have no opportunity to contest a claim of filiation." (Doctrine from the B29 reviewer.)

Cases: proof of illegitimate filiation

Vda. de Alberto v. CA (173 SCRA 436, 1989)

A claimant sought recognition as the illegitimate child of the deceased Alberto, against the widow, relying in part on a birth certificate naming the alleged father, which he had not signed. Held: "A birth certificate not signed by the alleged father therein indicated, like in the instant case, is not competent evidence of paternity."

Baluyut v. Baluyut (186 SCRA 506, 1990; the syllabus prints "Baluyot, 166 SCRA 606")

The petitioners claimed to be spurious children of the late Enrique Baluyut and sought to share in his estate. Their birth records were in his name but not signed by him, and they presented no authentic writing or statement in a court of record. Held: not recognized. "There is no evidence as required by Article 278 which proves that the petitioners were recognized by the deceased during his lifetime as his spurious children. The petitioners' records of birth, although in the name of Enrique Baluyut, were not signed by the latter. There was no authentic writing presented nor any statement in a court of record which would prove that the petitioners were recognized by the deceased."

Paterno v. Paterno (186 SCRA 630, 1990; not the 2020 property-relations case of the same name)

The private respondents claimed to be illegitimate children of the late Dr. Paterno; his widow, Jacoba T. Paterno, denied it. Their witnesses testified, with some inconsistencies, that Dr. Paterno had treated them as his children from birth. Held: filiation proved. "True, certain inconsistencies may be noted in the testimony given by the witnesses for the private respondents, but it is on the whole unanimous and consistent as to the really crucial fact that Dr. Paterno treated and acted towards said respondents, from their birth onward, in a manner only a real father would and leaving little doubt that he recognized and considered them as in truth his children. The simple denials of the widow, petitioner Jacoba T. Paterno, do not suffice to refute such proof."

Compulsory recognition in criminal cases (RPC 345)

Art. 345, RPC. Persons guilty of rape, seduction or abduction, shall also be sentenced: 1. To indemnify the offended woman. 2. To acknowledge the offspring, unless the law should prevent him from so doing. 3. In every case to support the offspring. …
People v. Rafanan (182 SCRA 811, 1990)

The accused was convicted of raping Filomena, and a child was born of the rape; the record did not show the child's identity. Held: the trial court correctly sentenced him "to acknowledge and support her off-spring", under RPC 345 and NCC 283(1) (the father must recognize the child "in cases of rape, abduction or seduction, when the period of the offense coincides more or less with that of the conception").

People v. Rizo (189 SCRA 265, 1990)

The trial court ordered a married man convicted of rape to recognize the child born of it, John Paul Valencia, as his legitimate son. Held: that portion is eliminated. "The rule is that if the rapist is a married man, he cannot be compelled to recognize the offspring of the crime, should there be any, as his child, whether legitimate or illegitimate."

Dempsey v. RTC (164 SCRA 384, 1988; the syllabus prints "Demsey, 164 SCRA 364")

The accused pleaded guilty to criminal charges concerning the child Christina Marie (under P.D. 603, for failure to give support), and the court ordered him, as part of the civil liability, to recognize her as his natural child. Held: error. "The recognition of a child by her father is provided for in the Civil Code and now in the new Family Code. In this criminal prosecution, where the accused pleaded guilty to criminal charges and the issue of recognition was not specifically and fully heard and tried, the trial court committed reversible error when it ordered recognition of a natural child as part of the civil liability in the criminal case."

People v. Barranco (177 SCRA 103, 1989; decided in Filipino)

A married man convicted of rape was ordered to acknowledge the child. Held: "Subalit mayroong pagkakamali ang mababang hukuman ng ipag-utos nito na kilalanin ng nahahabla bilang anak ang bunga ng kanyang kasalanan. Ang nahahabla ay may asawa. Hindi maaari na kilalanin ang batang bunga ng kasalanan na anak ng isang may-asawa. Subalit may katungkulan siyang sustentuhan ang bata ayon sa batas." (The lower court erred in ordering the married accused to acknowledge the child of his crime; but he must support the child.)

Gapusan-Chua v. CA (183 SCRA 160, 1990; the syllabus prints "Gapusan v. CA, 185 SCRA 160")

Ligaya Gapusan-Chua had been voluntarily recognized by her mother, Prudencia Gapusan, as her natural child; the recognition was questioned in the settlement of the mother's estate. Held: valid. "Voluntary recognition … 'is an admission of the fact of paternity or maternity by the presumed parent, expressed in the form prescribed by the Civil Code'", i.e., "in the record of birth, a will, a statement before a court of record, or in any authentic writing." If Ligaya was of age, she consented, impliedly by never impugning it during her mother's life and expressly by asserting it in the estate proceeding. If she was a minor, the lack of judicial approval "was cured by her ratification of that recognition, after having reached the age of majority, by her initiation of the proceedings for the settlement of her deceased mother's estate". (Listed under "compulsory recognition" in the syllabus, but it is a voluntary-recognition case.)

Legitimated children (Arts. 177–182; R.A. No. 9858)

Art. 177, FC (as amended by R.A. No. 9858). Children conceived and born outside of wedlock of parents who, at the time of conception of the former, were not disqualified by any impediment to marry each other, or were so disqualified only because either or both of them were below eighteen (18) years of age, may be legitimated. (269a)
Art. 178, FC. Legitimation shall take place by a subsequent valid marriage between parents. The annulment of a voidable marriage shall not affect the legitimation. (270a)
Art. 179, FC. Legitimated children shall enjoy the same rights as legitimate children. (272a)
Art. 180, FC. The effects of legitimation shall retroact to the time of the child's birth. (273a)
Art. 181, FC. The legitimation of children who died before the celebration of the marriage shall benefit their descendants. (274)
Art. 182, FC. Legitimation may be impugned only by those who are prejudiced in their rights, within five years from the time their cause of action accrues. (275a)
Uribe (Quiz 5, CivRev 2025, situationers): "What is important is at the time of the conception, not conception and birth: no legal impediment to marry. Age is not a legal impediment but capacity, so if the parents are 16 years old and thereafter, when 18, they marry, may the child be legitimated? Of course, being less than 18 is not a legal impediment, so long as the marriage is a valid marriage." Situationer: "C is the child of A and B. Was C legitimated? Is it not required by law that this was conceived AND born?" Answer: "Law only requires that the child was CONCEIVED during the time when there was no legal impediment to marriage. No issue as to impediments during the birth. 38, 37: legal impediments. This is not the same as capacity." In Quiz 5, a statement that a child born when the parents had no impediment "will be legitimated" was attacked: the law says may be legitimated, because a subsequent valid marriage is still required.

Tolentino (Arts. 177–182): legitimation is a remedy by which children not born in lawful wedlock are, by fiction, considered legitimate, as if born when their parents were validly married. It is "a product of nature and law": unlike adoption, which creates by fiction a relation that does not exist, legitimation makes legal what exists by nature, and needs no prior judicial approval (Colorado v. CA, 135 SCRA 47). Only children formerly classed as "natural" can be legitimated. A child of a married father and a single mother cannot be legitimated even if the father is later widowed and marries the mother (but see Tongay v. CA, 123 SCRA 99, a liberal ruling under the NCC). Tolentino criticizes the inconsistency: had the same parents contracted a void (bigamous) marriage, the child would be legitimate under Art. 54, so the law favors children of prohibited marriages over children of parents who respected the prohibition. Art. 178: the NCC required acknowledgment by both parents; the FC requires only the marriage, but filiation must still be established, if disputed, under Arts. 172, 173 and 175 (e.g., a seduced young mother gave the child away at birth and later married the father). If the subsequent marriage is void, there is no legitimation; if voidable and annulled, the legitimation stands. Art. 179: legitimation creates a relationship with all the parents' relatives (De los Santos v. Luciano, 60 Phil. 328: a legitimated daughter is the legitimate sister of her parents' legitimate son, so her child inherits from him as a legitimate niece). Art. 180: most codes date legitimation from the marriage; ours dates it from birth to protect the child, since the parents may marry long afterwards. Example: Z born in 1945 to X and Y, both capacitated; X's father dies intestate in 1951; X repudiates and X's brother B takes everything; X marries Y in 1953. Z, legitimated retroactively to 1945, was a legitimate grandchild in 1951 and takes the estate from B. Art. 181: a logical consequence: a child who died before the wedding transmits the rights of a legitimate child to his descendants, who inherit by representation. Art. 182: legitimation may be attacked when the child was not legitimable or is not the child of the persons who married. Who may sue: Sánchez Román and Burón say only those who suffer economic or material injury (testate or intestate heirs); Castán would include those with a moral interest. Tolentino sides with Sánchez Román: "rights" means rights recognized and enforceable by law.
Tolentino's text of Art. 177 is outdated. His 1990 commentary quotes the original Art. 177, which had no clause on parents below 18. R.A. No. 9858 (2009) added "or were so disqualified only because either or both of them were below eighteen (18) years of age". Quote the amended text. (Some reviewers also describe the original wording as "conceived and born outside of wedlock of parents who, at the time of the conception of the former, were not disqualified by any impediment to marry each other".)
Quiz traps (legitimation): "Children conceived and born outside of wedlock will be legitimated if their parents had no impediment" → false: may, upon a subsequent valid marriage. "The impediment is tested at the time of the birth" or "at the wedding" → false: at conception. "Legitimation takes effect from the date of the marriage" → false: it retroacts to birth (Art. 180). "Legitimation requires acknowledgment by both parents" → false under the FC. "Anyone may impugn legitimation at any time" → false: only those prejudiced in their rights, within 5 years from accrual (Art. 182). "Children of parents who were 17 at conception cannot be legitimated" → false (R.A. 9858). Syllabus misprint: it lists "Action to impugn, FC 162"; the article is 182.

1990 Bar (cleaned up): B and G, college students, both single, had an affair, and G became pregnant. B then married C in Cebu; two weeks after the wedding, G gave birth to E. B and C had two children, X and Y. During the last five years of that marriage, B renewed his relationship with G, and F was born to them two years before C died. B later married G. What is the status of each child?

X and Y: legitimate children of B and C (Art. 164). E: legitimated by the marriage of B and G, because when E was conceived both were single and not disqualified; that B was already married when E was born does not matter (Art. 177; Uribe). E has the same rights as X and Y (Art. 179). F: illegitimate and cannot be legitimated, because B was married to C when F was conceived. F uses G's surname (or B's if he expressly recognized F), and F's legitime is one-half of that of each legitimate (or legitimated) child (Art. 176).

Practice (after Tolentino's example): X and Y, both 17 and single, had a child, Z, in 2018. In 2022, X's father F died intestate, leaving X as his only child and a brother, B. X repudiated the inheritance, and B took the whole estate. In 2024, X and Y, now of age, married each other. May Z claim F's estate from B?

Yes. Z was legitimable even though X and Y were below 18 at conception (Art. 177, as amended by R.A. 9858: minority is not an impediment), and the subsequent valid marriage legitimated him (Art. 178). Legitimation retroacts to Z's birth in 2018 (Art. 180), so when F died in 2022 Z already had the rights of a legitimate grandchild. With X repudiating, Z, a legitimate descendant, excludes F's brother B, a collateral relative, so the estate must go to Z. Had the effect dated only from the 2024 wedding (the Spanish rule), Z would have had no claim.
