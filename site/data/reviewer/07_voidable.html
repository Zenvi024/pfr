Voidable Marriages and Annulment (FC Arts. 45–54)

Covers Art. 4 (pars. 1–2) cf. Art. 45; the six grounds for annulment (Art. 45), the exclusive list of fraud (Art. 46), who may sue and when (Art. 47), safeguards against collusion (Art. 48), support and custody pendente lite (Art. 49), effects of annulment (Arts. 50–54), the related Civil Code articles on vices of consent (NCC 1335–1344), NCC 2035, RA 6809, A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC and A.M. No. 02-11-12-SC, every syllabus case under "Voidable Marriages," Tolentino's commentary (1 Tolentino 270–310), and Uribe's own recitation positions.

Concept: what a voidable marriage is

Art. 4, FC. The absence of any of the essential or formal requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio, except as stated in Article 35 (2).
A defect in any of the essential requisites shall render the marriage voidable as provided in Article 45.
An irregularity in the formal requisites shall not affect the validity of the marriage but the party or parties responsible for the irregularity shall be civilly, criminally and administratively liable. (n)

Keep the three Art. 4 terms apart:

  • Absence of an essential or formal requisite makes the marriage void. The only exception is Art. 35(2): either or both parties believed in good faith that the solemnizing officer had authority.
  • A defect in an essential requisite (legal capacity; consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer) makes it voidable, "as provided in Article 45." Only the six Art. 45 causes make a marriage voidable.
  • An irregularity in a formal requisite leaves the marriage valid. The responsible parties are liable civilly, criminally and administratively.
Uribe: "There is no voidable in formal requisites." A defect in the formalities is either an absence (void) or an irregularity (valid). If you answer "voidable," the defect must lie in an essential requisite. He also asked in recitation: "May a marriage be voidable if there is no vitiation of consent?" The answer is YES. Lack of parental consent for a party aged 18 to below 21, unsound mind, impotence and a serious, incurable STD are Art. 45 grounds even though the party's own consent was freely given. Only fraud and force, intimidation or undue influence are true vices of consent.

Status of a voidable marriage. It is valid and produces all its civil effects until set aside by final judgment of a competent court in an action for annulment (Tolentino). Juridically, annulment erases the marriage as if it had never been contracted. The law, however, expressly saves some of its effects: legitimacy of the children (Art. 54) and liquidation of the community or conjugal partnership (Art. 50).

Tolentino: Annulment is an action in rem because it concerns the status of the parties. The res is the marriage tie, and jurisdiction over it depends on the nationality or domicile of the parties, not on the place of celebration (citing Rayray v. Chae Kyung Lee). Annulment differs from legal separation in three ways. (1) Annulment rests on a cause existing at the time of the marriage, while legal separation rests on a cause arising after the celebration. (2) Annulment severs the marital bond and legal separation does not. (3) Once final, an annulment cannot be set aside to restore the marriage, while legal separation ends upon reconciliation.
Uribe: When a marriage has a ground for annulment and also a ground for nullity (e.g., no license and no parental consent), the status is void ab initio. "Walang saysay ang voidable kung void ang marriage." There is nothing to annul. The same holds for ordinary contracts: if one contract is at once rescissible, unenforceable and void, its status is void.
Uribe (do not presume voidability): He criticized a student who "already presumed that the marriage is VOIDABLE." Ratification cannot happen to a valid marriage, and prescription cannot run against a party who has no right of action. Establish the ground first, especially that it existed at the time of the celebration. Only then discuss who may sue, prescription and ratification. He also corrected a student on terms: "it is not the ground which prescribed; what prescribes is the right of action."

Art. 45: the six grounds (verbatim)

Art. 45, FC. A marriage may be annulled for any of the following causes, existing at the time of the marriage:
(1) That the party in whose behalf it is sought to have the marriage annulled was eighteen years of age or over but below twenty-one, and the marriage was solemnized without the consent of the parents, guardian or person having substitute parental authority over the party, in that order, unless after attaining the age of twenty-one, such party freely cohabited with the other and both lived together as husband and wife;
(2) That either party was of unsound mind, unless such party after coming to reason, freely cohabited with the other as husband and wife;
(3) That the consent of either party was obtained by fraud, unless such party afterwards, with full knowledge of the facts constituting the fraud, freely cohabited with the other as husband and wife;
(4) That the consent of either party was obtained by force, intimidation or undue influence, unless the same having disappeared or ceased, such party thereafter freely cohabited with the other as husband and wife;
(5) That either party was physically incapable of consummating the marriage with the other, and such incapacity continues and appears to be incurable; or
(6) That either party was afflicted with a sexually-transmissible disease found to be serious and appears to be incurable. (85a)

Source: NCC Art. 85, with par. (6) new (Tolentino). The introductory clause, "existing at the time of the marriage," governs all six grounds. A cause that arises after the wedding is never a ground for annulment, though it may be a ground for legal separation (Art. 55).

Quiz trap: Uribe swaps words in the codal. Watch for these:
  • "18 or over but below 21" vs. "below 18". Below 18 is void under Art. 35(1), even with parental consent.
  • Parental consent vs. parental advice. Lack of advice (ages 21 to 25, Art. 15) only suspends issuance of the license for three months. It never makes the marriage voidable.
  • "either party" (grounds 2–6) vs. "the party in whose behalf" (ground 1).
  • "serious and appears to be incurable" (45(6)) vs. "regardless of its nature" (46(3)).
  • "physically incapable of consummating" (impotence) vs. sterility. Sterility is not a ground.
  • Mistake as to identity is void (Art. 35(5)), not voidable. Under the old Civil Code (Art. 86(1)) misrepresentation as to identity was fraud and made the marriage voidable. The Family Code moved it to void marriages.

Ground (1): no parental consent, age 18 to below 21

Elements

  1. The party in whose behalf annulment is sought was 18 or over but below 21 at the time of the marriage.
  2. The marriage was solemnized without the consent of the parents, guardian or person having substitute parental authority, in that order.
  3. No ratification: the party did not, after attaining 21, freely cohabit with the other with both living together as husband and wife.
  4. The action is brought by the proper party within the Art. 47(1) period.
Art. 14, FC. In case either or both of the contracting parties, not having been emancipated by a previous marriage, are between the ages of eighteen and twenty-one, they shall, in addition to the requirements of the preceding articles, exhibit to the local civil registrar, the consent to their marriage of their father, mother, surviving parent or guardian, or persons having legal charge of them, in the order mentioned. Such consent shall be manifested in writing by the interested party, who personally appears before the proper local civil registrar, or in the form of an affidavit made in the presence of two witnesses and attested before any official authorized by law to administer oaths. The personal manifestation shall be recorded in both applications for marriage license, and the affidavit, if one is executed instead, shall be attached to said applications. (61a)
RA 6809, Sec. 3 (amending Art. 236, FC), relevant sentence. Contracting marriage shall require parental consent until the age of twenty-one.
  • RA 6809 (1989) lowered the age of majority from 21 to 18, but it expressly kept parental consent for marriage until 21. Being of age does not remove the Art. 14 requirement.
  • "Not having been emancipated by a previous marriage." A person aged 18 to 20 who was previously married (e.g., widowed) does not need parental consent to remarry. Consent was given for the first marriage and need not be repeated.
  • Consent must refer to a particular person. A blanket "you are free to marry anyone" is not consent; it is a relinquishment of the parent's duty (Tolentino).
  • A widowed mother who remarries keeps parental authority, so her consent is still required (Tolentino).
  • An orphan over 18 with no one "having legal charge" of him cannot comply. The law does not require the impossible.
Tolentino (who may ratify): The Code does not expressly let the parent ratify. The Code Commission thought parental ratification should not be allowed, because it would encourage young people to marry first and seek confirmation later. Tolentino believes ratification by the parent whose consent was lacking should be recognized, provided it is made before the child reaches 21. His reasons: Art. 47(1) gives the parent a right of action that he may waive, and consent is what the law requires, whether given in advance or afterwards by ratification. For the quiz, the safe answer is the codal one: Art. 45(1) ratification is by the party's free cohabitation after attaining 21.
Uribe (counting cohabitation): "Di mo pwede icount ang free cohabitation until umabot siya ng 21." Cohabitation before 21, however long, does not ratify. The ratifying cohabitation starts only when the party reaches 21. This is why, in his 2025 key, free cohabitation was "not a defense" against an action by the parent for lack of parental consent (see the Uribe recitation views section below).
Uribe (status vs. validity): "A and B were married. B was only 20, without parental consent. Is it possible that the marriage will be valid even without parental consent?" He answered YES. (1) A voidable marriage is valid until annulled, and the question is about present status. (2) B may have been previously married with parental consent, so no new consent was needed. Elsewhere, when the facts said the couple "eloped," he insisted the answer is "voidable," not "voidable if no consent": "Do not qualify because it is already a fact. Wag mag 'if'."
Note: The syllabus cites "FC 237 for minors" under effects of annulment. Art. 237 dealt with the annulment of a minor's marriage, but RA 6809, Sec. 2 expressly repealed Arts. 235 and 237. With majority at 18 and marriage below 18 void, emancipation of a minor by marriage no longer exists. Rely on RA 6809.

Solenn (19) and Sonny (20), step-siblings raised in the same household, elope and marry with a license in church, against their parents' wishes. Status?

Answer: Voidable under Art. 45(1) for lack of parental consent. Being step-siblings is no impediment: Art. 37 covers blood relatives only, and Art. 38 lists step-parent/step-child, not step-brother/step-sister. The license and church rites satisfy the formal requisites but do not cure the lack of consent.

Conrad and Linda, both 20, falsely state they are over 25, marry before an unsuspecting judge, and cohabit for 6 years. Linda's parents then sue to annul for lack of parental consent. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. The parents may sue only "at any time before such party has reached the age of twenty-one" (Art. 47(1)). Linda is now about 26. Separately, the couple's free cohabitation after reaching 21 ratified the marriage (Art. 45(1)).

Ground (2): unsound mind

Elements

  1. Either party was of unsound mind, i.e., by reason of a defect or disease of the mind, incapable of intelligently consenting.
  2. The insanity existed at the time of the marriage. Insanity before or after the marriage is not enough.
  3. No ratification: the insane party did not freely cohabit with the other after coming to reason.
Tolentino: The theory is not that the sane spouse made a bad bargain by getting an insane partner. It is that the insane party gave a consent that is unreal for lack of intelligence. The test is whether, at the time of the marriage, the party could understand the nature and consequences of the marriage. It is not whether the party has enough mentality to meet the responsibility of raising children.
  • Weakness of mind: mere mental weakness that does not take away that understanding does not affect validity. The same goes for insane delusions or impulses on other subjects (e.g., kleptomania), insane parents, or occasional peculiar behavior, if the party was otherwise normal at the wedding.
  • Burden of proof: the law presumes sanity, so whoever alleges insanity must prove it. Once general insanity is proved, it is presumed to continue, and whoever alleges recovery or a lucid interval must prove it.
  • Intoxication: a marriage may be annulled if a party was so drunk that he had no mental capacity to assent, e.g., he had only a dim recollection of the ceremony and never intended to marry. Intoxication short of depriving him of reason is no ground.
  • Somnambulism has the same effect as insanity.
  • Art. 36 may also apply if the insane spouse's mental condition prevents compliance with the essential marital obligations.
Katipunan v. Tenorio (38 O.G. 173)

The husband sued to annul on the ground that the wife was insane at the time of the marriage, unknown to him. There was no proof that she was insane at the wedding; it was admitted that she became insane afterwards (Tolentino). She, through her guardian, opposed, invoking their four children (B29). Doctrine: Every presumption favors the validity of a marriage. Insanity must be proved clearly, unequivocally and convincingly to have existed at the time of the celebration. Insanity occurring after the marriage is not a cause for annulment. (B29 adds: if the insane spouse recovers and the spouses voluntarily live together as husband and wife, the marriage is validated.)

Tolentino (ratification and estoppel for insanity):
  • Art. 45(2) gives the insane spouse the right of action and lets him ratify after regaining reason.
  • The sane spouse who knew of the insanity has no right of action. He is in estoppel and must come to court with clean hands. A spouse who lived for years with the allegedly insane wife and had children with her is estopped.
  • The sane spouse who did not know may sue under Art. 47(2) "at any time before the death of either party." The law does not say he ratifies by continuing to cohabit after learning of the insanity. However long the cohabitation, he may sue as long as both live, because "it is totally unfair to keep him chained for life to an insane partner." This is Tolentino's third marriage not subject to ratification, as far as the sane spouse is concerned.
Quiz trap: The insane spouse sues "during a lucid interval or after regaining sanity." No five-year period applies. Relatives and guardians sue "at any time before the death of either party." A statement that the insane spouse must sue "within five years after regaining sanity" is false.

A married B in 2020. B was perfectly sane then but became insane in 2022. May A annul?

Answer: No. Art. 45 requires the cause to exist "at the time of the marriage." Supervening insanity is not a ground (Katipunan v. Tenorio). Art. 36 also requires the incapacity to exist at the celebration. A's remedies lie elsewhere, e.g., the rules on administration of the property of an incapacitated spouse.

Ground (3) and Art. 46: fraud (exclusive list)

Art. 46, FC. Any of the following circumstances shall constitute fraud referred to in Number 3 of the preceding Article:
(1) Non-disclosure of a previous conviction by final judgment of the other party of a crime involving moral turpitude;
(2) Concealment by the wife of the fact that at the time of the marriage, she was pregnant by a man other than her husband;
(3) Concealment of sexually transmissible disease, regardless of its nature, existing at the time of the marriage; or
(4) Concealment of drug addiction, habitual alcoholism or homosexuality or lesbianism existing at the time of the marriage.
No other misrepresentation or deceit as to character, health, rank, fortune or chastity shall constitute such fraud as will give grounds for action for the annulment of marriage. (86a)

Source: NCC Art. 86. Paragraphs (3) and (4) are new (Tolentino).

General rule on fraud in marriage

  • The enumeration is exclusive: inclusio unius est exclusio alterius. No other fraud, however serious and however essential to the marital relation, annuls a marriage (Tolentino). The Civil Code and the Family Code dropped the flexible American rule that any serious fraud about a fact essential to the marital relation is enough.
  • The last paragraph adds an express exclusion: no other misrepresentation or deceit as to character, health, rank, fortune or chastity. Memory aid: C-H-R-F-C.
  • Marriage is harder to avoid for fraud than ordinary contracts. Because of its solemn importance, the parties must exercise care and diligence to learn the facts beforehand (Tolentino).
  • The Civil Code rules on fraud (NCC 1338–1344) supply the general concept: insidious words or machinations, or silence when there is a duty to speak. For marriage, however, what counts as fraud is fixed by Art. 46.
NCC Arts. 1338–1344. Art. 1338. There is fraud when, through insidious words or machinations of one of the contracting parties, the other is induced to enter into a contract which, without them, he would not have agreed to. (1269)

Art. 1340. The usual exaggerations in trade, when the other party had an opportunity to know the facts, are not in themselves fraudulent. (n)
Art. 1341. A mere expression of an opinion does not signify fraud, unless made by an expert and the other party has relied on the former's special knowledge. (n)
Art. 1342. Misrepresentation by a third person does not vitiate consent, unless such misrepresentation has created substantial mistake and the same is mutual. (n)
Art. 1343. Misrepresentation made in good faith is not fraudulent but may constitute error. (n)
Art. 1344. In order that fraud may make a contract voidable, it should be serious and should not have been employed by both contracting parties.

Art. 46(1): non-disclosure of previous conviction

Requisites (Tolentino):

  1. The crime involves moral turpitude.
  2. There was a conviction by final judgment before the marriage. A pending case or an acquittal does not count.
  3. It was not disclosed to the other party.

The Civil Code also required a penalty of at least two years' imprisonment. The Family Code dropped this, so the length of the penalty no longer matters.

Uribe: "If the respondent failed to disclose a conviction where a penalty of 30 days was imposed, would the action prosper?" Yes. A light penalty does not mean the crime lacks moral turpitude. Theft involves moral turpitude even when the imposable penalty is very short, and he also cited BP 22.
Tolentino (criticism): The wisdom of this paragraph is "very doubtful." A conviction is really a deceit as to character, with no essential bearing on the marital relation. Concealing a past life of prostitution would be a graver fraud than a conviction for theft or estafa, yet the former is no ground (it goes to character or chastity) while the latter is.
Quiz trap: "Non-disclosure of a previous conviction by final judgment of the other party shall constitute fraud" is FALSE, because "of a crime involving moral turpitude" is missing. "...only if the penalty imposed was at least two years" is FALSE; that was the old Civil Code rule.

Manuel, previously convicted of theft in Palawan and having served his sentence, marries Marianne without telling her. On learning of it, she leaves him. May she annul?

Answer: Yes. Theft involves moral turpitude, and the conviction was final. Non-disclosure is fraud under Art. 46(1) and a ground under Art. 45(3). Serving the sentence does not erase the conviction or the concealment. She must sue within five years after discovery (Art. 47(3)). She left on learning of it, so there was no ratification.

Art. 46(2): concealment of pregnancy by another man

Requisites:

  1. The wife was pregnant at the time of the marriage.
  2. She was pregnant by a man other than her husband.
  3. She concealed the pregnancy from the husband.
Tolentino: Concealment of pregnancy by another man "goes to the very essence of marriage." One of its most important objects is procreating children whom the parents know with certainty to be their own. A husband may require that his wife not bring aliens into his bloodline.
  • No fraud where the husband knew of the pregnancy. If the child was born less than three months after the wedding, the advanced pregnancy must have been patent to him (Buccat v. Mangonon).
  • Premarital intercourse with the husband: where the man had intercourse with the woman before the marriage and she is pregnant at the wedding, the marriage will not be annulled even if he is not the father. He knew she was unchaste because he took part in it, and he comes to court with unclean hands (Carris v. Carris).
  • Deceit as to paternity: where the woman makes him believe he is the father, the majority line (Massachusetts) denies annulment on unclean hands. Tolentino calls the Connecticut line, which allows annulment, "more reasonable."
Buccat v. Mangonon de Buccat (72 Phil. 19, 1941)

Godofredo Buccat, a first-year law student, married Luida Mangonon on November 26, 1938, relying on her assurance that she was a virgin. She gave birth to a full-term child 89 days after the wedding, and he sued to annul for fraud. Doctrine: No fraud. Luida was in an advanced stage of pregnancy at the wedding, so her condition must have been plainly visible, and it is unlikely that Godofredo suspected nothing. Marriage is a sacred institution and the foundation of society, and annulling it requires clear and convincing proof of fraud. None was shown.

Aquino v. Delizo (109 Phil. 19, 1960)

The husband sued to annul because the wife concealed that, at the time of the marriage, she was about four months pregnant by another man. The lower courts dismissed the case on the authority of Buccat. Doctrine: Under the Civil Code (Art. 85(4) in relation to Art. 86(3), now FC 45(3) and 46(2)), concealment by the wife that she was pregnant by another man at the time of the marriage is fraud and a ground for annulment. Buccat does not apply. A four-month pregnancy is not necessarily apparent, especially in a "naturally plump" woman. Medical authorities say the abdomen rises above the umbilicus, making the roundness general and apparent, only around the sixth month, and even at five months the enlargement may pass for fat. That the couple may have had premarital relations, making the child his, was pure conjecture. The SC set aside the dismissal and let the husband prove his case.

Republic v. Villacorta (2021)

Melvin and Janufi had a daughter in 2001 and married in 2004. Melvin had doubted paternity, but Janufi assured him he was the father. A 2010 DNA test showed he was not, and the RTC annulled the marriage under Arts. 45(3) and 46(2). Doctrine: Reversed. Art. 46(2) covers only concealment by the wife that she was pregnant at the time of the marriage by another man. Janufi was not pregnant at the wedding, since the child was born three years earlier. Fraud as a ground is exclusive and restrictive. Misrepresentation as to past relationships, chastity or a child's paternity outside Art. 46 is not fraud. There was also no proof of bad faith, since her messages showed she believed Melvin was the father.

Uribe ("this is your child"): "The wife, before the marriage, was already pregnant. She told the future spouse, 'This is your child.' After the marriage the child turned out not to be the husband's. Will the action for annulment prosper?" His answer was NO. "Isn't the law clear that the concealment is on the pregnancy and not on who the father was?" The pregnancy was not concealed. Answer it this way in his quiz, citing Tolentino's unclean-hands rule as support. Be aware that one could argue the contrary from the Connecticut view.
Quiz trap: "Concealment by the wife of the fact that at the time of the marriage she was pregnant constitutes fraud" is FALSE, because "by a man other than her husband" is missing. "Concealment by the wife or the husband..." is also wrong, because 46(2) applies only to the wife.

Art. 46(3): concealment of STD, "regardless of its nature"

Requisites:

  1. One party had a sexually-transmissible disease existing at the time of the marriage.
  2. That party concealed it.
  3. The disease need not be serious or incurable ("regardless of its nature").

Here the STD is not itself the ground. It is an element of the fraud that vitiated the other party's consent (Tolentino). The comparison with Art. 45(6) is in the next section.

Quiz trap: "Concealment of STD, regardless of its nature, shall constitute fraud resulting in a voidable marriage" is FALSE because "existing at the time of the marriage" is missing. This exact item appears on several Uribe quizzes. "A marriage may be annulled on the ground of STD regardless of its nature existing at the time of the marriage" is also FALSE, because annulment for the disease itself (45(6)) requires that it be serious and appear incurable. "Concealment of STD existing at the time of the marriage, if serious and appears incurable, constitutes fraud" is TRUE, because "regardless of its nature" includes serious diseases.

Art. 46(4): concealment of drug addiction, habitual alcoholism, homosexuality or lesbianism

Requisites:

  1. The condition (drug addiction, habitual alcoholism, homosexuality or lesbianism) existed at the time of the marriage.
  2. It was concealed from the other party.

The ground is the concealment, not the condition.

Almelor v. RTC of Las Piñas (2008)

Doctrine: Homosexuality per se is not a ground to annul a marriage. It is only a ground for legal separation (Art. 55(6)). What the law treats as fraud is the concealment of homosexuality existing at the time of the marriage, and that concealment must be proved.

Tolentino (effect of cure): If, after discovering the concealed STD, addiction or alcoholism, the innocent spouse separates and the condition is later completely cured, the cure does not bar annulment. The defect is the fraud that vitiated consent, not the condition, and curing the condition does not cure the defect in consent.
Uribe (alcoholism that arose after the wedding): "Habitual alcoholism is not a ground. It is the concealment. The ground is fraud." Alcoholism that began a few months after the wedding could not have been concealed at the wedding: "How can you conceal when it hasn't happened yet?" Such a petition fails, and the remedy is legal separation (Art. 55(5)).
Uribe (bisexual defense): In recitation he asked what a judge should do if, in an action for concealment of homosexuality, the defendant answers that he or she is bisexual, not homosexual. The notes record no final answer. A reasoned answer: Art. 46(4) is strictly construed, but what it protects is the other party's consent against a concealed same-sex orientation existing at the time of the marriage. A concealed bisexual orientation that includes same-sex attraction falls within "homosexuality or lesbianism," and the label the defendant prefers does not change the facts. The petitioner must still prove existence at the wedding and concealment (Almelor).

The last paragraph: what is NOT fraud

Anaya v. Palaroan (36 SCRA 97, 1970)

Aurora sued to annul her 1953 marriage to Fernando because he had not told her, before the wedding, of his premarital relationship with another woman. In her reply she added that he had never intended to live with her and had courted her only to avoid a breach-of-promise suit. Doctrine: Non-disclosure of a husband's premarital relationship is not among the exclusively enumerated kinds of fraud (then NCC 86). It is further excluded by the last paragraph: "no other misrepresentation or deceit as to ... chastity." A woman may detest the non-disclosure or feel cheated into consenting, but the law does not ease her grief once consent has been solemnly given. By marrying she entered an institution in which society, not she alone, is interested. "The lawmaker's intent being plain, the Court's duty is to give effect to the same, whether it agrees with the rule or not." The new theory of feigned courtship was not pleaded in the complaint. It would also have prescribed, because she should have discovered it soon after the 1953 wedding but raised it only in 1966, beyond the four-year period then applicable.

Tolentino (not grounds for annulment):
  • Chastity of wife: Mere incontinence before marriage, not resulting in pregnancy, does not keep a woman from being a faithful wife. A marriage cannot be annulled because the wife concealed that she had been lewd and had an illegitimate child before the marriage. The parties must make their own inquiries.
  • No intent to consummate: American courts annul where a party secretly resolves never to have sexual intercourse after the marriage. Under our law this is not fraud, because it is not in the enumeration.
  • Accidental matters (health except extreme cases, rank, fame, fortune, habits, temperament) were never sufficient even under the old flexible rule.
Uribe: "The fact that the wife was a prostitute before the marriage, and this was concealed from the husband. Will the action prosper?" No. Under Art. 46 the list is exclusive, and no other deceit as to character or chastity is a ground. Nor is it a ground for legal separation (Art. 55) or for separation of property. The husband has no remedy.

Rene posed as macho, but one month after the wedding Lily discovers he is gay. He will not go to bed with her, keeps magazines of nude men and seeks the company of young men. Remedy?

Answer: Annulment for fraud: concealment of homosexuality existing at the time of the marriage (Arts. 45(3), 46(4)). She must sue within five years after discovery and must not freely cohabit with him after full knowledge. Homosexuality is not per se psychological incapacity, and divorce is unavailable between Filipinos. Legal separation under Art. 55(6) is an alternative.

A concealed his drug addiction, existing at the time of their 2021 marriage, from B. B discovered it in 2023 and left at once. In 2024, before B sued, A was fully rehabilitated. May B still annul?

Answer: Yes, within five years from discovery, i.e., until 2028. The defect is the fraud on B's consent, not the addiction, so the cure does not bar the action (Tolentino). B left upon discovery, so there was no ratification.

Ground (4): force, intimidation, undue influence

Elements

  1. The consent of either party was obtained by force (violence), intimidation or undue influence, as defined in NCC 1335 and 1337.
  2. The vice existed at the time of the marriage and actually caused the consent.
  3. No ratification: after the force etc. disappeared or ceased, the party did not freely cohabit with the other as husband and wife.
NCC Arts. 1335–1337. Art. 1335. There is violence when in order to wrest consent, serious or irresistible force is employed.
There is intimidation when one of the contracting parties is compelled by a reasonable and well-grounded fear of an imminent and grave evil upon his person or property, or upon the person or property of his spouse, descendants or ascendants, to give his consent.
To determine the degree of intimidation, the age, sex and condition of the person shall be borne in mind.
A threat to enforce one's claim through competent authority, if the claim is just or legal, does not vitiate consent. (1267a)
Art. 1336. Violence or intimidation shall annul the obligation, although it may have been employed by a third person who did not take part in the contract. (1268)
Art. 1337. There is undue influence when a person takes improper advantage of his power over the will of another, depriving the latter of a reasonable freedom of choice. The following circumstances shall be considered: the confidential, family, spiritual and other relations between the parties, or the fact that the person alleged to have been unduly influenced was suffering from mental weakness, or was ignorant or in financial distress.
  • Violence: serious or irresistible force used to wrest consent.
  • Intimidation has five elements: (a) reasonable and well-grounded fear; (b) of an imminent and grave evil; (c) upon the person or property of the party, or of his spouse, descendants or ascendants; (d) that compels consent; (e) judged by the age, sex and condition of the person.
  • Undue influence: improper advantage of power over another's will, depriving him of reasonable freedom of choice. Consider confidential, family or spiritual relations, mental weakness, ignorance and financial distress.
  • A third person may be the source (NCC 1336), e.g., the bride's father or relatives.
  • A just or legal threat does not vitiate consent (NCC 1335, last par.).
Tolentino: The duress must be such as to keep the party from acting as a free agent, his will being destroyed by fear or compulsion.
  • A marriage entered into through fear of death or bodily harm is voidable.
  • A man compelled to marry a girl under threat of imprisonment by a void process or false charge may annul.
  • A marriage entered into to escape punishment for seduction will not be annulled, even if consent was secured by threats to prosecute a crime of which the party was guilty.
  • No duress where a man marries under threat of charges for immorality he actually committed, made to obstruct his admission to the bar (Ruiz v. Atienza).
  • No duress where a man who seduced a woman yields reluctantly to pressure to marry her and repair the wrong.
  • No duress where a woman marries because the man threatened to reveal to her parents an indiscretion of her youth.
  • No duress where the party had sufficient time and opportunity for deliberation between the alleged duress and the ceremony.
Ruiz v. Atienza (CA, 40 O.G. 1903, 1941)

A man was threatened with a complaint for immorality that would block his admission to the bar unless he married her, and he married her. Doctrine: Where a man marries under threat of a lawful prosecution or charge (seduction, bastardy, immorality he committed), he cannot avoid the marriage on the ground of duress. A threat to enforce a just or legal claim through competent authority does not vitiate consent. (Uribe's doctrines outline also quotes the case: the Marriage Law's reference to "force or violence" does not seem to include mere intimidation, at least where it does not in legal effect amount to force or violence.)

Sison v. Te Lay Li (CA)

Doctrine: A marriage effected by force or intimidation may be ratified and confirmed by cohabitation, but the cohabitation must be voluntary. It "must be something more than merely living together in the same house or even occupying the same bed." It is the living together of the parties as husband and wife, including sexual relations.

Villanueva v. Court of Appeals (not in Uribe's syllabus; from the B29 repository)

The husband sued to annul, claiming that his consent was obtained through threats and that he was deceived into believing the wife was pregnant with his child. Doctrine: Annulment denied. Fraud and duress must be proved by clear evidence. A party alleging intimidation must show that the force or duress was so overwhelming it left him no choice but to marry, and that he stopped cohabiting once it ceased. An extended, unexplained delay in suing belies the claimed intimidation, and an admission of consensual sexual relations after the wedding negates the claimed fraud. Where the fraud alleged is that the wife was carrying another man's child, the husband bears the burden of proving the child is not his.

Wiegel v. Sempio-Diy (1986)

Doctrine (void vs. voidable): Even if the first marriage were vitiated by force against both parties, it would be merely voidable, not void, and therefore valid until annulled. The wife need not present evidence of the force when she remarries without an annulment, because her prior marriage subsists until set aside.

Note (RPC 344, last paragraph): The syllabus cross-references RPC 344: "In cases of seduction, abduction, acts of lasciviousness and rape, the marriage of the offender with the offended party shall extinguish the criminal action or remit the penalty already imposed upon him..." (for rape, now RPC 266-C under RA 8353). An offender who marries the victim to extinguish his criminal liability makes a choice, not a marriage under duress. This is consistent with Tolentino's rule that a marriage to escape prosecution for seduction will not be annulled.
Note (prejudicial question and bigamy): A pending action to annul the second marriage because the accused's consent was obtained by force, violence or intimidation may be a prejudicial question in a bigamy case (Donato v. Luna; Tolentino). If his consent to the second marriage was vitiated, he did not freely contract it. An action to annul the first marriage is not prejudicial, because a voidable marriage is valid until annulled and was subsisting when the second marriage was contracted. A Leviathan samplex notes that Uribe does not subscribe to the jurisprudential reasoning on this point, but the samplex answer remains "annulment of the second marriage."

Marforth, a lawyer and cousin of the pregnant Princess, threatens to file an immorality complaint with the Supreme Court to keep Ricky out of the bar exams unless he marries Princess. Ricky marries her. Voidable for intimidation?

Answer: No. The threat was to enforce a just and legal claim through competent authority (NCC 1335, last par.; Ruiz v. Atienza). It is not "because Marforth is not a party." Intimidation by a third person can vitiate consent (NCC 1336), but this threat was lawful.

Baldo intimidates Judy into marrying him. Two years after the intimidation ceases, Judy decides to freely cohabit with him "in the meantime." More than five years after the wedding she sues. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. She ratified the marriage by freely cohabiting with him after the intimidation ceased (Art. 45(4)). Once ratified, the marriage cannot be set aside even if the prescriptive period (five years from cessation, not from the wedding) has not yet run.

Ground (5): physical incapacity (impotence)

Elements (Tolentino: these must concur)

  1. The incapacity exists at the time of the celebration of the marriage.
  2. It continues up to the time the case for annulment is tried.
  3. It appears to be incurable.
  4. It is unknown to the other contracting party.

The action must also be filed by the injured party within five years after the marriage (Art. 47(5)).

  • Meaning: the incapacity referred to is impotence, a physical condition of the husband or wife in which sexual intercourse with a normal person of the opposite sex is impossible. It may lie with either spouse. Italian decisions test it by the ability to copulate with the wife who is a virgin, not with others who are not.
  • Impotence vs. sterility. Impotence is the lack of power to copulate, the absence of functional capacity for the sexual act. Sterility is a pathological condition that negates reproduction. Sterility is not a ground, because it is almost impossible to diagnose and to prove that it existed at the time of the marriage. The test is copulation, not procreation.
  • Curable by treatment or surgery? Then no annulment, because it does not "appear to be incurable."
  • Old age: annulment is generally denied where impotence is due to old age. The other party should know that old age may bring impotence.
  • Excessive sensibility that makes intercourse practically impossible because of the pain it inflicts may be physical incapacity (Tolentino). Uribe accepted vaginismus as an example of a condition that may make the wife physically incapable.
  • Psychological origin: under Alcazar, non-consummation may come from a physical or structural defect, or from chronic illness and inhibitions or fears arising in whole or in part from psychophysical conditions.
Tolentino (action barred): The law does not say so, but the action on this ground is barred in two cases. (1) The other party knew of the incurable impotence before the marriage, which implies he renounced copulation, a personal right. (2) Both spouses are impotent, the impotence existed before the marriage, continues and appears incurable, because an impotent plaintiff could not have expected copulation. The clean-hands rule applies.
Sarao v. Guevara (CA, 40 O.G. 1st Supp. 263)

The spouses married on June 3, 1936. On the wedding night the wife complained of pain and the husband desisted, and he noticed a foul discharge. With his consent, a tumor was removed together with her uterus and ovaries, which left her sterile but able to copulate. He had lost all desire for her and sued for impotence. Doctrine: Annulment denied. The incapacity to copulate was only temporary, and the defect must be lasting. The test of impotence is the capacity to copulate, not to procreate. Removal of the uterus and ovaries made her sterile but not unfit for intercourse, and barrenness does not invalidate a marriage.

Jimenez v. Cañizares (109 Phil. 273, August 31, 1960)

Joel Jimenez sued to annul because his wife Remedios's genitals were too small for copulation. She did not answer, and she refused to submit to the physical examination the court ordered. On Joel's lone testimony, the trial court annulled the marriage. Doctrine: Set aside. "Impotency being an abnormal condition should not be presumed. The presumption is in favor of potency." The husband's lone testimony that his wife is physically incapable of intercourse is insufficient to tear apart the marriage, because the State is interested in its preservation. Her refusal to be examined shows indifference, but the presumption from suppression of evidence does not arise. Filipino women are by nature "coy, bashful and shy" and would not submit to such an examination unless compelled by competent authority. The court may compel the examination without violating her constitutional rights. It is not self-incrimination, since she is not charged with any offense and is not being made a witness against herself. The case was remanded so she could be examined.

Alcazar v. Alcazar (2009)

Doctrine: Art. 45(5) refers to lack of power to copulate. Incapacity to consummate means the permanent inability of a spouse to perform the complete act of sexual intercourse. Non-consummation may be on the part of the husband or the wife. It may come from a physical or structural defect in the anatomy of one party, or from chronic illness and inhibitions or fears arising in whole or in part from psychophysical conditions. A spouse's failure to come home or to communicate after the wedding is neither impotence under Art. 45(5) nor psychological incapacity under Art. 36.

Doctrine of triennial cohabitation

This doctrine was applied in England and some American courts. If the wife is still a virgin and apt after three years of cohabitation, the husband is presumed impotent, and the burden shifts to him to overcome the presumption. It does not prevent proof of impotence by other evidence (Tolentino).

Uribe (triennial cohabitation): The wife sues and invokes the doctrine. Will the action prosper? He criticized the student who answered "Yes" outright: "Merong disputable presumption so bakit ka magrerely?" The presumption is only disputable, and the student did not even state the doctrine.
  • State the doctrine with its reckoning point: three years of cohabitation from the celebration, the wife remaining a virgin.
  • The wife must first show the court the facts that make the doctrine apply.
  • Once those facts are shown, the burden to prove potency falls on the husband, not the wife.
  • The other Art. 45(5) requisites still apply: incapacity existing at the wedding, continuing, apparently incurable, unknown to the plaintiff, and suit within five years after the marriage.
  • The doctrine is a foreign one. Philippine jurisprudence starts from the presumption of potency (Jimenez).
Uribe (refusal to be examined): The husband sues, the wife refuses a physical examination, and the court annuls on the basis of her refusal. Is the judgment proper? No (Jimenez v. Cañizares). The husband's remedy is a motion to order the examination. If she invokes self-incrimination, the objection fails because this is not a criminal case. If she still refuses, she may be cited in contempt. "If there are 2–3 reasons, ubusin mo ang reasons in an exam."

The day after the wedding, John tells Marsha he is impotent. She stays with him for two years. Is she now estopped from suing?

Answer: No. Art. 45(5) has no ratification clause, so cohabitation does not bar the action. Estoppel applies only if she knew before the marriage. She may sue within five years after the marriage.

A and B married on March 1, 2019. On the wedding night B discovered that A was incurably impotent. B stayed, hoping A would seek treatment, and sued on June 1, 2024. Will it prosper?

Answer: No, the action has prescribed. Art. 47(5) counts five years "after the marriage," not from discovery, so the period ended in March 2024. Ratification is not the reason, because ground (5) cannot be ratified.

  2. It is found to be serious.

Ratification vs. prescription

Tolentino: A voidable marriage is generally subject to ratification through continued voluntary cohabitation by the party entitled to sue, after the cause of nullity has ceased to exist.
  • The law fixes no minimum length. The cohabitation need not last the whole prescriptive period, since otherwise the action would be barred by prescription, not by ratification. It must last long enough, after the cause has ceased, to support a reasonable inference that the party prefers to continue the marriage (citing Castro v. Dabu).
  • Once ratification has taken place, the marriage becomes perfectly valid. A later change of mind does not revive the nullity, even if the prescriptive period has not lapsed.
  • Ratification refers to the right of action itself. Prescription refers only to the remedy. Ratification cures the defect existing at the time of the marriage and validates it. Prescription bars the remedy because the period lapsed.

What cannot be ratified (Tolentino)

  1. Incurable impotence (45(5)).
  2. Incurable STD (45(6)). For both (1) and (2), the law gives no ratification clause. The physical defect never ceases, so the cause of nullity never ceases to exist. It defeats a principal purpose of marriage, procreation. Ratification cures a defect in consent, not a physical defect. The action survives as long as the prescriptive period has not expired.
  3. Insanity, as to the sane spouse who married without knowledge. The insane spouse can ratify after recovering reason, but the sane spouse is not barred however long he cohabits after learning of the insanity.

Estoppel (Tolentino): parties who cannot sue

  • A sane spouse who knew of the other's insanity at the time of the marriage, e.g., one who lived with the allegedly insane wife for years and had children with her.
  • A spouse who knew of the other's incurable impotence at the time of the marriage. He is deemed to have renounced copulation.
  • A spouse who knew of the other's serious and incurable STD at the time of the marriage.
  • Both spouses impotent: clean hands.

Who may NOT sue

  • The guilty party, e.g., the spouse who committed the fraud or used the intimidation, or the party who was of age and married a person aged 18 to 20 without consent.
  • Heirs or creditors, after death. Art. 47 lists the parties exclusively. A voidable marriage cannot be attacked after the death of either party; even relatives of the insane may sue only "before the death of either party." Compare void marriages, which heirs may question after death in a proper proceeding (Niñal v. Bayadog).
  • The State is not a party. It intervenes only through the prosecutor or the OSG to prevent collusion (Art. 48).
Uribe (personality of a 19-year-old to sue): "One of the contracting parties was 19 at the time of the marriage, without parental consent. Six months after, he filed an action for annulment. A, 19, married B without his parents' consent. They lived together continuously until A was 23, when A sued. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. Although filed within five years after 21, the action is barred by ratification: A freely cohabited with B after attaining 21. Once ratified, the marriage cannot be set aside even if the period has not run.

B was 19 when she married A without parental consent. After the birth of their third child she left the conjugal home and sued for lack of parental consent. Will it prosper? (Recitation situationer; no recorded answer.)

Answer: It depends on her age when she left and when she sued. If she cohabited freely with A after turning 21, the marriage is ratified and the action fails. If she sued more than five years after turning 21, it has prescribed. Only if she left before 21, or sued within five years after 21 without free cohabitation after 21, does it prosper. (4) Future support;
(5) The jurisdiction of courts;
(6) Future legitime.
  • Purpose: to prevent collusion between the parties in obtaining an annulment (Tolentino).
  • Policy: the State attorney intervenes to emphasize that marriage is more than a mere contract. It is a social institution in which the State is actually interested, and its continuation or interruption cannot be made to depend on the parties themselves (Tolentino).
  • Collusion is an agreement between the parties to obtain a decree by fabricating or suppressing evidence, or by pretending a ground exists. It is a fraud upon the court.
  • Effect of collusion: NCC 221 declared every collusion to obtain a decree of legal separation or annulment void. The Family Code repealed it without a similar rule. Tolentino nonetheless submits that a decree obtained by collusion, where no cause really existed, must be held absolutely void as against public policy.
  • Stipulation of facts and confession of judgment are barred because the court must be convinced by evidence that the ground exists. The validity of a marriage cannot be compromised (NCC 2035(2)).
  • The same rule appears for legal separation in Art. 60.
Tolentino: It has been held that a judgment annulling a marriage or granting legal separation based on a stipulation of facts may be promulgated when the possibility of collusion is remote, e.g., when the interests of two wives conflict (citing Cardenas v. Cardenas). This is an exception developed under the old law. Under the Family Code and A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, Sec. 17(2), "no judgment on the pleadings, summary judgment, or confession of judgment shall be allowed." In Uribe's quiz, give the codal rule.
Jocson v. Robles (22 SCRA 521, February 10, 1968)

In an action to annul a marriage on the ground of bigamy, summary judgment was sought on the strength of affidavits that practically admitted the prior subsisting marriage. Doctrine: The Court of Domestic Relations correctly denied summary judgment. Then NCC 88 and 101 (now FC 48 and 60) expressly prohibit a decree of annulment upon a stipulation of facts or a confession of judgment. Affidavits that practically amount to these methods are not countenanced. The grounds must be proved by evidence received in a trial, so that collusion is guarded against. (Uribe's doctrines outline also attaches to this case the rule that a subsequent marriage during a prior subsisting one is void unless the Art. 41 absence and well-founded belief are present.)

Tolentino v. Villanueva (56 SCRA 1, March 15, 1974)

The husband sued to annul. The wife did not answer, and he asked that she be declared in default; the court instead referred the case to the fiscal to investigate whether there was collusion. The Supreme Court sustained the trial court. Doctrine: Then NCC 88 and 101 (now FC 48 and 60) prohibit a decision in suits for annulment or legal separation based on a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment. Where the defendant does not appear, the court shall order the prosecuting attorney to inquire whether there is collusion and, if none, to intervene for the State to prevent fabrication of evidence for the plaintiff. A default judgment is not the route.

A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC: procedural safeguards

  • Sec. 4, venue: the Family Court of the province or city where the petitioner or the respondent has been residing for at least six months before filing. For a non-resident respondent, where he may be found in the Philippines, at the petitioner's election.
  • Sec. 5, petition: it alleges the complete facts constituting the cause of action, and the names and ages of the common children, the property regime and the properties. It is verified and accompanied by a certification against forum shopping signed personally by the petitioner, never by counsel or an attorney-in-fact. A copy is served on the OSG and the City or Provincial Prosecutor within five days from filing. Non-compliance may be a ground for immediate dismissal.
  • Sec. 7, motion to dismiss: none is allowed except for lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter or the parties. Other grounds may be raised as affirmative defenses in the answer.
  • Sec. 8, answer: due within 15 days from service, or 30 days from the last publication. It is verified by the respondent himself. If he fails to answer, the court shall not declare him in default. If no answer is filed or the answer tenders no issue, the court orders the public prosecutor to investigate whether collusion exists.
  • Sec. 9, prosecutor's report: due within one month of the order. If the prosecutor finds collusion, the court sets the report for hearing and dismisses the petition if convinced. If there is no collusion, the case proceeds to pre-trial, where the prosecutor appears for the State.
  • Sec. 11, pre-trial: mandatory.
  • Sec. 17, trial: the judge personally conducts the trial, and there is no delegation to a commissioner except on property relations. The grounds must be proved. No judgment on the pleadings, summary judgment or confession of judgment is allowed. The court may exclude persons without direct interest, and the records are confidential.
  • Sec. 19, decision: the decree of annulment issues only after compliance with Arts. 50 and 51. The decision becomes final 15 days from notice to the parties, the OSG and the prosecutor if no motion for reconsideration, new trial or appeal is filed. The entry of judgment is registered in the civil registry where the marriage was recorded and where the Family Court is located.
  • Sec. 22, decree: issued after (1) registration of the entry of judgment; (2) registration of the approved partition in the Register of Deeds where the real properties are located; and (3) delivery of the children's presumptive legitimes in cash, property or sound securities.
  • Sec. 24, death of a party: if a party dies before entry of judgment, the case is closed and terminated, without prejudice to settlement of the estate in the regular courts. If a party dies after entry of judgment, the judgment binds the parties and their successors in interest in the estate settlement.
Quiz trap: "An action for annulment shall in no case be tried before six months shall have elapsed since the filing of the petition" is FALSE. The six-month cooling-off period (Art. 58) applies only to legal separation. "A judgment of annulment may be based on a stipulation of facts provided both parties are assisted by counsel" is FALSE, since assistance of counsel does not matter.

Art. 49: support, custody and visitation pendente lite

Art. 49, FC. During the pendency of the action and in the absence of adequate provisions in a written agreement between the spouses, the Court shall provide for the support of the spouses and the custody and support of their common children. The Court shall give paramount consideration to the moral and material welfare of said children and their choice of the parent with whom they wish to remain as provided to in Title IX. It shall also provide for appropriate visitation rights of the other parent. (n)
Art. 198, FC. During the proceedings for legal separation or for annulment of marriage, and for declaration of nullity of marriage, the spouses and their children shall be supported from the properties of the absolute community or the conjugal partnership. After the final judgment granting the petition, the obligation of mutual support between the spouses ceases. However, in case of legal separation, the court may order that the guilty spouse shall give support to the innocent one, specifying the terms of such order.
Art. 213, FC. In case of separation of the parents, parental authority shall be exercised by the parent designated by the Court. The Court shall take into account all relevant considerations, especially the choice of the child over seven years of age, unless the parent chosen is unfit.
No child under seven years of age shall be separated from the mother unless the court finds compelling reasons to order otherwise.
  • Spouses' agreement first. The spouses get the first opportunity to provide, in a written agreement, for their support and the custody and support of the children. The court checks that the provisions are adequate, and if they are not, it orders the necessary supplement (Tolentino). The court steps in only "in the absence of adequate provisions in a written agreement."
  • Source of support pendente lite: the absolute community or conjugal partnership (Art. 198). Support pendente lite is given precisely because the marriage still subsists until the final judgment.
  • After the final judgment of annulment, mutual support between the former spouses ceases (Art. 198). Unlike legal separation, no support may be ordered from the guilty to the innocent spouse. Support for the children continues, since parent-child support is independent of the marriage.
  • Custody: paramount consideration goes to the children's moral and material welfare and to their choice of parent. A child over seven is ordinarily followed unless the chosen parent is unfit, and the tender-age presumption applies to children under seven. Visitation rights go to the other parent.

A.M. No. 02-11-12-SC (provisional orders)

  • Upon receipt of a verified petition for nullity, annulment or legal separation, and at any time during the proceedings, the court, motu proprio or on sworn application, may issue provisional orders and protection orders, with or without a hearing, enforceable immediately, with or without bond.
  • Spousal support comes from the community or partnership property, absent an adequate written agreement. The court considers the standard of living and the listed factors, and may order salary deduction.
  • Child support also comes from the common property, in proportion to the resources of the giver and the needs of the recipient.
  • Provisional custody, order of preference: (1) both parents jointly; (2) either parent, especially the one chosen by a child over seven, unless unfit; (3) the surviving grandparent, or the one chosen by the child; (4) the eldest brother or sister over 21; (5) the actual custodian over 21; (6) any other suitable person. The temporary custodian must give five days' notice of any plan to change the child's residence or take the child out for more than three days.
  • Visitation goes to the parent not awarded provisional custody, unless unfit or disqualified.
  • Other provisional relief: a hold departure order (no child may be taken out of the country without court order); an order of protection; and administration of common property, by appointing the applicant or a third person as receiver or sole administrator if a spouse abandons the other without just cause or fails to meet family obligations.
Quiz trap: "During the pendency of an action for annulment, the Court shall provide for support and custody even if the spouses have executed a written agreement adequately providing for such matters" is FALSE. The court acts only in the absence of adequate written provisions.
Uribe (annulment vs. legal separation, advising the client): "What advice would you give, legal separation or annulment?" Ask the client what outcome he or she wants: to end the marriage or not. Annulment dissolves the bond, so the parties may remarry. It also ends mutual support and the spouses' right to inherit from each other. Legal separation keeps the bond, so a spouse may still be entitled to support from the guilty spouse (Art. 198) and reconciliation remains possible. The same facts may be grounds for both (e.g., drug addiction or homosexuality existing at the wedding and concealed). Note also Uribe's distinction: "With annulment, there will be a dissolution [of the property regime]. If void, co-ownership governs; there is nothing to dissolve."

Art. 50: effects of the final judgment

Art. 50, FC. The effects provided for by paragraphs (2), (3), (4) and (5) of Article 43 and by Article 44 shall also apply in the proper cases to marriages which are declared ab initio or annulled by final judgment under Articles 40 and 45.
The final judgment in such cases shall provide for the liquidation, partition and distribution of the properties of the spouses, the custody and support of the common children, and the delivery of their presumptive legitimes, unless such matters had been adjudicated in previous judicial proceedings.
All creditors of the spouses as well as of the absolute community or the conjugal partnership shall be notified of the proceedings for liquidation.
In the partition, the conjugal dwelling and the lot on which it is situated, shall be adjudicated in accordance with the provisions of Articles 102 and 129.
Note on the text: Some printings, including the B29 reviewer and online copies of the official text, read "delivery of third presumptive legitimes." Tolentino prints "their presumptive legitimes," the evident meaning. Similarly, the first paragraph as commonly printed says "declared ab initio"; Tolentino prints "declared void ab initio."
Art. 43 (2)–(5), FC (applied by Art. 50). The termination of the subsequent marriage referred to in the preceding Article shall produce the following effects: ...
(2) The absolute community of property or the conjugal partnership, as the case may be, shall be dissolved and liquidated, but if either spouse contracted said marriage in bad faith, his or her share of the net profits of the community property or conjugal partnership property shall be forfeited in favor of the common children or, if there are none, the children of the guilty spouse by a previous marriage or in default of children, the innocent spouse;
(3) Donations by reason of marriage shall remain valid, except that if the donee contracted the marriage in bad faith, such donations made to said donee are revoked by operation of law;
(4) The innocent spouse may revoke the designation of the other spouse who acted in bad faith as beneficiary in any insurance policy, even if such designation be stipulated as irrevocable; and
(5) The spouse who contracted the subsequent marriage in bad faith shall be disqualified to inherit from the innocent spouse by testate and intestate succession. (n)
Art. 44, FC. If both spouses of the subsequent marriage acted in bad faith, said marriage shall be void ab initio and all donations by reason of marriage and testamentary dispositions made by one in favor of the other are revoked by operation of law. (n)

The effects of annulment, itemized

  1. The marriage bond is severed. The former spouses are no longer married and may remarry after complying with Arts. 52–53.
  2. Property regime (Art. 43(2)): the ACP or CPG is dissolved and liquidated. A voidable marriage, unlike a void one, had a valid regime until annulled. The share in the net profits of the spouse in bad faith, i.e., the one who caused the defect, such as the defrauding or intimidating spouse, is forfeited in favor of: (a) the common children; if none, (b) the children of the guilty spouse by a previous marriage; and in default of children, (c) the innocent spouse.
  3. Donations by reason of marriage (Art. 43(3)): they remain valid, except those made to a donee who contracted the marriage in bad faith, which are revoked by operation of law.
  4. Insurance (Art. 43(4)): the innocent spouse may revoke the designation of the bad-faith spouse as beneficiary, even if the designation is irrevocable. Revocation is optional and not automatic.
  5. Succession (Art. 43(5)): the bad-faith spouse is disqualified from inheriting from the innocent spouse, testate and intestate.
  6. Both in bad faith (Art. 44): donations by reason of marriage and testamentary dispositions in favor of each other are revoked by operation of law.
  7. The judgment must provide for (a) liquidation, partition and distribution of the properties; (b) custody and support of the common children; and (c) delivery of presumptive legitimes, unless already adjudicated in earlier proceedings.
  8. Creditors of the spouses and of the ACP or CPG must be notified of the liquidation.
  9. Conjugal dwelling and lot (Arts. 102 and 129): adjudicated, unless otherwise agreed, to the spouse with whom the majority of the common children choose to remain. Children below seven are deemed to have chosen the mother unless the court decides otherwise. If there is no majority, the court decides in the children's best interests.
  10. Children: legitimate if conceived or born before the judgment becomes final and executory (Art. 54). Their custody and support are settled in the judgment, and their presumptive legitimes are delivered (Art. 51).
  11. Mutual support between the former spouses ceases (Art. 198).
  12. Surname (NCC 371): "In case of annulment of marriage, and the wife is the guilty party, she shall resume her maiden name and surname. If she is the innocent spouse, she may resume her maiden name and surname. However, she may choose to continue employing her former husband's surname, unless: (1) The court decrees otherwise, or (2) She or the former husband is married again to another person."
  13. Legitimation is not affected: "The annulment of a voidable marriage shall not affect the legitimation" (Art. 178, FC).
  14. Damages: the Family Code dropped NCC 91, which listed cases for damages upon annulment. Tolentino submits that damages may still be recovered under NCC 20 and 21 from a party, or a third person, whose bad faith or unlawful act caused the nullity, e.g., a girl's father who forced the man to marry her under threat of death. A party in good faith is not liable, and if both acted in bad faith neither recovers.
Tolentino (uniformity of judgment): Arts. 50–54 give the same effects to the setting aside of all defective marriages: void ab initio, voidable, or a subsequent marriage terminated on reappearance. They are (1) liquidation, partition and distribution; (2) revocation by operation of law of donations by the innocent spouse to the spouse in bad faith; (3) legitimacy of children conceived or born before finality, their custody and support, and delivery of presumptive legitimes; (4) optional revocation of insurance designations; (5) disqualification of the bad-faith spouse from inheriting; (6) the conjugal dwelling going to the spouse chosen by the children; (7) revocation of donations and testamentary provisions if both acted in bad faith; (8) recording; and (9) freedom to remarry only after all of this, otherwise the subsequent marriage is void. He calls the forfeiture of all net profits "too harsh a penalty for bad faith" where most of the property came from the guilty spouse's own labor.
Note (scope of Art. 50, Diño v. Diño, 2011; Valdes v. RTC, 1996): The liquidation, partition and distribution under Art. 50, and the rule that the decree issues only after them (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, Sec. 19(1)), apply only to marriages declared void under Art. 40 (a subsequent marriage contracted without a prior judicial declaration of nullity) and to marriages annulled under Art. 45. For other void marriages, e.g., Art. 36, no license, or incest, there was never an ACP or CPG. Property relations are governed by co-ownership under Art. 147 or 148, and the decree issues upon finality without waiting for liquidation. This matches Uribe's point: "With annulment, there will be a dissolution. If void, co-ownership governs; there is nothing to dissolve."
Quiz trap: Art. 50 applies paragraphs (2), (3), (4) and (5) of Art. 43, not (1). Legitimacy of the children is covered separately by Art. 54. It applies to marriages under Arts. 40 and 45, not Art. 36.
Note (Uribe MCQ: "The decree of annulment shall have the following effects, except:"). The choices are (a) ACP or CPG dissolved and liquidated; (b) donations by reason of marriage remain valid, except those to a bad-faith donee, which are revoked by operation of law; (c) the innocent spouse may revoke the insurance designation of the bad-faith spouse; (d) all of the above; (e) none of the above. On the text of Art. 50, all of (a) to (c) are effects of annulment, since they are Art. 43(2), (3) and (4), so the answer is (e) none of the above. One reconciliation: Art. 43(3) via Art. 50 governs donations between the spouses, and Art. 86(3) governs donations by third persons. Be ready to explain both. The codal answer is (e).

Art. 51: presumptive legitimes

Art. 51, FC. In said partition, the value of the presumptive legitimes of all common children, computed as of the date of the final judgment of the trial court, shall be delivered in cash, property or sound securities, unless the parties, by mutual agreement judicially approved, had already provided for such matters.
The children or their guardian or the trustee of their property may ask for the enforcement of the judgment.
The delivery of the presumptive legitimes herein prescribed shall in no way prejudice the ultimate successional rights of the children accruing upon the death of either or both of the parents; but the value of the properties already received under the decree of annulment or absolute nullity shall be considered as advances on their legitime. (n)
  • What it is: the presumptive legitime is the legitime each common child would receive from each parent as if the parent had died on the date of the final judgment. Under NCC 888, the legitime of legitimate children is one-half of the parent's estate (Tolentino).
  • Valued as of the date of the final judgment of the trial court, not the date of finality and not the date of filing.
  • Form of delivery: cash, property or sound securities.
  • Exception: the parties had already provided for it by mutual agreement judicially approved.
  • Who may enforce: the children, their guardian, or the trustee of their property.
  • Effect on succession: delivery does not prejudice the children's ultimate successional rights when either or both parents die. What they received is treated as an advance on their legitime.
  • Presumptive legitimes are required only upon annulment or judicial declaration of nullity (Arts. 50–53). They are not required upon dissolution by death, and not in legal separation, where the bond is not severed.
Note on the text: The B29 reviewer prints "upon the death of either of both of the parents." Tolentino prints "either or both."
Quiz trap: "The delivery of the presumptive legitimes shall be in full satisfaction of the successional rights of the children, and the value shall not be considered as advances" is FALSE on both counts.

Arts. 52–53: recording, and remarriage void without it

Art. 52, FC. The judgment of annulment or of absolute nullity of the marriage, the partition and distribution of the properties of the spouses and the delivery of the children's presumptive legitimes shall be recorded in the appropriate civil registry and registries of property; otherwise, the same shall not affect third persons. (n)
Art. 53, FC. Either of the former spouses may marry again after compliance with the requirements of the immediately preceding Article; otherwise, the subsequent marriage shall be null and void. (n)
  • What must be recorded (Art. 52): (1) the judgment of annulment or nullity; (2) the partition and distribution of the properties; and (3) the delivery of the presumptive legitimes. They are recorded in the civil registry and the registries of property.
  • Sanction under Art. 52: if not recorded, they "shall not affect third persons." They are not void.
  • Sanction under Art. 53: a former spouse who remarries without complying with Art. 52 contracts a void marriage. This is the marriage listed in Art. 35(6): "those subsequent marriages that are void under Article 53."
  • Children of that void subsequent marriage are nonetheless legitimate (Art. 54, second sentence).
  • Scope: only for remarriage after annulment or a judicial declaration of nullity. It does not apply where the prior marriage was dissolved by death. There the sanction for remarrying without liquidating is a mandatory regime of complete separation of property in the subsequent marriage (Arts. 103 and 130); the subsequent marriage stays valid.
  • The Family Code imposes no waiting period (300 days or one year) before remarriage. The old "premature marriage" under RPC 351 was repealed by RA 10655 (2015), and it never affected civil validity anyway.
Uribe: Situation: A died, the surviving spouse remarried, then also died, and the heirs claimed the second marriage was void for failure to deliver presumptive legitimes. The heirs are not correct. The legitime is "presumptive" precisely because no one has died, and Art. 53 speaks only of nullity and annulment. In legal separation, the bond is not severed, so a separated spouse who remarries contracts a bigamous marriage. That is not because legitimes were undelivered. It is because he is still married.
Quiz trap: "...shall be recorded...; otherwise, the same shall be null and void" is FALSE, because the codal says "shall not affect third persons." Nullity is the sanction of Art. 53, and it falls on the subsequent marriage.

H and W's marriage is annulled. H wants to marry S as soon as possible. How soon, and what are the requisites?

Answer: As soon as H complies with Art. 52: record the judgment of annulment, the partition and distribution of properties, and the delivery of the children's presumptive legitimes in the civil registry and registries of property. He must also meet the ordinary essential and formal requisites, including a license (Art. 13 requires presenting the judicial decree of annulment). Without Art. 52 compliance, the H–S marriage is void (Art. 53). Children of H and W conceived or born before finality are legitimate, and so are the children of H and S (Art. 54).

Art. 54: status of children

Art. 54, FC. Children conceived or born before the judgment of annulment or absolute nullity of the marriage under Article 36 has become final and executory shall be considered legitimate. Children conceived or born of the subsequent marriage under Article 53 shall likewise be legitimate.
  • "Conceived or born" means either suffices. A child conceived before finality but born after it is legitimate. A child born before finality, even if conceived before the marriage, is legitimate.
  • The cut-off is finality, i.e., when the judgment "has become final and executory." It is not promulgation and not filing.
  • The first sentence covers: (a) annulled (voidable) marriages; and (b) void marriages under Art. 36 only, among void marriages.
  • The second sentence covers children of the void subsequent marriage under Art. 53.
  • Children of other void marriages (no license, incestuous, public policy, bigamous outside Art. 41, below 18) are illegitimate (Art. 165).
  • Under the Civil Code, children conceived of voidable marriages before the decree of annulment were also legitimate, so the rule for voidable marriages has not changed (Tolentino).
Uribe: "If the child was born after this decree was promulgated?" The child is legitimate as long as the child was conceived before the judgment became final. A child both conceived and born after finality is illegitimate, because the parents are no longer married. "Huwag mong gamitin yung for the best interest of the child." Treating such a child as legitimate is unfair to the other children, whose shares would be reduced.
Quiz trap: Several variants appear.
  • "Children conceived and born before..." is still true for the children it covers, but narrower than the law.
  • "...before the promulgation of judgment..." was marked TRUE in the samplex, since every child conceived or born before promulgation is also before finality.
  • "Children born after the dissolution of the marriage may be legitimate" is TRUE because of "may": conception before finality, or a child of the husband born after his death.
  • "If a child was born after the judicial declaration of nullity of his parents' marriage, the child is illegitimate" is FALSE, because he may have been conceived before finality of an Art. 36 judgment.
  • "Children conceived or born of the subsequent marriage under Art. 53 are illegitimate since the parties failed to comply with Art. 52" is FALSE.

  • 19-year-old's personality to sue: he has personality. RA 6809 makes him of age with no qualification (see the note in the Art. 47 section).
  • B was 20 without parental consent, and the marriage may be valid: YES. It is valid until annulled, and B may have been previously married with consent.
  • Parents' period: the "two years after majority" item is TRUE and the "five years after majority" item is FALSE.
  • Free cohabitation as a defense, EXCEPT: when the choices were (a) lack of parental consent, (b) unsound mind, (c) fraud, (d) all of the above, (e) none, his 2025 key was (a). The parent's action before 21 cannot be defeated by the child's cohabitation, and cohabitation counts only after 21. When "physically incapable of consummating" is among the choices, that is the answer, since ground (5) has no ratification clause. On the text, free cohabitation is a qualified defense for grounds (1)–(4), so "none of the above" is arguable in the first version. Follow his key (a).
  • "This is your child": the action will NOT prosper, because the pregnancy was not concealed.
  • Prostitute wife: no annulment. The list is exclusive, and deceit as to character or chastity is excluded.
  • 30-day penalty conviction: the action may prosper, since moral turpitude does not depend on the penalty (theft; BP 22).
  • STD discovered six months after the wedding: it depends on whether the STD existed at the time of the marriage. Herpes: prosper only if it existed then, is serious, and appears incurable.
  • HIV knowledge (Yvette and Joseph): a spouse who knew of the STD before the wedding is not an "injured party" and cannot annul.
  • STD and free cohabitation: "Depends if it is a ground for annulment [45(6)] or for fraud [46(3)]." It is no defense under 45(6) and is a defense under fraud.
  • "Transmissible," not "transmitted": the disease need not have been acquired through sex. Syphilis is curable (penicillin). HIV and AIDS are incurable.
  • Impotence, wife refuses examination: a judgment based on the refusal is not valid (Jimenez). The remedy is a motion to compel, and contempt if she still refuses. Self-incrimination does not apply.
  • Triennial cohabitation: it is only a disputable presumption, so state the doctrine and its reckoning point. The burden then falls on the husband, but the wife must first establish the facts that trigger it.
  • Vaginismus is an example of a wife's physical incapacity.
  • Alcoholism that began after the wedding: not a ground, because fraud requires a condition existing and concealed at the wedding. The remedy is legal separation.
  • Surname after annulment: a guilty wife shall resume her maiden name, because "di na sila kasal." An innocent wife may keep her husband's surname, subject to NCC 371.
  • Child born after the decree: legitimate if conceived before finality. "Don't use 'best interest of the child'" to make an illegitimate child legitimate.
  • Support after annulment: none between the former spouses, because they are no longer married. Contrast legal separation (Art. 198).
  • Presumptive legitimes on death: not required. Arts. 52–53 apply only to annulment and nullity.
  • "Don't assume. Huwag mag-imbento." Establish that the ground existed at the celebration before discussing prescription or ratification. "There can be no ratification if it is a valid marriage. There can be no prescription if he does not have a right of action."
  • Prejudicial question to bigamy: annulment of the second marriage on the ground of vitiated consent (Donato v. Luna), noting his reservation. Annulment of the first marriage is not prejudicial, since the first marriage was subsisting.

Application drills (Uribe style)

A and B, both 25, marry. A later discovers B had been convicted, by final judgment, of reckless imprudence resulting in damage to property. Annulment?

Answer: No. A quasi-offense by negligence does not involve moral turpitude. Art. 46(1) requires a crime involving moral turpitude, and the list is exclusive.

W was three months pregnant by X when she married H. She concealed it, and H discovers it eight months after the wedding when the child is born. Four years later H sues. Meanwhile he had continued living with W, with sexual relations, for three years after the birth. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. The ground exists under Art. 46(2), and the action is within five years of discovery. But H, with full knowledge of the facts constituting the fraud, freely cohabited with W as husband and wife, which ratified the marriage (Art. 45(3)). Under Sison v. Te Lay Li, cohabitation means living together as husband and wife, including sexual relations.

Same facts, but H had premarital intercourse with W before the wedding, and she told him the child was his.

Answer: No annulment. There was no concealment of the pregnancy (Uribe), and H comes to court with unclean hands as a party to her premarital unchastity (Tolentino, Carris v. Carris).

On the wedding day, the bride's brothers held a gun to the groom's head. Two weeks later he escaped and never returned. Seven years after the wedding (six years after he escaped) he sues. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. The intimidation ceased when he escaped, and the five-year period under Art. 47(4) ran from then and has lapsed. There was no ratification, but the action has prescribed.

A married B, who was insane at the time; A did not know. They lived together for ten years, and A then sues. B's mother opposes, claiming ratification by A's ten-year cohabitation.

Answer: The action prospers if B's insanity at the wedding is proved. A sane spouse without knowledge may sue at any time before the death of either party (Art. 47(2)), and the law provides no ratification by the sane spouse's cohabitation (Tolentino). Had A known of the insanity at the wedding, he would be in estoppel.

H sues to annul for impotence one year after the wedding. W is impotent, but H was also impotent before the marriage and still is, incurably. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. Where both spouses are incurably impotent from before the marriage, the plaintiff could not have expected copulation, and the clean-hands rule bars him (Tolentino).

During the annulment case, W and H sign a compromise in which H admits the fraud, and they ask the court to render judgment on it. Proper?

Answer: No. No judgment may be based on a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment (Art. 48; A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, Sec. 17(2)), and the validity of a marriage cannot be compromised (NCC 2035(2); Jocson v. Robles). The prosecutor must appear to prevent collusion. If H does not answer, he is not declared in default, and the prosecutor investigates collusion (Sec. 8; Tolentino v. Villanueva).

H dies while his annulment case against W is pending, before entry of judgment. May his heirs continue the case to exclude W from his estate?

Answer: No. The case is closed and terminated (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, Sec. 24(a)), without prejudice to estate settlement. A voidable marriage cannot be attacked after a party's death, so W inherits as surviving spouse unless a ground of disqualification applies.

The marriage of H and W is annulled because H intimidated W into marrying him. H had donated a house to W on account of the marriage, and W had donated a car to H. W also had H as the irrevocable beneficiary of her life insurance. Effects?

Answer: H is the spouse in bad faith. The donation to H (the car) is revoked by operation of law (Art. 43(3) via Art. 50). The donation to W (the house) remains valid. W may revoke H's designation as beneficiary even though it is irrevocable (Art. 43(4)). H is disqualified from inheriting from W, testate or intestate (Art. 43(5)). H's share in the net profits of the ACP or CPG is forfeited in favor of the common children, otherwise his children by a previous marriage, otherwise W (Art. 43(2)). W, the innocent spouse, may resume her maiden name or keep H's surname (NCC 371).

Last-minute checklist

  • Art. 4: absence = void (except 35(2)); defect in essential = voidable (Art. 45 only); irregularity in formal = valid, with liability. "No voidable in formal requisites."
  • Art. 45 intro: all grounds must exist "at the time of the marriage." Supervening causes go to legal separation, not annulment.
  • Six grounds: (1) 18 to below 21, no consent of parents, guardian or person with substitute parental authority, in that order; (2) unsound mind; (3) fraud (Art. 46 only); (4) force, intimidation, undue influence; (5) physical incapacity to consummate, continuing and apparently incurable; (6) STD found serious and apparently incurable.
  • Below 18 is void even with parental consent (35(1); RA 11596 makes child marriage criminal). Lack of parental advice (21 to 25) leaves the marriage valid and only suspends the license for three months.
  • RA 6809: majority is 18, but parental consent for marriage until 21.
  • Ratification clauses exist only for grounds (1)–(4): (1) after 21, and both live together; (2) after coming to reason, by the insane party; (3) with full knowledge of the fraud; (4) after the force ceased. Grounds (5) and (6) cannot be ratified. The sane spouse is not barred by cohabitation (Tolentino's third case).
  • Art. 46 is an exclusive list: (1) conviction by final judgment of a crime involving moral turpitude, not disclosed, with no minimum penalty (the NCC's two-year rule was dropped); (2) concealment by the wife that she was pregnant at the wedding by a man other than her husband; (3) concealment of an STD regardless of its nature, existing at the time of the marriage; (4) concealment of drug addiction, habitual alcoholism, homosexuality or lesbianism existing at the time of the marriage.
  • Last paragraph: no other deceit as to character, health, rank, fortune or chastity (C-H-R-F-C).
  • Cases: Anaya (premarital relations of the husband, not fraud); Buccat (visible pregnancy, child 89 days after the wedding, no fraud); Aquino v. Delizo (four months pregnant, "naturally plump," fraud); Villacorta 2021 (not pregnant at the wedding, so no 46(2)); Almelor (concealment, not homosexuality per se).
  • Cure of the STD, addiction or alcoholism does not bar a fraud action.
  • Intimidation (NCC 1335): reasonable, well-grounded fear of an imminent, grave evil to the person or property of self, spouse, descendants or ascendants, judged by age, sex and condition. A just or legal threat does not vitiate (Ruiz v. Atienza: bar and immorality charge; seduction cases). A third person can intimidate (1336).
  • Free cohabitation means more than the same house or bed. It means living as husband and wife, including sexual relations (Sison v. Te Lay Li).
  • Impotence has four requisites: existing at the wedding, continuing to trial, apparently incurable, unknown to the other. Test: copulation, not procreation (Sarao). Sterility is no ground. Presumption of potency, and lone testimony is insufficient (Jimenez). A physical exam is not self-incrimination. Old age and curable conditions do not qualify. Barred if the plaintiff knew, or both are impotent.
  • Triennial cohabitation: wife a virgin and apt after three years of cohabitation, so the husband is presumed impotent and bears the burden. It is only disputable and a foreign doctrine.
  • 45(6) vs. 46(3): serious and incurable vs. any nature; concealment not needed vs. needed; no ratification vs. ratifiable; five years from the wedding vs. five years from discovery.
  • Art. 47 periods: (1) party within 5 years after reaching 21, and parent any time before 21; (2) sane spouse without knowledge, or relatives or guardian, before the death of either party, and the insane spouse during a lucid interval or after regaining sanity; (3) fraud, 5 years after discovery; (4) force, 5 years after cessation; (5) and (6), 5 years after the marriage.
  • 47(2) says "same spouse," which means the sane spouse.
  • Ratification goes to the right; prescription bars only the remedy. Once ratified, no annulment even if the period has not run.
  • Estopped: a spouse who knew of the insanity, impotence or STD at the time of the marriage.
  • Heirs cannot annul. A voidable marriage cannot be attacked after death or collaterally. A party's death before entry of judgment closes the case (A.M. Sec. 24).
  • Art. 48: prosecutor appears in all annulment and nullity cases to prevent collusion and fabrication or suppression of evidence. No judgment on stipulation of facts or confession of judgment (and none on the pleadings or summary judgment, A.M. Sec. 17). No default (Sec. 8). No six-month cooling-off (that is legal separation, Art. 58). NCC 2035: validity of a marriage cannot be compromised. Cases: Jocson v. Robles; Tolentino v. Villanueva.
  • A.M. 02-11-10-SC: venue is where the petitioner or respondent has resided at least six months. The petition is verified and the certification against forum shopping is signed personally. Copies go to the OSG and prosecutor within five days. Motion to dismiss only for lack of jurisdiction. Answer in 15 or 30 days. Prosecutor's report within one month. Decision final 15 days after notice. Decree issues only after Arts. 50–51 compliance.
  • Art. 49: support and custody pendente lite only absent adequate written agreement. Paramount consideration goes to the children's welfare and their choice. Visitation for the other parent. Support pendente lite comes from the ACP or CPG (Art. 198). After the final judgment, mutual support ceases.
  • Art. 50 applies Art. 43 (2), (3), (4), (5) (not (1)) and Art. 44 to marriages under Arts. 40 and 45. The judgment provides for liquidation, partition and distribution, custody and support, and presumptive legitimes. Creditors are notified. The conjugal dwelling goes to the spouse chosen by the majority of the children (Arts. 102 and 129). Diño: Art. 50 liquidation does not apply to Art. 36 or other void marriages (Arts. 147/148).
  • Bad-faith spouse: forfeits net profits (to common children, then his children by a prior marriage, then the innocent spouse); donations to him are revoked by operation of law; his insurance designation is revocable by the innocent spouse even if irrevocable; he is disqualified from inheriting, testate and intestate. Both in bad faith: donations and testamentary dispositions revoked by operation of law.
  • Art. 51: presumptive legitimes valued as of the date of the final judgment of the trial court, delivered in cash, property or sound securities, unless provided for by a judicially approved agreement. Enforceable by the children, guardian or trustee. They are advances on the legitime and do not prejudice ultimate successional rights.
  • Art. 52: record the judgment, partition and distribution, and delivery of legitimes, or they "shall not affect third persons" (not void).
  • Art. 53: remarriage before Art. 52 compliance is null and void (= Art. 35(6)). Applies only after annulment or nullity, not after death (there, Arts. 103 and 130 impose separation of property).
  • Art. 54: children conceived OR born before the judgment of annulment, or of nullity under Art. 36, has become final and executory are legitimate, as are children of Art. 53 void subsequent marriages. Other void marriages produce illegitimate children.
  • Art. 178: annulment of a voidable marriage does not affect legitimation.
  • NCC 371: guilty wife shall resume her maiden name. Innocent wife may resume it or keep her husband's surname, unless the court decrees otherwise or either remarries.
  • Void vs. voidable: void is inexistent, collateral attack allowed, imprescriptible, not ratifiable, attackable after death, co-ownership, children generally illegitimate. Voidable is valid until annulled, direct attack only, prescribes, ratifiable (1–4), not attackable after death, ACP or CPG liquidated, children legitimate.
  • If a void ground and a voidable ground coexist, the marriage is void.
