The Family Home

Title V, Chapter 2, Family Code, Arts. 152–162 (syllabus XII: what constitutes the family home; who may constitute it, Arts. 152 and 161; when deemed constituted, Art. 153; when terminated, Art. 153 compared with Art. 159; beneficiaries, Art. 154; exemptions, Arts. 155 and 160). Cases: Modequillo v. Breva, Siari Valley Estates v. Lucasan, Spouses De Mesa v. Acero, and Salazar v. Felias (listed under XI.B in the syllabus but decided on the family home), plus Patricio v. Dario and the other cases that come up in reviewers and the bar.

What constitutes the family home; who may constitute it (Arts. 152, 156, 161)

Art. 152, FC. The family home, constituted jointly by the husband and the wife or by an unmarried head of a family, is the dwelling house where they and their family reside, and the land on which it is situated. (223a)
Art. 156, FC. The family home must be part of the properties of the absolute community or the conjugal partnership, or of the exclusive properties of either spouse with the latter's consent. It may also be constituted by an unmarried head of a family on his or her own property.
Nevertheless, property that is the subject of a conditional sale on installments where ownership is reserved by the vendor only to guarantee payment of the purchase price may be constituted as a family home. (227a, 228a)
Art. 161, FC. For purposes of availing of the benefits of a family home as provided for in this Chapter, a person may constitute, or be the beneficiary of, only one family home. (n)
  • What: the dwelling house where the family resides and the land on which it stands.
  • Who: (a) the husband and wife jointly, or (b) an unmarried head of a family (on his or her own property).
  • On what property: ACP or CPG property; the exclusive property of either spouse with that spouse's consent; the unmarried head's own property; and, by exception, property bought on a conditional sale on installments where the vendor reserves ownership only to secure payment.
  • Only one family home per person, whether as constitutor or as beneficiary (Art. 161).
Uribe (CivRev 2025 and 2018 situationers): "X and Y are the parents of A. A married B. XY invited AB to construct their house in a parcel of land owned by XY … Later on, there was a judgment against AB … the house and lot were levied upon. AB claimed that levy was not valid because it is their family home. Is this claim tenable?" The 2018 answer: no, under the case in the syllabus (De Mesa): they should have set up the claim when the property was levied upon. On whether spouses must own both the house and the lot: Art. 152 says "the dwelling house … and the land on which it is situated", but Sir pushed back on that reading. The purpose of the family home is to serve as the family's dwelling, "where they can rest and sleep". Many families in Metro Manila live in condominium units and do not own the land. "Kahit dwelling house can be constituted as family home by itself. Moreso for the purpose of the law." The 2018 situationer: "A family home may be constituted in a property which is neither owned by one of the constituting parties nor is a community or conjugal property. Do you agree?" Answer: yes (e.g., the conditional sale on installments under Art. 156, 2nd paragraph). Sir added that a decided case requiring ownership of the land "is not consistent with the policy of the FC".
Conflict: Uribe vs. the text, Tolentino and jurisprudence. Art. 156 requires the family home to be ACP or CPG property, a spouse's exclusive property (with consent), or the unmarried head's own property; the only textual exception is the conditional sale on installments. Tolentino: a family home "cannot be created on property co-owned by the husband and a third person", although one built partly on conjugal and partly on the wife's paraphernal property is valid. The Supreme Court has held that a family home cannot be constituted on land owned by another (e.g., Cabang v. Basay, G.R. No. 180587, March 20, 2009, likely the case Sir could not name). Sir's own midterm key follows this line: the statement that the family home "is the dwelling house where they and their family resides, or the land on which it is situated" was marked false ("…and the land…"; "SC: If the land is not owned by the spouses who are the owners of the house, they could not have constituted a family home"). The 2011 bar MCQ (spouses who built a house on land leased from the wife's parents) is keyed: "No, since the land does not belong to A and B, it cannot qualify as a family home." For a written quiz, answer with the codal and the SC line; in recitation, add Sir's policy view (the dwelling house alone, e.g., a condo unit, should qualify).
Tolentino (Art. 152): the family home is the dwelling place of a person and his family; Muñoz described it as "a real right, gratuitous, inalienable, free from attachment", over the dwelling house and its land. Its sources are the Fuero Viejo of Castile, the American "homestead", and the codes of Mexico, Switzerland and California. Before the Civil Code, only a dwelling worth ₱300 was exempt; raising the value encourages home building. The home is "the sacred symbol of family love and the repository of cherished memories"; "when a home is seized by creditors, it seems as though the heart of the family were shattered." Limitations: (1) only one family home per family; (2) only on the dwelling place, in the locality of the domicile; (3) within the value limit. Use: actual occupancy in good faith with the intention of dedicating it as the dwelling. In a double house with two entrances, only the half the claimant occupies qualifies. Premises used primarily for business (a store or hotel) do not qualify, even if the owner lives there; but a bona fide residential portion may qualify even if a large part of the building is used otherwise. "Head of a family" is undefined; Tolentino would not limit it to one who has Art. 154 beneficiaries, since the head is himself a beneficiary.

When the family home is constituted (Arts. 153, 162)

Art. 153, FC. The family home is deemed constituted on a house and lot from the time it is occupied as a family residence. From the time of its constitution and so long as any of its beneficiaries actually resides therein, the family home continues to be such and is exempt from execution, forced sale or attachment except as hereinafter provided and to the extent of the value allowed by law. (223a)
Art. 162, FC. The provisions in this Chapter shall also govern existing family residences insofar as said provisions are applicable. (n)
Modequillo v. Breva (185 SCRA 766, 1990)

In a 1976 vehicular-accident case, judgment for damages was rendered against Jose Modequillo; it became final in 1988, and the sheriff levied on a residential house and lot in Davao del Sur. Modequillo moved to quash, claiming the property was his family home, occupied since 1969, and exempt under Arts. 152, 153 and 162. Held: not exempt. Art. 162 "does not mean that Articles 152 and 153 … have a retroactive effect such that all existing family residences are deemed to have been constituted as family homes at the time of their occupation prior to the effectivity of the Family Code and are exempt from execution for the payment of obligations incurred before the effectivity of the Family Code. Article 162 simply means that all existing family residences at the time of the effectivity of the Family Code, are considered family homes and are prospectively entitled to the benefits accorded to a family home under the Family Code." The debt and liability on which the judgment was based were incurred before the Family Code took effect (and the property had not been constituted as a family home under the Civil Code), so Art. 155(2) applied.

Ramos v. Pangilinan (G.R. No. 185920, July 20, 2010; B29 reviewer)

"Prior to the effectivity of the Family Code, for a residence to be considered a family home it must have been constituted, either extrajudicially or judicially as such; as provided by Articles 225, 229–231, and 233 of the Civil Code. After the effectiveness of the Family Code, this requirement set by the Civil Code is erased." The exemption is not absolute; the claimant must still prove that the property is a family home and that the debt falls outside Art. 155.

Tolentino (Art. 153): under the Civil Code the family home was constituted judicially or extrajudicially and took effect from the recording of the court order or public instrument. Under the Family Code, actual occupation as a family residence creates the family home without any proceeding or instrument; recording gives notice only, and a home that is recorded but not occupied is not constituted. Tolentino's criticism: the rule is "made too simple and may give rise to fraud against creditors", e.g., by families with several houses. The home continues as long as any beneficiary actually resides in it; the death of the constitutors does not necessarily end it (Art. 159), but a valid alienation does.
Quiz traps (Art. 153): "A family home must be constituted by a public instrument recorded in the Registry of Property" → false under the FC (true only under the NCC). "Art. 162 makes the family-home exemption retroactive to residences occupied before August 3, 1988, even as to earlier debts" → false (Modequillo). "The family home ceases once the spouses who constituted it die" → false: it continues while any beneficiary actually resides there (Art. 153), and in any case for the Art. 159 period.

Beneficiaries (Art. 154)

Art. 154, FC. The beneficiaries of a family home are: (1) The husband and wife, or an unmarried person who is the head of a family; and (2) Their parents, ascendants, descendants, brothers and sisters, whether the relationship be legitimate or illegitimate, who are living in the family home and who depend upon the head of the family for legal support. (226a)
Uribe (CivRev 2025 and 2018): "XY … constitute this house and lot as family home. Their children A, married to B … had to work in Metro Manila. The house is in the province. They left their 5-year-old child with XY. What if XY died via accident, this family home may remain as such until when?" Students answered "10 years, or as long as there are minor beneficiaries", and one computed 13 years (until the child turns 18). Sir: the premise is wrong. "This child is not a beneficiary because he is not relying on the grandparents for support. He is only living in the family home." In real life, the parents send money to XY for the child's care. The 2018 answer: a beneficiary "must be living in the home and … depend upon the head of the family for legal support."
Patricio v. Dario III (G.R. No. 170829, November 20, 2006; the case behind Uribe's situationer)

After Marcelino Dario died, his widow and one son sought partition of the family home; another son, Marcelino Dario III, opposed, invoking Art. 159 because his minor son (the decedent's grandson) lived in the home. Held: partition allowed. To be a beneficiary under Art. 154, a person must (1) be among the relationships enumerated, (2) live in the family home, and (3) depend on the head of the family for legal support. The grandson met the first two, but his legal support was the obligation of his own father, Marcelino III, not of his grandparents; the grandparents' obligation is only subsidiary. Since no minor beneficiary remained and more than 10 years had passed since the death, the home could be partitioned.

Tolentino (Art. 154): three requisites: (1) one of the enumerated relationships, (2) living in the family home, and (3) dependence on the head of the family for legal support. "Legitimate or illegitimate" must be read with Arts. 195–196: a brother or sister of age whose need for support is due to his or her own fault or negligence is not entitled to support, and so is not a beneficiary. The Code does not state the beneficiaries' rights; following the Mexican code, they acquire no ownership but have the right to live in and use the home.

Exemption and its exceptions (Art. 155)

Art. 155, FC. The family home shall be exempt from execution, forced sale or attachment except: (1) For nonpayment of taxes; (2) For debts incurred prior to the constitution of the family home; (3) For debts secured by mortgages on the premises before or after such constitution; and (4) For debts due to laborers, mechanics, architects, builders, materialmen and others who have rendered service or furnished material for the construction of the building. (243a)
Siari Valley Estates, Inc. v. Lucasan (109 Phil. 294, 1960)

Siari Valley Estates obtained a money judgment against Filemon Lucasan in the CFI. While the case was on appeal, Lucasan constituted his house and lot as a family home (extrajudicially, under the Civil Code), and then claimed it was exempt from execution. He argued that the judgment debt was not a "debt incurred before the constitution" because it was still disputed. Held: not exempt. "The reason why a family home constituted after a debt had been incurred is not exempt from execution is to protect the creditor against a debtor who may act in bad faith by resorting to such declaration just to defeat the claim against him. If the purpose is to protect the creditor from fraud it would be immaterial if the debt incurred be undisputed or inchoate, for a debtor acting in good faith would prefer to wait until his case is definitely decided before constituting the family home." The money judgment "comes within the purview of the word debt used in Article 243(2)" (now Art. 155(2)).

Fortaleza v. Lapitan (G.R. No. 178288, August 15, 2012; B29 reviewer)

The spouses Fortaleza mortgaged their residential house and lot to secure a ₱1.2 million loan; on default it was foreclosed and sold to the spouses Lapitan. They later resisted the writ of possession, claiming the property was their family home. Held: the claim fails. A debt secured by a mortgage on the premises, "before or after such constitution", is an exception under Art. 155(3), and by voluntarily mortgaging the property the owners waived the protection. The claim must in any case be set up and proved before the sale at public auction.

Equitable PCI Bank v. OJ-Mark Trading, Inc. (G.R. No. 165950, August 11, 2010; B29 reviewer)

A condominium unit was mortgaged to the bank, which foreclosed. The mortgagors sought an injunction, claiming the unit was their family home. Held: the claim is inconsistent with the mortgage contract, and "assuming arguendo that the mortgaged condominium unit constitutes respondents' family home, the same will not exempt it from foreclosure as Article 155(3) … allows the execution or forced sale of a family home 'for debts secured by mortgages on the premises before or after such constitution.'" (Note that the Court was willing to assume that a condominium unit could be a family home, which fits Uribe's view.)

Samplex trap (Uribe midterm, Part Two #42): "The family home shall NOT be exempt from execution, forced sale or attachment except: (a) for nonpayment of taxes; (b) for debts incurred prior to the constitution of the family home; (c) for debts due to laborers who have rendered service for the construction of the building; (d) for debts due to laborers who have rendered service for the repair and improvements of the building; (e) none of the above." One circulated key marks (e); another marks (d). By the codal, (a)–(c) are exceptions under Art. 155, while repairs and improvements are not mentioned (only "construction"), so (d) is the item that is not an exception. Tolentino would read (4) to include repairs and improvements, which would make (e) correct. Know both readings; on Sir's literal style, (d) is the safer answer, but say why.
Tolentino (Art. 155): a money judgment rendered after the constitution, on an obligation contracted before it, is a debt "incurred prior to the constitution", so the home is not exempt. Read literally, paragraph (4) is superfluous: construction always precedes the family home (which arises only on occupancy), so builders' claims are already "prior debts" under paragraph (2). Tolentino would read it to cover repairs and improvements made after the home was constituted (like the lien in NCC 2242(3)–(4) and California Code 1241).

Value limits and the execution sale (Arts. 157, 160)

Art. 157, FC. The actual value of the family home shall not exceed, at the time of its constitution, the amount of three hundred thousand pesos in urban areas, and two hundred thousand pesos in rural areas, or such amounts as may hereafter be fixed by law.
In any event, if the value of the currency changes after the adoption of this Code, the value most favorable for the constitution of a family home shall be the basis of evaluation.
For purposes of this Article, urban areas are deemed to include chartered cities and municipalities whose annual income at least equals that legally required for chartered cities. All others are deemed to be rural areas. (231a)
  • The value is the actual value at the time of constitution, not the present market value (Eulogio v. Bell, G.R. No. 186322, July 8, 2015).
  • If the currency's value changes, the value most favorable to the constitution of a family home is used.
  • "Urban" = chartered cities and municipalities whose annual income at least equals that required for chartered cities; everything else is rural.
Tolentino (Art. 157): with inflation, the former (basic) value is more favorable: a property worth ₱300,000 in basic value but ₱600,000 in depreciated pesos may still be a family home. With a stronger currency, the new value is used: a property worth ₱500,000 in basic value but ₱300,000 in the stronger currency may be a family home. The Central Bank fixes the base year.
Art. 160, FC. When a creditor whose claims is not among those mentioned in Article 155 obtains a judgment in his favor, and he has reasonable grounds to believe that the family home is actually worth more than the maximum amount fixed in Article 157, he may apply to the court which rendered the judgment for an order directing the sale of the property under execution. The court shall so order if it finds that the actual value of the family home exceeds the maximum amount allowed by law as of the time of its constitution. If the increased actual value exceeds the maximum allowed in Article 157 and results from subsequent voluntary improvements introduced by the person or persons constituting the family home, by the owner or owners of the property, or by any of the beneficiaries, the same rule and procedure shall apply.
At the execution sale, no bid below the value allowed for a family home shall be considered. The proceeds shall be applied first to the amount mentioned in Article 157, and then to the liabilities under the judgment and the costs. The excess, if any, shall be delivered to the judgment debtor. (247a, 248a)
Tolentino (Art. 160): there are two cases in which a creditor outside Art. 155 may levy: (1) the actual value at the time of constitution already exceeded the limit (then the value at levy is immaterial; later depreciation below the maximum does not exempt the home); and (2) at the time of levy the value exceeds the maximum because of later voluntary improvements (then what counts is the value at constitution plus the improvements, not the improvements alone). The court must hold a hearing and appraise the property; a mere allegation is not enough. Liens are excluded: the home must be worth more than the exemption above all liens, since only the excess is available to unsecured creditors. If the land can be divided without material injury, the court should set aside a portion equal to the exemption and execute on the rest, selling the whole only if it is indivisible. A bid must exceed the exemption plus all liens, or the sale is futile for the creditor. Tolentino's order of the proceeds: (1) lien holders; (2) the beneficiaries, the maximum amount (Art. 157), for a new family home; (3) the judgment creditor; (4) the balance to the judgment debtor-owner.

Alienation and encumbrance (Art. 158)

Art. 158, FC. The family home may be sold, alienated, donated, assigned or encumbered by the owner or owners thereof with the written consent of the person constituting the same, the latter's spouse, and a majority of the beneficiaries of legal age. In case of conflict, the court shall decide. (235a)
Tolentino (Art. 158): on alienation the property ceases to be a family home, and a new one may be constituted from the proceeds. A mortgage does not end the family home while beneficiaries live there; a foreclosure sale does. Once the home passes to third persons, there is no reason to keep the protection, whose purpose is a residence exempt from attachment for the family's debts.
Quiz trap (Art. 158): the consent needed is that of a majority of the beneficiaries of legal age, not all the beneficiaries, and not the minors. It must be written.

Duration after death; partition (Arts. 153, 159)

Art. 159, FC. The family home shall continue despite the death of one or both spouses or of the unmarried head of the family for a period of ten years or for as long as there is a minor beneficiary, and the heirs cannot partition the same unless the court finds compelling reasons therefor. This rule shall apply regardless of whoever owns the property or constituted the family home. (238a)
Tolentino (Art. 159): if no beneficiary remains at the death, the family home ceases. If beneficiaries survive, it continues for 10 years; if at the end of the 10 years there is still a minor beneficiary, it continues until that beneficiary comes of age. After these periods the heirs may partition; if they keep the property undivided, it is no longer a family home as such.
Quiz traps (Art. 159): "The family home terminates upon the death of both spouses" → false. "The heirs may partition the family home at any time after the death of the constitutors" → false: not within the Art. 159 period, unless the court finds compelling reasons. "Art. 159 applies only if the deceased owned the property" → false: "regardless of whoever owns the property or constituted the family home". The minor must be a beneficiary (Art. 154), which a grandchild supported by his own parents is not (Patricio).

Claiming the exemption: when and how

Spouses De Mesa v. Spouses Acero (G.R. No. 185064, January 16, 2012)

The spouses De Mesa bought land in Meycauayan, Bulacan, built a house on it, and lived there. Araceli de Mesa issued a check to Claudio Acero for a loan; the check bounced, and in the B.P. 22 case she was ordered to pay. The house and lot were levied upon, sold at public auction to Acero, and a final deed of sale was issued; the De Mesas did not redeem. Years later they sued to annul the sale, claiming the property was their family home and exempt. Held: the claim fails. The family home's exemption "is not automatic" and may be waived. "For all intents and purposes, the petitioners' negligence or omission to assert their right within a reasonable time gives rise to the presumption that they have abandoned, waived or declined to assert it. Since the exemption under Article 153 of the Family Code is a personal right, it is incumbent upon the petitioners to invoke and prove the same within the prescribed period and it is not the sheriff's duty to presume or raise the status of the subject property as a family home." The claim must be set up and proved at the time of the levy or within a reasonable time after, and before the sale at public auction.

Salazar v. Felias (G.R. No. 213972, February 5, 2018)

In 1990, Felias, for the heirs of Nivera, sued the spouses Lastimosa to recover four parcels of land. Romualdo Lastimosa died during the case, and his heirs were substituted. In 2004 the RTC declared the Nivera heirs owners and ordered the Lastimosa heirs to vacate. The judgment became final, and a writ of execution and demolition issued. Salazar, a daughter of the Lastimosas who had not been impleaded, tried to stop the execution, claiming the writ was against the wrong party and that the property was her family home and exempt. Held: the judgment binds all the heirs who claim under the original defendants, and the family-home claim fails for want of proof. The family home "must be backed with evidence showing that the home was indeed (i) duly constituted as a family home, (ii) constituted jointly by the husband and wife or by an unmarried head of a family, (iii) resided in by the family (or any of the family home's beneficiaries), (iv) forms part of the properties of the absolute community or the conjugal partnership, or of the exclusive properties of either spouse with the latter's consent, or property of the unmarried head of the family, and (v) has an actual value of ₱300,000.00 in urban areas, and ₱200,000.00 in rural areas." Her own pleadings gave her residence as Nueva Ecija, while the land is in Pangasinan, and the property had belonged to the Niveras since the 1950s.

Quiz traps (claiming the exemption): "The sheriff must determine on his own whether the levied property is a family home" → false (De Mesa). "The family-home exemption may be raised at any time, even after the auction sale has become final" → false: waiver and laches. "Once a house is occupied by a family, it is conclusively presumed to be a family home" → false: the claimant must prove the elements (Salazar).

Practice: In 1987 X borrowed ₱100,000 from Y. X and his family had lived in their house and lot (worth ₱150,000, in a rural municipality) since 1975. In 1992 Y obtained judgment and levied on the property. X objects that it is his family home. Rule.

The objection fails. The residence became a family home only on August 3, 1988, when the Family Code took effect (Art. 162 operates prospectively: Modequillo), and it was not constituted as a family home under the Civil Code. The 1987 debt was incurred before the constitution, so Art. 155(2) applies. (X should also have raised the claim at the levy: De Mesa.)

Practice: The spouses A and B both died in 2020, leaving their family home to their three adult children and a 9-year-old grandchild, C, who lives in the home and whose parents (one of the children and spouse) support him. In 2022 one child sues for partition. Will it prosper?

Not yet, as a rule. Under Art. 159 the family home continues for 10 years after the death (until 2030) and the heirs cannot partition it unless the court finds compelling reasons. C does not extend the period beyond 10 years, since he is not a beneficiary: he depends on his own parents, not on the head of the family, for legal support (Art. 154; Patricio v. Dario). (If the adult children who live there no longer depend on anyone for support, the home may cease to have beneficiaries at all, and partition may then be allowed.)
