Requisites of Marriage: Family Code Arts. 2–34

Covers Arts. 2–25 and 27–34 of the Family Code: the essential requisites (Arts. 2, 5), the formal requisites (Arts. 3, 6–8), the effects of non-compliance (Art. 4), the solemnizing officers (Arts. 7, 10, 31, 32 and the LGC), the marriage license (Arts. 9–20), the certificate of legal capacity (Art. 21), the marriage certificate and registry (Arts. 22–25) and license-exempt marriages (Arts. 27–34). Art. 1, the presumption of marriage and Art. 26 appear only as cross-references for the quiz-bank points. Syllabus VII.C (1 Tolentino 222–269).

Overview: kinds of requisites, kinds of non-compliance, effects

Uribe's syllabus organizes this part in three steps: (1) kinds of requisites (FC 2, 3, 5; cf. NCC 53), (2) kinds of non-compliance and (3) effect of non-compliance (FC 4). Learn the map first, because nearly every quiz item comes down to which box a fact belongs in.

Art. 53, NCC (repealed; for comparison). No marriage shall be solemnized unless all these requisites are complied with: (1) Legal capacity of the contracting parties; (2) Their consent, freely given; (3) Authority of the person performing the marriage; and (4) A marriage license, except in a marriage of exceptional character.

What changed from the NCC. The Civil Code lumped four requisites together. The Family Code separates the essential requisites (Art. 2) from the formal ones (Art. 3), makes the ceremony an express formal requisite, writes the opposite-sex requirement into the text, and graduates the effect of non-compliance (Art. 4). Under NCC 80(2), a marriage by an unauthorized officer was void even if the parties were in good faith. The FC restored the good-faith rule of Act 3613 (Art. 35(2)).

Tolentino: calling the Art. 3 requirements "formal" is only a convenient classification. They are essential to validity: without them, just as without capacity and consent, no valid marriage can exist, and their total absence makes the marriage void (Art. 4). A mere defect or irregularity in a formal requirement has no effect on the marriage if the essential requisites are present. The person responsible may be prosecuted or punished administratively.
Uribe: asked whether a marriage is valid "if all the essential and formal requisites are present," the answer is not necessarily. The essential and formal requisites are only the minimum. A marriage with all of them may still be void under Art. 36 (psychological incapacity), Arts. 37–38 (incestuous and public-policy impediments), Art. 35(4) (bigamy), Arts. 40, 41, 44 or 53. He also faults answers that are not responsive: answer the exact question asked, and do not answer "will it be valid even if a requisite is lacking" when the question is the reverse.
Uribe (a ground for nullity plus a ground for annulment): one marriage can have both a ground for nullity (e.g. no license) and a ground for annulment (e.g. no parental consent). Its status is then void ab initio. "Walang saysay ang voidable" if the marriage is void, just as a contract that is at once rescissible, unenforceable and void is treated as void.
Pugeda v. Trias (4 SCRA 849, 1962)

Listed at the head of syllabus VII.C. Doctrine (per Uribe's doctrines outline): a parish priest's failure to send a copy of the marriage certificate of an in articulo mortis marriage to the municipal secretary does not invalidate the marriage, because sending the certificate is not a requisite of validity and nothing showed that the requisites were absent. Tolentino also cites Pugeda for the rule that a marriage may be proved by competent evidence other than the certificate (see Proof of marriage below).

Cross-reference: Art. 1 and the presumption of marriage (quiz-bank points)

Art. 1, FC. Marriage is a special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman entered into in accordance with law for the establishment of conjugal and family life. It is the foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences, and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation, except that marriage settlements may fix the property relations during the marriage within the limits provided by this Code.
  • A stipulation fixing the duration of the marriage (e.g. "valid for 5 years, renewable by mutual consent") is void. Only property relations may be stipulated, and only within the limits provided by this Code (Arts. 74–81), never "without any limitation".
  • Rule 131, Sec. 3(aa): a man and woman deporting themselves as husband and wife are disputably presumed to have entered into a lawful contract of marriage. The presumption is not conclusive.
  • NCC 220: "In case of doubt, all presumptions favor the solidarity of the family. Thus, every intendment of law or facts leans toward the validity of marriage, the indissolubility of the marriage bonds, the legitimacy of children…"
Quiz trap: watch for "conclusively presumed" (false, it is disputable) and "without any limitation" (false, it is "within the limits provided by this Code").

Art. 2: the essential requisites

Art. 2, FC. No marriage shall be valid, unless these essential requisites are present:
(1) Legal capacity of the contracting parties who must be a male and a female; and
(2) Consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer. (53a)

Element 1: legal capacity, "who must be a male and a female"

The syllabus breaks legal capacity into (1) gender (FC 2(1)), (2) age (FC 5, 35(1), RA 6809; NCC 54, 80(1)) and (3) parental consent (FC 14, 45; NCC 61, 85(1)). Tolentino adds that capacity requires the minimum age and also that no impediment exists between the parties, such as a relationship or a subsisting prior marriage. Uribe, however, separates capacity from impediment (see the next h3).

Tolentino (difference in sex): an indispensable requisite "dictated by biological law," treated as a natural element even when the Civil Code did not state it, because procreation cannot be realized by two persons of the same sex. If, through deceit, a marriage is celebrated between persons of the same sex (e.g. a hermaphrodite succeeds in marrying a man), the marriage is void and inexistent, without prejudice to the deceived party's damages. Chapter 3: a marriage between persons of the same sex is one of the void marriages not enumerated in Arts. 35–38.
Silverio v. Republic (G.R. No. 174689, Oct. 22, 2007)

A male who underwent sex reassignment sought to change the name and sex entries in his birth certificate. Doctrine: no law allows the change of the entry of sex on the ground of sex reassignment. Sex is fixed at birth, and a person's "sex" for the purpose of marriage under Art. 2(1) remains the sex at birth. A post-surgery "female" therefore cannot marry a male; such a marriage lacks an essential requisite and is void (2014 Bar MCQ, bank R-5).

Note: contrast Republic v. Cagandahan (2008), where a person with congenital adrenal hyperplasia (an intersex condition) was allowed to change the sex entry because the condition was natural, not surgically induced. Same-sex marriage is not recognized; Obergefell v. Hodges (2015) is US law, listed under Concept and Nature only for comparison. Fraud under Art. 46 does not fit a same-sex case: the answer is "void, absence of an essential requisite," not "voidable."

Element 2: consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer

The syllabus lists, under consent: (1) mistake as to identity, FC 35(5); NCC 86(1); (2) unsound mind, FC 45(2); (3) fraud, FC 45(3), 46; NCC 1338–1344; (4) force, intimidation and undue influence, FC 45(4); NCC 1335–1337; (5) physical incapacity, FC 45(5); (6) affliction with STD, FC 45(6). Grounds (2) to (6) are treated in full in the voidable-marriages section. Here, the point is the classification: total absence of consent makes the marriage void; vitiated or defective consent makes it voidable.

Tolentino (mutual consent): whether a man and a woman become husband and wife is for them to decide. Even a valid agreement to marry in the future cannot be specifically enforced, and either party may refuse consent up to the actual marriage. Consent must be (a) real, not vitiated by mistake, duress or fraud; (b) conscious or intelligent, not affected by insanity, intoxication, drugs or hypnotism; and (c) given personally by the parties, without any term or condition.
  • Marriage in jest. A pretended marriage, legal in form but entered into in jest with a clear understanding that the parties are not to be bound, and not followed by conduct showing an intent to enter the relation, is no marriage. Tolentino's illustration is McClurg v. Terry (N.J.): friends on an excursion "got married" by a justice of the peace on a dare and went home as if nothing had happened. Held, no marriage, since words without the corresponding intention do not make a marriage. A secret lack of serious intent by one party, unknown to the other, does not prevent the innocent party from asserting validity, and the jesting party cannot assert validity against the other's will. (Tolentino at one point says jest marriages are "subject to annulment at the suit of the parties"; in Chapter 3 he classes them as void for total want of consent. For Uribe's purposes, total absence of consent means void, per Art. 4 par. 1.)
  • Simulated marriages, such as one between actors in a play or film, are inexistent even if the person playing the officiant is really a priest (Tolentino, Chapter 3).
  • Mistake as to identity (Art. 35(5)): mistake as to the person himself, with another person substituted for the one desired, without the knowledge of the other party (e.g. X and Y are twins and Y substitutes for X; Jacob married Leah instead of Rachel). This is void. Mistake as to rank, fortune, character or health does not invalidate the marriage: "Nothing would be more dangerous than to allow those who have agreed to take each other for better or for worse to be permitted to say that one of the parties is worse than expected." Under NCC 86(1), misrepresentation as to identity was fraud making the marriage voidable. Under the FC it is a ground for nullity.
  • In the presence of the solemnizing officer. Consent outside the officer's presence is no consent for this purpose. That is why a mayor signing forms away from the parties produces a void marriage (2009 Bar, bank R-6).
Sison v. Te Lay Li (CA, 48 O.G. No. 9)

Listed in the syllabus as "Sison v. Te Jay Li"; same case. Doctrine: as a general rule the law will not look behind a clearly manifested appearance of consent to determine whether it was real. But mere words without the corresponding intention do not create the marriage relation, and even when the formalities indicating consent have been complied with, there is no valid marriage if the parties did not intend to marry. The policy of maintaining marriage ties does not apply where it is amply proved that the marriage was effected through duress and intimidation, against the will of a party. Then there are no ties to preserve and the marriage should be annulled.

Republic v. Albios (G.R. No. 198780, Oct. 16, 2013)

Albios, a Filipina, married Fringer, an American, for a fee so that she could acquire US citizenship, intending never to live together as spouses. The lower courts declared the marriage void as a sham. Doctrine (SC reversed): the marriage is valid. Consent under Art. 2(2) requires a conscious and intelligent act, and the parties here freely and knowingly consented to be married, because a real marriage was precisely what they needed to achieve their goal. Motives are immaterial: marriages for convenience, companionship, money, status, title or citizenship are valid if they comply with all the legal requisites. A "marriage for convenience" is not a jest marriage. The Court also refused to let Albios use the courts to escape a marriage she entered into for an illicit purpose.

Quiz trap: "A marriage entered into solely to acquire foreign citizenship is void for lack of consent" is false (Albios). Do not confuse the parties' motive with the absence of consent.

Art. 5: legal capacity by age; RA 6809 and RA 11596

Art. 5, FC. Any male or female of the age of eighteen years or upwards not under any of the impediments mentioned in Articles 37 and 38, may contract marriage. (54a)
Art. 35(1), FC. The following marriages shall be void from the beginning: (1) Those contracted by any party below eighteen years of age even with the consent of parents or guardians;
Art. 54, NCC (repealed). Any male of the age of sixteen years or upwards, and any female of the age of fourteen years or upwards, not under any of the impediments mentioned in articles 80 to 84, may contract marriage.

  • Reckoning point: the minimum age is reckoned on the date of the marriage, not the date of the license application (DOJ Opinion No. 146, s. 1991). A 17-year-old may apply if he turns 18 before the ceremony. The B29 reviewer notes that the LCR should not refuse to issue a license if the party will come of age within the 120-day validity period, but should note on the license that the marriage may not be celebrated until the party comes of age.
  • If a party lied about his age to get a license and married while still below 18, the marriage is void, because he lacked the age when he contracted it. A misrepresentation cannot supply capacity.
  • No maximum age and no required difference in ages. A big age gap is not an impediment, and marriages of persons of advanced age, entered for sentimental reasons, are respected (Tolentino).
  • RA 6809 (approved Dec. 13, 1989) lowered the age of majority to 18 but expressly provides that "contracting marriage shall require parental consent until the age of twenty-one."
  • RA 11596 (Dec. 10, 2021) prohibits child marriage (a party below 18). Such a marriage is void ab initio, and the action or defense for nullity does not prescribe (in line with FC 35 and 39). Arts. 50–54 govern support, property relations and custody after its termination. Those who facilitate, solemnize or arrange it, and the adult who cohabits with the child, are penalized. After a one-year transition period it also covers Muslim and indigenous cultural communities.
Tolentino: under the old canonical and Civil Code systems the age of consent tracked puberty (14/12, later 16/14). The FC disregards puberty and adopts 18 for both sexes to prevent hasty and premature marriages, with consent (below 21), advice (below 25) and counseling as added safeguards. Below 18 a party "cannot give a binding valid consent." Between 18 and 21 consent is "insufficient," so the parents must give theirs, and for a perfect consent the parties should be 21. He also cites a sociological reason: a higher age is a legal check on population growth.
Note (outdated Tolentino view): Tolentino submitted that a girl below 18 who was the victim of rape, seduction, abduction or acts of lasciviousness could validly marry the offender, as an exception tied to RPC 344 (marriage extinguishes criminal liability). This is no longer good law. Art. 35(1) voids the marriage even with parental consent, RA 11596 penalizes child marriage, and RPC 344 presupposes a valid marriage (bank R-10). Likewise, his discussion of a widow's 300-day waiting period (NCC 84) is history: the FC dropped it, and RA 10655 (2015) repealed RPC 351 on premature marriages.
Quiz trap: "A and B were married on September 2, 1988, both 17": FC already in force (effective Aug. 3, 1988), so the answer is "void even if with parental consent" (bank R-11). Do not pick "voidable" or "valid if with parental consent." The date decides whether NCC 54 (16/14) or FC 5 (18) applies.

Legal capacity vs. legal impediment (Uribe's recitation)

Uribe (Aug. 30, 2018 recitation; repeated 2025): "One of the parties was 17 at the time of solemnization. Is this a legal impediment?" No, it goes to legal capacity, not impediment. Arts. 37 and 38 are the legal impediments (Art. 5 itself distinguishes "eighteen years or upwards" from "not under any of the impediments mentioned in Articles 37 and 38"). "Capacity is in the person (nasa tao); impediment is in relation to the other contracting party."

Why the distinction matters (Uribe): legal impediment decides other questions, most notably legitimation. Under Art. 177 as amended by RA 9858, a child conceived and born outside wedlock of parents who, at the time of conception, were not disqualified by any impediment to marry each other, or were disqualified only because either or both were below 18, may be legitimated by their subsequent valid marriage. Minority is a matter of capacity, so it does not bar legitimation. An Art. 37–38 impediment does. Uribe also stressed that the law looks to the time of conception, not birth.

Uribe (2025, first cousins): "A and B are first cousins; the marriage is void because they have no legal capacity." False as reasoned. The marriage is void, but because of a legal impediment (Art. 38(1), collateral blood relatives within the fourth civil degree), not lack of capacity. "If you have legal capacity, you can get married to anyone except your sibling, cousins and other legal impediments."

A (17) and B (19) live together and have a child, C. When A turns 18 they marry. Is C legitimated?

Answer: Yes. A's minority at conception is a lack of capacity, not an impediment, and RA 9858 expressly allows legitimation where the only disqualification was that either or both parents were below 18. Contrast: if B had been married to someone else at conception (a subsisting-marriage impediment), C could not be legitimated even if B later became free and married A.

Art. 3: the formal requisites

Art. 3, FC. The formal requisites of marriage are:
(1) Authority of the solemnizing officer;
(2) A valid marriage license except in the cases provided for in Chapter 2 of this Title; and
(3) A marriage ceremony which takes place with the appearance of the contracting parties before the solemnizing officer and their personal declaration that they take each other as husband and wife in the presence of not less than two witnesses of legal age. (53a, 55a)
Uribe: "There are only three formal requisites: authority of the solemnizing officer, marriage license, and marriage ceremony." The marriage certificate (marriage contract) is not a formal requisite. Neither is the age of the witnesses (it is part of the ceremony, and a defect there is only an irregularity), the venue, the publication, or the residence of the parties. "There is no voidable in formal requisites": a problem with a formal requisite is either total absence (void) or an irregularity (valid). It is never voidable.
Quiz trap (repeated across many Uribe samplexes): "A marriage certificate is one of the formal requisites of marriage": FALSE. Variants say "marriage contract" or "formal contract"; same answer (bank R-12, R-38).

Art. 4: absence, defect and irregularity

Art. 4, FC. The absence of any of the essential or formal requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio, except as stated in Article 35 (2).

A defect in any of the essential requisites shall render the marriage voidable as provided in Article 45.

An irregularity in the formal requisites shall not affect the validity of the marriage but the party or parties responsible for the irregularity shall be civilly, criminally and administratively liable. (n)

Three rules, three key words

  1. ABSENCE of an essential or formal requisite means VOID. The only exception named in Art. 4 is Art. 35(2): no authority, but either or both parties believed in good faith that the officer had it. A second "exception" exists in effect: the license is not required at all in Chapter 2 marriages (Arts. 27–34), so its absence there does not void the marriage (Art. 35(3), "except those covered the preceding Chapter" [sic]).
  2. DEFECT in an essential requisite means VOIDABLE "as provided in Article 45." There is no such thing as a "defect in a formal requisite" producing voidability.
  3. IRREGULARITY in a formal requisite means VALID, but the responsible party or parties are liable civilly, criminally and administratively (all three).
Tolentino (Art. 4): the absence or want of an essential requisite makes the supposed marriage non-existent or void, e.g. a party below 18, or no consent to a real marriage (a party under hypnosis or drugs, a ceremony that is part of a movie or drama, or one made in fun during an excursion). Where consent is given but not knowingly, voluntarily or freely, or the party is misled, consent is present but vitiated, so the marriage is voidable. The absence of a formal requisite has the same effect as the absence of an essential one, with one exception: Art. 35(2). Examples of mere irregularity (marriage valid): the license is issued (a) without the documentary proofs required by law, (b) before the posting period expires, or (c) when the notice was not posted where the law requires; or (d) the marriage is solemnized in a place not provided by law.
Quiz traps on Art. 4 (Uribe's favorites, from bank R-13 to R-19):
"The absence of any of the formal requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio": FALSE (Art. 35(2) exception, and license-exempt marriages).
"The absence of any of the essential or formal (or 'essential and formal') requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio": FALSE, for the same reason. The codal sentence ends "except as stated in Article 35(2)."
"The absence of any of the essential requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio": TRUE. There is no exception for essential requisites.
"A defect in any of the essential requisites shall not affect the validity of the marriage": FALSE (voidable).
"An irregularity in the formal requisites shall": not affect the validity of the marriage.
"…shall be civilly and administratively liable": incomplete. The codal says "civilly, criminally and administratively."
Uribe (2025 class, on "absence of formal requisite"): in correcting answers he insisted on three things. (1) The exception is "either or both" parties believed in good faith; an answer saying "they" (implying both) is wrong. (2) It is not the absence of authority but the belief in good faith that saves the marriage. (3) A judge solemnizing outside his territory is a different matter, not a case of the Art. 35(2) exception, because that is only an irregularity.

Authority of the solemnizing officer: Art. 7, Art. 10, the LGC

Art. 7, FC. Marriage may be solemnized by:
(1) Any incumbent member of the judiciary within the court's jurisdiction;
(2) Any priest, rabbi, imam, or minister of any church or religious sect duly authorized by his church or religious sect and registered with the civil registrar general, acting within the limits of the written authority granted by his church or religious sect and provided that at least one of the contracting parties belongs to the solemnizing officer's church or religious sect;
(3) Any ship captain or airplane chief only in the case mentioned in Article 31;
(4) Any military commander of a unit to which a chaplain is assigned, in the absence of the latter, during a military operation, likewise only in the cases mentioned in Article 32;
(5) Any consul-general, consul or vice-consul in the case provided in Article 10. (56a)
Art. 10, FC. Marriages between Filipino citizens abroad may be solemnized by a consul-general, consul or vice-consul of the Republic of the Philippines. The issuance of the marriage license and the duties of the local civil registrar and of the solemnizing officer with regard to the celebration of marriage shall be performed by said consular official. (75a)
RA 7160 (Local Government Code of 1991), Secs. 444(b)(1)(xviii) [municipal mayor] and 455(b)(1)(xviii) [city mayor]. … the mayor shall: … Solemnize marriages, any provision of law to the contrary notwithstanding;

(1) Incumbent members of the judiciary

  • "Incumbent": a retired or resigned judge has no authority.
  • "Within the court's jurisdiction": the court's territorial area. A Supreme Court or Court of Appeals Justice may solemnize anywhere in the Philippines. A trial judge only within his court's territory. Solemnizing outside it is an irregularity (Navarro v. Domagtoy; Arañes v. Occiano; see below).
  • Venue for judges (Art. 8): chambers or open court, subject to the Art. 8 exceptions. Wrong venue is an irregularity.

(2) Priests, rabbis, imams, ministers: four conditions

  1. Duly authorized by his church or religious sect;
  2. Registered with the Civil Registrar GENERAL, not the local civil registrar;
  3. Acting within the limits of the written authority granted by his church or sect; and
  4. Proviso: at least one of the contracting parties belongs to his church or sect.
Tolentino: "priest" means one especially consecrated to the service of a divinity, through whom worship, prayer and sacrifice are offered (e.g. a priest of Baal or Jehovah, a Buddhist priest). "Minister" means the clergy of every denomination and faith. An imam is a priest or minister of Islam, and a rabbi a Jewish priest. He lists the same four requisites: registration with the Civil Registrar General (not the local civil registrar), written authority from the church, acting within its limits, and both or one of the parties belonging to his church.
Vilar (Villar) v. Paraiso (96 Phil. 659, March 14, 1955)

The respondent's status as a registered minister was in issue. Doctrine: registration of ministers authorized to solemnize marriages (then NCC 92–96) is mandatory, and its purpose is two-fold: to inform the public of the minister's authority to discharge religious functions, and equally to keep it informed of any change in his religious status (e.g. resignation), for the protection of the public, especially as regards authority to solemnize marriages. A change of status must likewise be registered (then with the Bureau of Public Libraries).

Tenchavez v. Escaño (15 SCRA 355, Nov. 29, 1965)

The marriage was celebrated before a Catholic army chaplain who lacked the parish priest's ecclesiastical authorization required by Canon Law. Doctrine: the chaplain's lack of ecclesiastical authorization is irrelevant under civil law, because of the separation of Church and State and because of Act 3613 (Marriage Law). He was a duly authorized and registered solemnizing officer under civil law, so the marriage was valid. (Listed in the B29 materials under "effect of absence of authority.")

Uribe (recitation: a Presbyterian minister marries two Catholics): status? Valid; there is only an irregularity in the formal requisite (authority), and the minister is liable. For Uribe's quizzes, answer valid / mere irregularity (bank R-29).
Note (contrary view): some commentators, and a 2006 Bar suggested answer, treat the membership proviso as a limit on the minister's authority. On that view, a marriage between two non-members is solemnized without authority and is void under Art. 35(2), unless a party believed in good faith that he had authority. Know that the view exists, but follow Uribe in his exam.
Uribe (priest outside his parish): "Priest may authority hindi lang sa kanyang parish." A priest's authority is not territorial. A Palo, Leyte parish priest may validly solemnize in Quezon City (bank R-96, R-97).

(3) Ship captains and airplane chiefs: Art. 31 only

  • Only a marriage in articulo mortis, only between passengers or crew members, while the ship is at sea or the plane in flight, and also during stopovers at ports of call (Art. 31).
  • Art. 7(3) says "airplane chief"; Art. 31 says "airplane pilot." Tolentino: the controlling designation is "airplane chief," the head of the crew with command of the airplane, who may not be the "pilot."
Uribe (recitation): a marriage solemnized on board by the First Officer, not the chief or captain: does he have authority? No, under Art. 7, which requires the "chief." He acknowledged the inconsistency between Art. 7 ("chief") and Art. 31 ("pilot"). If the airplane chief marries the parties at the Manila Hotel: not valid as a matter of authority, because the authority exists "only in flight," which technically means once the engine has started (plus stopovers at ports of call). Such a marriage is void unless Art. 35(2) good faith applies (bank R-32, R-33).
Note (1995 Bar hijacking problem): the suggested answer treated an assistant pilot acting for a chief pilot held hostage as authorized because of the extraordinary circumstances (bank R-98). That is debatable and limited to its facts. Even on the strict view, good faith under Art. 35(2) would save the marriage.

(4) Military commanders: Art. 32 only

  • He must be a commander of a unit, a commissioned officer (Art. 32), of a unit to which a chaplain is assigned, acting in the absence of the chaplain, during a military operation (Art. 7(4)).
  • Only a marriage in articulo mortis, between persons within the zone of military operation, "whether members of the armed forces or civilians."
  • Tolentino: the NCC limited this authority to "time of war." The FC expanded it to "military operations." Unlike the ship captain or airplane chief (passengers and crew only), the military commander may marry all persons within the zone.

(5) Consuls-general, consuls, vice-consuls: Art. 10

  • Only marriages between Filipino citizens (both parties) abroad. The consul also issues the license and performs the LCR's duties, so a license is still required (bank R-40).
  • The parties need not be permanent residents or immigrants of the foreign country; they may be there only temporarily (Tolentino). Philippine law governs, so the marriage must comply with the FC requisites.
  • Ambassador: no authority, even though he heads the mission. Ambassadors handle relations between states, while consuls handle matters affecting Filipino citizens (Tolentino; bank R-31).
  • A consul has no authority in the Philippines, even while on vacation here (1994 Bar, bank R-34), and no authority to marry a Filipino to a foreigner abroad (2012 Bar, bank R-35). Both cases are void unless the good-faith exception applies.

Muslim marriages (PD 1083)

Under the Code of Muslim Personal Laws (PD 1083, Feb. 4, 1977), a Muslim marriage may be solemnized by the proper wali of the woman, by any person competent under Muslim law upon the wali's authority, or (if the wali refuses) by a Shari'a judge or a person he designates. Authority vs. jurisdiction vs. venue (Navarro, Arañes, Beso)

Uribe (2025, verbatim gist): "Do not confuse the authority of the solemnizing officer with jurisdiction and venue. Authority is in the person (nasa tao): he carries it wherever he goes. But a judge is required to solemnize within his court's jurisdiction, so if he solemnizes outside it (Arañes v. Occiano), it is only an irregularity, because he has the authority as a person and merely failed to follow the law. Jurisdiction is also different from venue: even within the court's jurisdiction, a judge must solemnize in chambers or open court. A wedding at the Manila Hotel is not necessarily irregular, because Art. 8 allows the parties to request another place in writing, or it may be in articulo mortis."
Navarro v. Domagtoy (A.M. No. MTJ-96-1088, July 19, 1996)

An MCTC judge (1) solemnized a marriage of a man who was merely separated in fact from his wife, relying on an affidavit of absence without the judicial declaration of presumptive death required by Art. 41 (so the marriage was bigamous and void), and (2) solemnized another marriage at his residence in a municipality outside his court's territorial jurisdiction, on the written request of only one party. Doctrine: a judge's authority to solemnize is limited to his court's jurisdiction. Solemnizing outside it is an irregularity in the formal requisite (Art. 3) that does not affect validity but subjects the judge to administrative liability. Art. 8's exception requires a written request by both parties. The judge was suspended for gross ignorance of the law.

Arañes v. Occiano (A.M. No. MTJ-02-1390, April 11, 2002; 380 SCRA 402)

An MTC judge of Balatan, Camarines Sur solemnized the complainant's marriage in Nabua, outside his territorial jurisdiction, and without a marriage license. After the husband died, the complainant was denied his pension because the marriage was void. Doctrine (two rulings, keep them apart): (a) where a judge solemnizes a marriage outside his court's jurisdiction, there is an irregularity in the formal requisite of Art. 3 which, while it may not affect validity, may subject the judge to administrative liability; (b) solemnizing a marriage without a marriage license renders the marriage void and is gross ignorance of the law. The complainant's later desistance did not bar the administrative case, and the judge was fined.

Note: one line in the B29 case repository reads "a marriage solemnized by a judge outside his jurisdiction renders the marriage void ab initio." That is wrong. In Arañes the marriage was void because there was no license. Solemnizing outside jurisdiction is a mere irregularity. Answer accordingly.
Beso v. Daguman (A.M. No. MTJ-99-1211, Jan. 28, 2000)

A Samar MCTC judge solemnized a marriage in Calbayog City, outside his court's territory, at his residence, and then failed to register the certificate. Doctrine: same as Navarro. Solemnizing outside the court's jurisdiction is an irregularity in the formal requisite under Art. 3. It does not affect validity, but the judge is administratively liable, here also for failing to see to the registration of the certificate.

Absence of authority and the good-faith exception (Arts. 4, 35(2))

Art. 35(2), FC. The following marriages shall be void from the beginning: … (2) Those solemnized by any person not legally authorized to perform marriages unless such marriages were contracted with either or both parties believing in good faith that the solemnizing officer had the legal authority to do so;
  • Rule: no authority means void.
  • Exception: either or both parties believed in good faith that the officer had legal authority. The good faith of one party suffices.
  • Exception to the exception: if both knew he lacked authority, the marriage is void (bank R-19).
  • History: Act 3613 (Marriage Law) saved such marriages if either party believed in good faith. NCC 80(2) removed the exception and made them void ("the authority of the person performing the marriage is essential… [otherwise] the marriage might as well be performed before a twelve-year-old child or a policeman," per Dean Bocobo). The FC restored the old rule.
Tolentino: he favored restoration. If parties marry before a notary public or a twelve-year-old believing he has authority, the mistake is one of law and the marriage must be void. But where the mistake refers to a fact, namely the existence of the government's authorization of a priest, the situation is like the acts of an officer de facto, and public policy favors validity (protecting the children and the good-faith spouse). He also suggests that a marriage void under the NCC for want of authority may be validated by the FC if either party was in good faith, through Art. 255 (retroactivity, if no vested rights are impaired), as a curative interpretation favoring validity.
Uribe (2025): he raised the objection that whether an officer has authority is a question of law, and ignorance of the law excuses no one (NCC 3), so how can a party claim good faith? His resolution was that Art. 35(2) itself contemplates parties who believe in good faith in an authority that, under the law, the officer does not have. To require that the officer actually have authority would be absurd, because then there would be nothing to save. So the exception applies even though the officer has no legal authority. (Example: the Makati mayor who married Alex and Ben on Dec. 5, 1988, during the gap. The marriage is void unless either or both believed in good faith in his authority.)
Quiz trap: "A marriage is void if solemnized by one without authority to solemnize marriage": FALSE (not absolutely true, because of Art. 35(2)) (bank R-27). Also watch "both parties believing" (wrong) against "either or both" (correct).

The ceremony: Arts. 3(3) and 6

Art. 6, FC. No prescribed form or religious rite for the solemnization of the marriage is required. It shall be necessary, however, for the contracting parties to appear personally before the solemnizing officer and declare in the presence of not less than two witnesses of legal age that they take each other as husband and wife. This declaration shall be contained in the marriage certificate which shall be signed by the contracting parties and their witnesses and attested by the solemnizing officer.

In case of a marriage in articulo mortis, when the party at the point of death is unable to sign the marriage certificate, it shall be sufficient for one of the witnesses to the marriage to write the name of said party, which fact shall be attested by the solemnizing officer. (55a)

Elements of a valid ceremony

  1. Personal appearance of both contracting parties before the solemnizing officer;
  2. Their personal declaration that they take each other as husband and wife;
  3. In the presence of not less than two witnesses of legal age.

There is no prescribed form: civil, military or religious, simple or pompous (Tolentino). It is not necessary to recite the words of the statute; it is enough that the words used show mutual consent, and consent may even be inferred from the ceremony performed, the acts of the parties, and habit or repute.

Martinez v. Tan (12 Phil. 731, Feb. 5, 1909)

The parties signed a petition to a justice of the peace stating that they had agreed to marry and asking him to marry them. Before him and in the presence of witnesses they ratified the petition under oath and insisted on what they asked for, and the justice, the parties and the witnesses signed a document stating that the parties had been married by him. Doctrine: no particular form of ceremony is required, but the parties must declare, in the presence of the solemnizing officer, that they take each other as husband and wife. What took place amounted to a marriage.

Marriage by proxy

Tolentino: some jurisdictions recognize proxy marriages (royal proxy marriages were common in medieval Europe). Under Art. 6, however, marriage by proxy is not legally possible in the Philippines, because the parties themselves must declare, in the presence of the officer and two witnesses, that they take each other as husband and wife. Consent must be personally and solemnly manifested. In Chapter 3 he lists proxy marriages, together with common-law marriages, as void "for failure to comply with this requisite."
Uribe (recitation, proxy abroad): A and B were to be married in New York. A, stuck in California, had his brother represent him at the ceremony. Status? If valid where celebrated, the marriage is valid here under lex loci celebrationis (Art. 26 par. 1) even if A and B are Filipinos, unless it falls under the exceptions (Arts. 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37, 38). Is this a mistake as to identity? No, because A asked his brother to represent him (bank R-72, R-77).
Note: some authors (e.g. Sempio-Diy) argue that personal appearance goes to consent, an essential requisite governed by the parties' national law (NCC 15), so a proxy marriage of Filipinos abroad would be void. For Uribe: proxy in the Philippines means void (no ceremony with personal appearance); proxy abroad, valid there, means valid here.

Witnesses and the signing of the certificate

  • The witnesses must be "of legal age" (18). Minor witnesses are an irregularity. Uribe (Alex and Ben problem, two 16-year-old witnesses): "There is no voidable in formal requisites. The witnesses should be 18 is not a formal requisite; the marriage ceremony is." So the marriage is not voidable or void on that account.
  • Failure to sign or issue the certificate. Tolentino: signing a formal contract or certificate is not essential to validity, so long as consent was given before the officer and at least two witnesses of legal age. The certificate has only evidentiary value, and even the officer's failure to execute and issue it is a defect or irregularity in a formal requirement that does not invalidate a marriage completed by the exchange of consent. The consent can be proved by the testimony of the officer, the parties and the witnesses.
  • Articulo mortis: if the dying party cannot sign, one of the witnesses (not the solemnizing officer, not the other spouse) writes his name, and the solemnizing officer attests to that fact.
Tolentino (common-law marriages): the ceremony requirement prevents recognition in the Philippines of "common-law marriages," i.e. a present agreement between capacitated persons to take each other as husband and wife, followed by cohabitation. Even parties who have obtained a license but never go before an authorized officer are not married.
Morigo v. People (2004)

Doctrine (B29 repository): the mere private act of signing a marriage contract, without the presence of a solemnizing officer, bears no semblance to a valid marriage. There was no ceremony at all, so the first "marriage" was void ab initio, needed no judicial declaration for purposes of the bigamy charge, and the accused was acquitted. (Bigamy is treated in the void-marriages section.)

(2009 Bar) After getting a license, M and A go to the mayor's office. The mayor is away, so his secretary has the couple and witnesses sign the marriage contract, then takes the forms to the mayor at a reception in another town, where he signs them. The couple is told they are married and lives together for years. Status?

Answer: Void. There was no ceremony with personal appearance before the officer and no declaration in his presence (Arts. 2(2), 3(3), 6), so an essential and a formal requisite are absent (Art. 4 par. 1). Signing the contract is not a substitute; the certificate is only evidence.

Art. 8: place of solemnization

Art. 8, FC. The marriage shall be solemnized publicly in the chambers of the judge or in open court, in the church, chapel or temple, or in the office the consul-general, consul or vice-consul, as the case may be, and not elsewhere, except in cases of marriages contracted on the point of death or in remote places in accordance with Article 29 of this Code, or where both of the parties request the solemnizing officer in writing in which case the marriage may be solemnized at a house or place designated by them in a sworn statement to that effect. (57a)

Rule, exceptions, effect

  • Rule: publicly, in (a) the judge's chambers or open court, (b) the church, chapel or temple, or (c) the consular office, "as the case may be," and not elsewhere.
  • Exceptions: (1) in articulo mortis; (2) remote places under Art. 29; (3) both parties request the officer in writing, in which case the marriage may be held at a house or place they designate in a sworn statement. The officer keeps this affidavit in his file (Art. 23).
  • Effect of violation: mere irregularity. Venue is neither an essential nor a formal requisite (Arts. 2–3). A Supreme Court Justice marrying a couple inside a Catholic church is valid (2020–21 Bar, bank R-36).
  • Tolentino: a wedding on a Sunday does not violate the requirement of public solemnization in the judge's office or open court (Gabriel v. Gabriel, CA).
Quiz trap: "…if either of the contracting parties requests the solemnizing officer in writing": FALSE; it must be both (bank R-37; also Navarro v. Domagtoy, where only one party requested). Also note that the Art. 8 exception for remote places is "in accordance with Article 29."

The marriage license: nature, where to apply (Arts. 9–10)

Tolentino: the license is essential to validity even though it is only a "formal" requirement. A marriage that preceded the issuance of the license is void, and a later issuance cannot validate it or add an iota of validity. But the law does not require the solemnizing officer to investigate whether the license was issued by the LCR of the domicile of either party. It is enough that it was issued by a competent official, who is presumed to have done his duty. And a marriage under a license is not invalidated by the fact that the license was wrongfully obtained (citing Melcher v. Melcher, as cited by the CA in Eigenmann v. Guerra, 61 O.G. 4722).
Art. 9, FC. A marriage license shall be issued by the local civil registrar of the city or municipality where either contracting party habitually resides, except in marriages where no license is required in accordance with Chapter 2 of this Title. (58a)
  • "Either" contracting party, not "both." "Habitually resides."
  • A license from the LCR of a place where neither resides: irregularity only; the marriage is valid (Tolentino; Alcantara v. Alcantara). The solemnizing officer does not have to investigate whether the license was properly issued.
  • Marriages of Filipinos abroad: the consul issues the license (Art. 10).

(1) Full name of the contracting party;
(2) Place of birth;
(3) Age and date of birth;

  • Each party files separately a sworn application.
  • Residence certificates (cedulas) cannot be required, though a party may voluntarily present one in the absence of a birth or baptismal certificate (Tolentino) (bank R-42).
Art. 12, FC. The local civil registrar, upon receiving such application, shall require the presentation of the original birth certificates or, in default thereof, the baptismal certificates of the contracting parties or copies of such documents duly attested by the persons having custody of the originals. These certificates or certified copies of the documents required by this Article need not be sworn to and shall be exempt from the documentary stamp tax. The signature and official title of the person issuing the certificate shall be sufficient proof of its authenticity.

If either of the contracting parties is unable to produce his birth or baptismal certificate or a certified copy of either because of the destruction or loss of the original or if it is shown by an affidavit of such party or of any other person that such birth or baptismal certificate has not yet been received though the same has been required of the person having custody thereof at least fifteen days prior to the date of the application, such party may furnish in lieu thereof his current residence certificate or an instrument drawn up and sworn to before the local civil registrar concerned or any public official authorized to administer oaths. Such instrument shall contain the sworn declaration of two witnesses of lawful age, setting forth the full name, residence and citizenship of such contracting party and of his or her parents, if known, and the place and date of birth of such party. The nearest of kin of the contracting parties shall be preferred as witnesses, or, in their default, persons of good reputation in the province or the locality.

The presentation of birth or baptismal certificate shall not be required if the parents of the contracting parties appear personally before the local civil registrar concerned and swear to the correctness of the lawful age of said parties, as stated in the application, or when the local civil registrar shall, by merely looking at the applicants upon their personally appearing before him, be convinced that either or both of them have the required age. (60a)

Order of proof of age (Tolentino)

  1. Original or certified copies of the birth certificate;
  2. Failing that, original or certified copies of the baptismal certificate;
  3. Failing both (destroyed or lost, or requested at least 15 days before the application and not yet received): the current residence certificate or a sworn instrument with the declaration of two witnesses of lawful age, nearest of kin preferred.

Proof dispensed with: (a) the parents appear personally before the LCR and swear to the parties' lawful age; (b) the LCR is convinced, by merely looking at the applicants, that they have the required age; (c) a party previously married (widowed, divorced, or prior marriage invalidated) presents the documents under Art. 13 instead.

Quiz trap: "…required of the person having custody thereof at least thirty days prior": FALSE, it is fifteen days (bank R-43).
Art. 13, FC. In case either of the contracting parties has been previously married, the applicant shall be required to furnish, instead of the birth or baptismal certificate required in the last preceding article, the death certificate of the deceased spouse or the judicial decree of the absolute divorce, or the judicial decree of annulment or declaration of nullity of his or her previous marriage.

In case the death certificate cannot be secured, the party shall make an affidavit setting forth this circumstance and his or her actual civil status and the name and date of death of the deceased spouse. (61a)
  • A widower who cannot get the death certificate executes an affidavit (circumstance, actual civil status, name and date of death of the deceased spouse). He does not need a declaration of presumptive death, which is for an absent spouse whose death is uncertain (Art. 41) (bank R-44).
  • A previously married person who relies on a void prior marriage must present the judicial decree of nullity (cf. Art. 40).

Parental consent, parental advice, marriage counseling (Arts. 14–16; PD 965)

Art. 14, FC. In case either or both of the contracting parties, not having been emancipated by a previous marriage, are between the ages of eighteen and twenty-one, they shall, in addition to the requirements of the preceding articles, exhibit to the local civil registrar, the consent to their marriage of their father, mother, surviving parent or guardian, or persons having legal charge of them, in the order mentioned. Such consent shall be manifested in writing by the interested party, who personally appears before the proper local civil registrar, or in the form of an affidavit made in the presence of two witnesses and attested before any official authorized by law to administer oaths. The personal manifestation shall be recorded in both applications for marriage license, and the affidavit, if one is executed instead, shall be attached to said applications. (61a)

Art. 14 elements

  • Who: parties between 18 and 21 who are not emancipated by a previous marriage.
  • Whose consent: father, mother, surviving parent or guardian, or persons having legal charge of them, in the order mentioned. Art. 45(1) likewise says "in that order."
  • Form: in writing, either (a) by the parent personally appearing before the proper LCR (recorded in both applications), or (b) by an affidavit made in the presence of two witnesses and attested before an official authorized to administer oaths (attached to the applications).
  • Effect if lacking: the marriage is VOIDABLE (Arts. 4 par. 2, 45(1)), ratified by free cohabitation after the party reaches 21. Under Art. 47(1) the action may be brought by the party within five years after reaching 21, or by the parent or guardian before the party reaches 21 (details in the voidable-marriages section). There is no suspension of the license for lack of consent.
Tolentino (Art. 14): (1) Reason: consent supplements the natural incapacity of young persons, whose inexperience may lead them into a difficult union, and assures that the bond is not formed without serious prior examination by those responsible for the family. (2) Previous marriage: a person below 21 whose marriage ended with the spouse's death needs no parental consent to remarry; consent was given for the first marriage and need not be repeated. (3) RA 6809 did not affect this requirement for persons with living parents. But an orphan over 18, emancipated and not under guardianship, has nobody "having legal charge" of him, so consent may be dispensed with because compliance is impossible. (4) Widowed mother who remarries retains parental authority, so her consent is still required. (5) Specific consent: the better view is that consent must refer to marriage with a particular person; "You are free to marry anyone" is a relinquishment of parental authority, not an exercise of it. (6) Revocation: Tolentino submits that consent, once formally given and the license issued, should be irrevocable except for just cause (e.g. discovery that the intended spouse is morally depraved or has a dangerous contagious disease), and the revocation must be made known to the parties and the LCR. (7) Effect of want of consent: under Act 3613 the marriage was valid (Aguilar v. Lazaro; Ching Juat v. Ysip). Under the NCC and FC it is voidable. There is also a penal sanction for parties or an officer who knowingly proceed without it, but a minor who honestly but erroneously believed herself of age, or an officer who in good faith made the required investigation, is not criminally liable.

Uribe's challenge: the FC says nothing about a specific person, and since a 19-year-old who was previously married needs no consent at all, the law looks to the fact that the child is getting married and is not yet emancipated by marriage, not to the identity of the intended spouse. Even with a known impediment the LCR notes it and still issues the license unless a court orders otherwise (Art. 18). Lack of consent only makes the marriage voidable (bank R-52).

Art. 15, FC. Any contracting party between the age of twenty-one and twenty-five shall be obliged to ask their parents or guardian for advice upon the intended marriage. If they do not obtain such advice, or if it be unfavorable, the marriage license shall not be issued till after three months following the completion of the publication of the application therefor. A sworn statement by the contracting parties to the effect that such advice has been sought, together with the written advice given, if any, shall be attached to the application for marriage license. Should the parents or guardian refuse to give any advice, this fact shall be stated in the sworn statement. (62a)

Art. 15 elements

  • Who: parties between 21 and 25. It is advice, not consent.
  • If not obtained or unfavorable: the license "shall not be issued till after three months following the completion of the publication," counted from the end of the 10-day posting, not from the filing of the application.
  • Paperwork: a sworn statement that advice was sought, plus the written advice if any. A parental refusal to give advice is stated in the sworn statement.
  • Effect: validity is never affected. A license issued after the 3 months, even without advice, is regular. One issued within the 3 months is an irregularity (liability only).
Tolentino (Art. 15): advice recognizes the child's duty to honor his parents even after emancipation ("the moral authority of the parents never ends"). The want of favorable advice merely delays the marriage. If the license is issued and the marriage celebrated before the three months lapse, validity is not affected, without prejudice to penal consequences. Falsely swearing that advice was sought exposes the party to criminal liability but does not affect validity. Advice depends on filial relationship, not parental authority, so it is needed even if the child is emancipated. For an orphan over 18, advice is dispensed with because compliance is impossible.
Quiz traps (Arts. 14–15):
"Any contracting party between 21 and 25 shall be obliged to ask their parents or guardian for consent": FALSE, it is advice (bank R-45). The "advice" version is TRUE (R-46).

Art. 16, FC. In the cases where parental consent or parental advice is needed, the party or parties concerned shall, in addition to the requirements of the preceding articles, attach a certificate issued by a priest, imam or minister authorized to solemnize marriage under Article 7 of this Code or a marriage counselor duly accredited by the proper government agency to the effect that the contracting parties have undergone marriage counseling. Failure to attach said certificates of marriage counseling shall suspend the issuance of the marriage license for a period of three months from the completion of the publication of the application.   • Triggered "where parental consent or parental advice is needed." That generally means parties 18–25 (Tolentino), but it depends on actual need: an 18–21 party emancipated by a previous marriage needs no consent.
  • Counselors: a priest, imam or minister authorized under Art. 7, or a government-accredited marriage counselor.
  • No certificate: issuance is suspended for three months from the completion of publication. Issuance within that period means administrative sanctions for the issuing officer, but validity is unaffected.
  • If only one party needs consent or advice, the other party must be present at the counseling (bank R-57: "only that party" is FALSE).

He laments that the law does not require a physical examination or certificate of freedom from sexually transmissible disease: a good requirement, but not imposable administratively without a law.

Family planning certificate: PD 965 (syllabus item h)

PD 965 requires applicants for a marriage license to receive instructions and information on family planning and responsible parenthood. No license shall be issued unless the applicants present a certificate, issued at no cost by the family planning office, that they received such instructions. If the instructions cannot be given in time, the office informs the LCR, which delays issuance for two weeks, after which the license may issue (B29 summary). Non-compliance affects only the regularity of issuance, never validity.

Publication, impediments, fees (Arts. 17–19, 24–25)

Art. 17, FC. The local civil registrar shall prepare a notice which shall contain the full names and residences of the applicants for a marriage license and other data given in the applications. The notice shall be posted for ten consecutive days on a bulletin board outside the office of the local civil registrar located in a conspicuous place within the building and accessible to the general public. This notice shall request all persons having knowledge of any impediment to the marriage to advise the local civil registrar thereof. The marriage license shall be issued after the completion of the period of publication. (63a)
  • Ten consecutive days, on a bulletin board outside the LCR office, in a conspicuous place within the building, accessible to the general public.
  • Issuance before the 10 days end, or with the notice not posted where required, or with no posting at all, is an irregularity (Tolentino, citing Delima v. Cline, CA; Alcantara v. Alcantara) (bank R-58).
Art. 18, FC. In case of any impediment known to the local civil registrar or brought to his attention, he shall note down the particulars thereof and his findings thereon in the application for marriage license, but shall nonetheless issue said license after the completion of the period of publication, unless ordered otherwise by a competent court at his own instance or that of any interested party. No filing fee shall be charged for the petition nor a corresponding bond required for the issuance of the order. (64a)
  • The LCR's duty is ministerial: note the impediment in the application (not on the license), then issue anyway after publication, unless a competent court orders otherwise, "at his own instance or that of any interested party."
  • No filing fee and no bond for the petition.
  • Change from the NCC: under NCC 64, an LCR convinced that an impediment existed had to withhold the license unless a court ordered otherwise. Under the FC he must issue it unless restrained by a court (Tolentino).
Tolentino (marriage despite impediment): because the finding is noted on the application and the application need not be attached to the license, the solemnizing officer may not learn of the impediment and may solemnize the marriage. He incurs no criminal liability, but the marriage is void because of the impediment itself, not because of any license defect.
Uribe: "Issuance of license is a ministerial duty. Even if the LCR knows that one party is already married, he has no right to refuse." The remedy is a court order.

Art. 20: where and how long the license is valid

Art. 20, FC. The license shall be valid in any part of the Philippines for a period of one hundred twenty days from the date of issue, and shall be deemed automatically canceled at the expiration of the said period if the contracting parties have not made use of it. The expiry date shall be stamped in bold characters on the face of every license issued. (65a)
  • Place: any part of the Philippines, so using it in another city is not an irregularity (Uribe).
  • Period: 120 days from the date of issue (not from the application or the posting). Uribe's key treats "four months" as TRUE (a month is 30 days under NCC 13 and Admin. Code Sec. 31) (bank R-62).
  • Effect of lapse: automatically cancelled. A marriage after the 120 days is a marriage without a license: absence of a formal requisite, so void (Art. 35(3)), not a mere irregularity (Tolentino, citing People v. Edeza, CA, 59 O.G. 562) (bank R-63).
Uribe (counting): when the issue date is not given, do not assume it. If the LCR complied with the law (10-day posting, and a 3-month wait where advice or counseling is lacking), compute the earliest lawful issue date before concluding that the license expired. In the Alex and Ben problem (application July 30, 1988; marriage Dec. 5, 1988) the license would not have expired. In the X (23) and Y (widow, 20) problem (application Jan. 2, 2019; marriage May 31, 2019), Uribe chose "may be valid": a license properly issued around mid-April, after the 3-month suspension, is still good on May 31 (bank R-56).

Art. 21: certificate of legal capacity (foreigners; stateless persons and refugees)

Art. 21, FC. When either or both of the contracting parties are citizens of a foreign country, it shall be necessary for them before a marriage license can be obtained, to submit a certificate of legal capacity to contract marriage, issued by their respective diplomatic or consular officials.

Stateless persons or refugees from other countries shall, in lieu of the certificate of legal capacity herein required, submit an affidavit stating the circumstances showing such capacity to contract marriage. (66a)
  • Nationality principle: a foreigner's capacity to marry is governed by his national law (NCC 15). Tolentino: if the foreigner's country sets the age of consent at 16 and his diplomatic or consular representative issues a certificate of capacity, a license can be issued to him even at 16 or 17.
  • Stateless persons and refugees submit their own affidavit stating the circumstances showing capacity, in lieu of the certificate. Having no personal law, their capacity is determined by Philippine law (Tolentino).
  • Effect of absence: the certificate is a prerequisite for the license. It is neither an essential nor a formal requisite, so its absence is at most an irregularity in the issuance of the license, and the marriage is not void, without prejudice to the prosecution of those responsible (DOJ Opinion No. 050, s. 1991). The solemnizing officer need not investigate.
  • A duly authenticated certificate is only prima facie evidence of capacity (Garcia v. Recio, 2001). Actual capacity is still governed by the alien's national law.
Note (RA 11596): for a foreigner who is below 18 marrying in the Philippines, the Tolentino illustration above must now be read with RA 11596, which prohibits marriages where a party is a "child," i.e. below 18. Treat Tolentino's 16-year-old-foreigner example as historical and answer cautiously if it appears.

Proof of marriage

Tolentino: the best proof of marriage is the marriage certificate, but it is not the only proof. Competent evidence also includes: the declaration of one of the parties; the testimony of persons present at the celebration; public and open cohabitation as husband and wife after the alleged marriage; birth and baptismal certificates of the children; and statements of the marriage in later documents (e.g. a probated will naming a woman as the testator's wife, admissible as an admission against interest). A marriage is presumed valid and regular and will not be set aside because the priest cannot remember what was said, especially after long cohabitation. The presumption arising from cohabitation is prima facie and rebuttable, e.g. by proof that one party was married to another, but only by cogent proof. Abandonment of the common home after long cohabitation does not overthrow it.
Macua Vda. de Avenido v. Avenido (G.R. No. 173540, Jan. 22, 2014)

The first wife's marriage records had been destroyed during the war. Doctrine: while the marriage certificate is the primary evidence of a marital union, it is not the sole and exclusive evidence. The fact of marriage may be proved by other relevant evidence, such as testimony of witnesses to the ceremony, and even a person's birth certificate may be competent evidence of the marriage of his parents. The earlier marriage was upheld.

Proving the absence of a license; void-for-no-license cases

The absence of a license is a question of evidence, and the cases turn on the registrar's certification (Rule 132, Sec. 28: a written statement that after diligent search no record or entry is found is admissible to prove the non-existence of the record).

Republic v. Court of Appeals and Castro (G.R. No. 103047, Sept. 2, 1994)

A civil marriage was contracted secretly without the parties' families' knowledge. To prove that no license was issued, the wife presented the LCR's certification of "due search and inability to find" the license. Doctrine: a certification of due search and inability to find, issued by the civil registrar, unaccompanied by any circumstance of suspicion, sufficiently proves that no marriage license was issued. The marriage was void for lack of a license.

Sevilla v. Cardenas (G.R. No. 167684, July 31, 2006)

The husband sought nullity on the ground that no license was issued, relying on the LCR's certifications that the license number on the marriage contract could not be located. The registrar's own testimony showed that the entries were not well kept and the logbook could not be found. Doctrine: the presumption of regularity in the performance of official duty is disputable and was overcome here. A certification of inability to find must categorically state that the document does not exist, or that it could not be found despite diligent search. Since the certifications did not establish that, the presumption of validity of marriage prevailed and the marriage stood.

Alcantara v. Alcantara (G.R. No. 167746, Aug. 28, 2007)

The marriage certificate showed a license issued in Carmona, Cavite, where neither party resided, and the husband claimed the marriage was void for lack of a valid license. Doctrine: issuance of a license in a city or municipality that is not the residence of either party, and issuance despite the absence of publication or before the 10-day period is completed, are mere irregularities that do not affect validity; the responsible parties are civilly, criminally and administratively liable (Art. 4 par. 3). The husband failed to overcome the presumption of validity, so the marriage stood.

Abbas v. Abbas (G.R. No. 183896, Jan. 30, 2013)

The license number on the parties' marriage contract was shown, by the Carmona LCR's certification, to have been issued to a different couple, and neither party resided in Carmona. Doctrine: without the actual license, reliance on the marriage certificate does not prove that a license existed. The absence of a formal requisite makes the marriage void ab initio (Arts. 4, 35(3)), since the marriage did not fall under the license-exempt marriages of Arts. 27–34.

Kho v. Republic (G.R. No. 187462, June 1, 2016)

The CA presumed that a license was issued because a marriage had been solemnized. Doctrine (SC reversed): the marriage was void ab initio for lack of a valid license, proved by the LCR's certification of no record of a license plus testimony. The presumption that a license was issued yields to such proof.

Cariaga v. Republic (B29 repository)

The license number on the certificate was actually issued to a different couple, and the lower courts discounted the LCR certification because it lacked the words "despite diligent search." Doctrine: the absence of those words does not by itself diminish the certification's probative value; courts take a holistic approach, appreciating the certification in light of its purpose, the cause of action and the other evidence. The presumption of validity was overcome and the marriage declared void.

Cosca v. Palaypayon (A.M. No. MTJ-92-721, Sept. 30, 1994)

An MTC judge was charged with, among others, solemnizing marriages without the required marriage license; in one instance he left the marriage contract unsigned and undated and argued that no marriage had been solemnized. He was found administratively liable. Doctrine: a judge who solemnizes a marriage without a license disregards the law, and the marriage is void (Art. 35(3)). Not signing the contract does not undo a solemnization that actually took place, because it is the ceremony, not the signing, that constitutes the marriage. Cross-reference: Art. 26 (law governing validity) — quiz-bank points

Art. 26, FC. All marriages solemnized outside the Philippines, in accordance with the laws in force in the country where they were solemnized, and valid there as such, shall also be valid in this country, except those prohibited under Articles 35 (1), (4), (5) and (6), 36, 37 and 38.

Where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law. (As amended by E.O. 227)
  • Par. 1 (lex loci celebrationis): "All marriages… valid there… shall also be valid in this country" without the exceptions is FALSE. The exceptions are: below 18; bigamy or polygamy not under Art. 41; mistake as to identity; Art. 53 marriages; psychological incapacity (36); incest (37); public policy (38).
  • Not excepted: lack of authority of the officer (Art. 35(2)) and lack of a license (Art. 35(3)), which are matters of form; proxy marriages (form); voidable marriages (e.g. no parental consent); step-siblings (not a prohibited degree under Art. 38).
  • Filipinos abroad aged 16–17 (2025 Bar): void despite Hong Kong law, because capacity follows Philippine law (NCC 15; Art. 35(1)). Annulment abroad on a non-FC ground such as sterility (2003 Bar): not effective here for Filipinos.
  • Par. 2: citizenship is reckoned at the time the divorce is obtained (Republic v. Orbecido III, 2005); it does not matter which spouse obtained the divorce (Republic v. Manalo, 2018; see also Racho v. Tanaka, 2018, divorce by mutual agreement); the decree and the foreign law must be proven (Garcia v. Recio, 2001) and judicially recognized (Corpuz v. Sto. Marriages exempt from the license requirement (Arts. 27–34): overview

Tolentino: a marriage of exceptional character is one the law considers of such a nature as to dispense with the formal requirement of a license. These marriages are valid without a license, but they must have the essential requisites (Art. 2) and the other formal requisites (Art. 3): an authorized officer and a ceremony. Marriages in articulo mortis (Arts. 27, 29–32)

Art. 27, FC. In case either or both of the contracting parties are at the point of death, the marriage may be solemnized without necessity of a marriage license and shall remain valid even if the ailing party subsequently survives. (72a)
Art. 29, FC. In the cases provided for in the two preceding articles, the solemnizing officer shall state in an affidavit executed before the local civil registrar or any other person legally authorized to administer oaths that the marriage was performed in articulo mortis or that the residence of either party, specifying the barrio or barangay, is so located that there is no means of transportation to enable such party to appear personally before the local civil registrar and that the officer took the necessary steps to ascertain the ages and relationship of the contracting parties and the absence of legal impediment to the marriage. (72a)
Art. 30, FC. The original of the affidavit required in the last preceding article, together with the legible copy of the marriage contract, shall be sent by the person solemnizing the marriage to the local civil registrar of the municipality where it was performed within the period of thirty days after the performance of the marriage. (75a)
Art. 31, FC. A marriage in articulo mortis between passengers or crew members may also be solemnized by a ship captain or by an airplane pilot not only while the ship is at sea or the plane is in flight, but also during stopovers at ports of call. (74a)
Art. 32, FC. A military commander of a unit, who is a commissioned officer, shall likewise have authority to solemnize marriages in articulo mortis between persons within the zone of military operation, whether members of the armed forces or civilians. (74a)
  • Point of death: mere sickness is not enough; a party must actually be at the point of death. If he was, his later recovery does not affect validity ("shall remain valid even if the ailing party subsequently survives") (Tolentino).
  • Who executes the Art. 29 affidavit: the SOLEMNIZING OFFICER, not the parties, before the LCR or any person authorized to administer oaths. Contrast Art. 34, where the parties execute the affidavit and the officer adds a sworn statement.
  • Contents of the affidavit: (a) the marriage was performed in articulo mortis, or the residence of either party (specifying the barrio or barangay) has no means of transportation; and (b) the officer took the necessary steps to ascertain the ages and relationship of the parties and the absence of legal impediment.
  • Art. 30: the original affidavit, with a legible copy of the marriage contract, goes to the LCR of the municipality where the marriage was performed within thirty days. Failure is an irregularity (1995 Bar: not filing the affidavit does not void the marriage).
  • Venue: the place of an articulo mortis marriage is unrestricted (Art. 8 exception).
  • Signature: if the dying party cannot sign, one of the witnesses writes his name and the officer attests (Art. 6 par. 2).

Art. 28, FC. If the residence of either party is so located that there is no means of transportation to enable such party to appear personally before the local civil registrar, the marriage may be solemnized without necessity of a marriage license. (72a)

Muslims and ethnic cultural communities: Art. 33

Art. 33, FC. Marriages among Muslims or among members of the ethnic cultural communities may be performed validly without the necessity of marriage license, provided they are solemnized in accordance with their customs, rites or practices. (78a)
  • Two conditions: (1) the marriage is among Muslims, or among members of ethnic cultural communities (both parties); and (2) it is solemnized in accordance with their customs, rites or practices.
  • NCC 78 limited the privilege to non-Christians living in non-Christian provinces. The FC removed that limitation (Tolentino).
  • Art. 33 recognizes Mohammedan marriages and tribal-rite marriages and abrogates U.S. v. Tubban (Tolentino). People v. Rosil: a Tagbanua tribal marriage was valid, so killing the wife was parricide.
  • Extent of exemption: only the license. The essential requisites still apply.
  • Muslim marriages are governed by PD 1083 (since Feb. 4, 1977). Muslims who marry under civil law, or not in accordance with PD 1083, are governed by the FC (Nollora v. People, 2011; a convert to Islam whose first marriage was civil remains liable for bigamy, Malaki v. People, B29).
Tolentino: he notes the textual implication that the FC's essential requisites (e.g. age) apply even to non-Christian marriages, so a non-Christian aged 16 would not have a valid marriage even if solemnized according to custom. But he "believes" the intent is otherwise: the government's policy is to respect non-Christian customs, which include the requisites of validity, citing People v. Mora Dumpo (where a Muslim marriage was not shown to need the bride's father's consent).
Note (current law): Tolentino's suggestion that the age requirement may yield to custom is outdated. RA 11596 (2021) declares void and penalizes marriages of anyone below 18, and after its one-year transition it applies to Muslim and indigenous communities. Five-year cohabitation: Art. 34 (Niñal, Manzano, De Castro, Dayot)

Art. 34, FC. No license shall be necessary for the marriage of a man and a woman who have lived together as husband and wife for at least five years and without any legal impediment to marry each other. The contracting parties shall state the foregoing facts in an affidavit before any person authorized by law to administer oaths. The solemnizing officer shall also state under oath that he ascertained the qualifications of the contracting parties are found no legal impediment to the marriage. (76a)
Art. 76, NCC (repealed; governs marriages before Aug. 3, 1988). No marriage license shall be necessary when a man and a woman who have attained the age of majority and who, being unmarried, have lived together as husband and wife for at least five years, desire to marry each other. …
Tolentino: the purpose is to legalize unions: to facilitate and encourage the marriage of persons who have lived in concubinage for more than five years, whom the publicity and inconvenience of a license application might embarrass and deter. They are exempt from the license only; all other requirements apply. Source: NCC 76, which required the parties to be at least 21 (age of majority). The FC does not say so expressly, but its provisions on legal capacity apply.

Requisites (Borja-Manzano v. Sanchez)

  1. The man and woman have lived together as husband and wife for at least five years before the marriage;
  2. They have no legal impediment to marry each other;
  3. The absence of legal impediment is present at the time of the marriage;
  4. The parties execute an affidavit that they have lived together for at least five years and are without legal impediment to marry each other; and
  5. The solemnizing officer executes a sworn statement that he ascertained the parties' qualifications and found no legal impediment.
Niñal v. Bayadog (G.R. No. 133778, March 14, 2000; 328 SCRA 122)

Pepito's first wife was killed in 1985. About twenty months later he married Norma without a license, on an affidavit that they had cohabited for at least five years. After Pepito died, his children by the first marriage sued to declare the second marriage void. Doctrine: (a) the five-year period is counted back from the date of the marriage and must be the five years immediately before the wedding; (b) the cohabitation must be of a "perfect union," valid under the law but rendered imperfect only by the absence of the marriage contract, i.e. exclusive and continuous (unbroken), with the parties capacitated to marry each other throughout. Cohabitation while one party was still married is not the cohabitation the law contemplates, even if the spouses had separated in fact; (c) the marriage, celebrated under the Civil Code (Art. 76, "being unmarried"), was void for lack of a license; (d) heirs may question a void marriage even after a party's death, and the action does not prescribe.

Manzano (Borja-Manzano) v. Sanchez (A.M. No. MTJ-00-1329, March 8, 2001; 354 SCRA 1)

A judge solemnized, without a license, the marriage of a man and a woman who were each still married to other spouses (only separated in fact), on their affidavit of seven years' cohabitation. The man's first wife (Herminia Borja-Manzano) filed the complaint. Doctrine: the five requisites above. Because both parties were still married to others at the time of the marriage, there was a legal impediment, and separation in fact does not dissolve a marriage. Art. 34 did not apply, the marriage was void, and the judge, who knew of the subsisting marriages from the parties' own affidavit, was liable for gross ignorance of the law.

De Castro v. Assidao-De Castro (G.R. No. 160172, Feb. 13, 2008)

The couple's license expired before they married, so they executed an affidavit falsely stating that they had lived together for at least five years. Doctrine: a false affidavit of cohabitation cannot substitute for a license. The falsity is not a mere irregularity, so the marriage is void ab initio for want of a license. In an action for support, the court may pass on the validity of the marriage even without a direct action for nullity.

Republic v. Dayot (G.R. No. 175581, March 28, 2008)

A 1986 marriage (Civil Code) was celebrated without a license on an affidavit of five years' cohabitation, when in fact the parties had known each other only months. Doctrine: Art. 76 is an exception to the license requirement and must be strictly construed. The falsity of the affidavit is not a mere irregularity, the marriage is void, and the action for nullity does not prescribe; the spouse who executed the false affidavit is not estopped from assailing the marriage. Per the B29 note, the parties must also have been of age at the start of the five-year period, as Art. 76 NCC required "the age of majority."

Uribe (doctrines outline note and the 2025 class): Uribe draws a CC/FC split. Under the Civil Code (Art. 76, "being unmarried"), Niñal requires the five years immediately preceding the marriage to be entirely free of impediment, so the A–C marriage would be void. Under the Family Code, his reading of Borja-Manzano is that the five-year cohabitation need not be entirely free of impediment; what is required is that there is no impediment at the time of the solemnization. The FC says "without any legal impediment to marry each other" but does not say when. "SIR: Support Borja-Manzano… The SC interpreted [it] for the purpose of encouraging those who lived in illegal unions to legalize their marriage." The doctrines outline records it the same way: "Art. 34 of the Family Code now applies and a perfect 5-year cohabitation period is no longer needed based on Uribe's lecture."
Note (prevailing jurisprudence vs. Uribe): the commentators and the B29 materials read Niñal, Manzano and Dayot together as requiring, even under the FC, that the five years be continuous, exclusive and free of impediment throughout, as well as at the time of the marriage. The quiz bank (R-103) keys Uribe’s answer (valid) and notes this strict view. What to answer in Uribe's exam: first identify the governing law (date of marriage). CC: void if the five years before the marriage were not impediment-free (Niñal). FC: follow Uribe's stated view, that the marriage is valid if the parties lived together at least five years and there is no impediment at the time of the marriage, and state the strict view in one sentence as the contrary position. (Uribe 2025 recitation) A and B are married. A leaves and lives with C as husband and wife. Six years later B dies. Two years after, A and C, still living together, marry without a license on an Art. 34 affidavit. A dies; B's heirs say the A–C marriage is void for lack of a license.

Answer: Uribe's first point: "it depends on when A and C married." The facts must show which law applies. If before Aug. 3, 1988 (CC): void. Under Niñal, the five years immediately before the marriage must be free of impediment ("being unmarried"), and A was married to B for most of that period. If under the FC: per Uribe's reading of Borja-Manzano, valid. They cohabited for at least five years (eight), and at the time of solemnization there was no impediment because B was already dead. The strict reading (Niñal applied to Art. 34; Manzano; Dayot) would make it void because only two impediment-free years preceded the marriage. Write Uribe's answer and note the contrary view.

(Uribe class quiz) "If X died in 1987, the marriage between X and Y may be valid even if there was no marriage license if they cohabited as husband and wife for six years prior to the marriage and their cohabitation was not entirely free from legal impediment."

Answer: TRUE. CC governs (X died in 1987, so the marriage predates the FC). Under Niñal only the five years immediately preceding the marriage must be impediment-free. With six years of cohabitation, the impediment may have existed only in the first year, leaving five clean years. Uribe: "If the impediment is at the start, then the last five are free from impediment." Watch the word "may" (bank R-102).

(2008 Bar) Faye (16) begins living with Roderick (18). At 18 she is forced to marry Brad but keeps seeing Roderick when Brad is at work. At 25 she kills Brad and immediately marries Roderick without a license, claiming more than five years of continuous cohabitation.

Answer: Void under any reading. There was no five-year cohabitation as husband and wife (Faye lived with Brad; Roderick only visited). She was a minor at the start, and was married to Brad during the period. Even on Uribe's "time of marriage" view, killing her own spouse with intent to marry Roderick would be an Art. 38(9) impediment if proven. No license and no valid exemption means void (Art. 35(3)).

Liability for irregularities; illegal marriage ceremonies

  • Art. 4 par. 3: the "party or parties responsible" for an irregularity are liable civilly, criminally and administratively. This may be the LCR (premature issuance, wrong documents), the solemnizing officer (wrong venue, outside jurisdiction, non-transmittal), or the parties (false affidavits).
  • Art. 16: issuing the license within the counseling-suspension period means administrative sanctions for the issuing officer.
  • RPC 350 (marriage contracted against provisions of laws): punishes a person who, not covered by bigamy, contracts marriage knowing that the requirements of the law have not been complied with or in disregard of a legal impediment, with a higher penalty if consent was obtained by violence, intimidation or fraud.
  • RPC 352 (performance of illegal marriage ceremony): priests or ministers of any religious denomination or sect, or civil authorities, who perform or authorize any illegal marriage ceremony are punished under the Marriage Law.
  • The NCC 96 clause preserving the penal laws on licenses, solemnization and authority is the background for these sanctions.
Ronulo v. People (G.R. No. 182438, July 2, 2014)

An Aglipayan priest conducted a ceremony for a couple who had no marriage license. Doctrine: a person who knowingly conducts a marriage ceremony despite the absence of the legal requirements (such as a license) performs an illegal marriage ceremony under RPC 352. No prescribed form is needed for a ceremony (Art. 6); what matters is that the parties appeared before him and declared that they took each other as husband and wife in the presence of witnesses.

Application drills (Uribe-style)

1. (Uribe 2025) On July 30, 1988, Alex (17) and Ben, Marikina residents, elope and apply for a license at Manila City Hall. On Dec. 5, 1988 they are married in Taguig by the Makati mayor; the only witnesses are two 16-year-olds; no certificate is signed. Assume Alex is 18 by Dec. 5, no parent consented, and both believed in good faith in the mayor's authority.

Answer: Voidable (Art. 45(1), no parental consent; "they eloped," so do not hedge with "if"). Age is reckoned at solemnization, so Alex had capacity. The mayor had no authority during the Aug. 3, 1988 to LGC gap, but good faith saves the marriage (Art. 35(2)). The minor witnesses, the Manila license, the Taguig venue and the unsigned certificate are irregularities or non-issues. If Alex were still 17: void. Without good faith: void (bank R-26).

2. A (22) and B (20, never married) apply on Jan. 2, 2011. Without consent or advice, the LCR issues the license on Jan. 15. They marry on May 5, 2011.

Answer: Voidable, for lack of B's parental consent (Art. 45(1)). The license had not expired (120 days from Jan. 15 runs to about May 15). The early issuance without A's advice and without the counseling certificate is an irregularity. If B is a widow, she needs no consent, and the answer becomes valid, but with an irregularity in the issuance of the license (bank R-49, R-50).

3. A Filipina marries an Australian in the Philippine consular office in Australia. Australian law says consular marriages are valid if valid under the consul's own law.

Answer: Void. A Philippine consul may marry only Filipino citizens abroad (Arts. 7(5), 10), and lex loci sends the question back to Philippine law. Absence of authority is not an irregularity (bank R-35).

4. A Hong Kong-based Philippine Consul General, vacationing in Manila, marries a couple at the Manila Hotel; the couple executes an affidavit consenting to the venue.

Answer: Void for lack of authority (a consul's authority exists only abroad), unless either or both believed in good faith in his authority. The venue affidavit (Art. 8) cures only the place, never authority (bank R-34).

5. Two college sweethearts are married inside a Roman Catholic church by a Supreme Court Justice. One later sues for nullity because the officiant was not a priest.

Answer: Not tenable. An incumbent SC Justice has authority throughout the Philippines, and the church venue is at most an irregularity under Art. 8. Venue is neither an essential nor a formal requisite (bank R-36).

6. A couple marries before a judge four days after applying, the license having been issued on the third day; the posting was never done.

Answer: Valid. Premature issuance and non-posting are irregularities (Art. 4 par. 3; Tolentino; Alcantara). The LCR is liable.

7. Marriage solemnized first; the license is issued two days later.

Answer: Void. There was no license at the time of the marriage, and a later license cannot validate it (Tolentino) (bank R-20).

8. A First Officer marries two dying passengers in flight while the airplane chief is in the cabin.

Answer: Under Uribe's answer the First Officer has no authority (Art. 7(3) requires the "chief"). The marriage is void unless either or both believed in good faith that he had authority (Art. 35(2)). If the airplane chief had solemnized it in flight: valid and license-exempt (Arts. 27, 31).

9. A fraternity "wedding" skit is performed by a real judge who is a guest; the "bride" and "groom" sign a souvenir certificate.

Answer: Void and inexistent. There is total absence of consent to a real marriage (jest; simulation for a performance). Contrast Albios: a sham for immigration purposes is still valid, because the parties really wanted to be legally married.

10. A judge solemnizes a marriage in his chambers within his territory, using a valid license; one party is 19 and has written parental consent; there is only one witness, aged 20.

Answer: Safer answer: valid, with an irregularity. Uribe's class treated defects concerning the witnesses (their age) as irregularities in the ceremony ("there is no voidable in formal requisites"); the ceremony itself, meaning personal appearance and declaration before the officer, took place. But Art. 3(3) lists "not less than two witnesses of legal age" as part of the ceremony, so a stricter view could call the ceremony defective. Explain both if the item is an essay.

Last-minute checklist

  • Essential (Art. 2): legal capacity (male and female) and consent freely given in the presence of the solemnizing officer. Formal (Art. 3): authority, license (except Chapter 2), ceremony. Exactly three formal requisites. The marriage certificate is NOT one.
  • Art. 4: absence means void, except Art. 35(2); defect in an essential requisite means voidable (Art. 45); irregularity in a formal requisite means valid, with liability civil, criminal and administrative.
  • "Absence of any of the formal requisites → void": FALSE. "Absence of any essential requisite → void": TRUE. No "voidable" in formal requisites.
  • Good faith: either or both parties. If both knew: void.
  • Below 18: void even with parental consent (35(1); RA 11596, imprescriptible). Age is reckoned at solemnization.
  • 18–21 (not emancipated by a previous marriage): consent, "in the order mentioned," in writing (personal appearance, or an affidavit before 2 witnesses). Missing: voidable; no suspension.
  • 21–25: advice. Missing or unfavorable: license only 3 months after completion of publication. Validity is never affected.
  • Counseling (Art. 16): needed when consent or advice is needed; the other party must be present; issuance within the 3 months means administrative sanction only.
  • Capacity vs. impediment: age is capacity; Arts. 37 and 38 are impediments. This matters for legitimation (RA 9858). First cousins are void because of an impediment, not incapacity.
  • Same sex is void (Silverio: sex at birth). Jest or simulation is void (no consent). Albios: a marriage for convenience is valid. Mistake in identity is void (35(5)); mistake in rank, fortune, character or health is no ground.
  • Proxy: void if in the Philippines (Art. 6 personal appearance; Tolentino); valid if valid abroad (Uribe, Art. 26 lex loci).
  • Art. 7: incumbent judiciary within the court's jurisdiction; priest, rabbi, imam or minister (authorized, registered with the Civil Registrar General, within written authority, at least one party a member); ship captain or airplane chief (Art. 31 only); military commander (Art. 32 only; chaplain absent; military operation); consul (Art. 10, Filipinos abroad). Mayors: LGC 444/455.
  • Mayors: authorized up to Aug. 2, 1988 (NCC 56); none from Aug. 3, 1988 to the LGC; restored by the LGC (effective Jan. 1, 1992). Governors: never. Ambassador: no.
  • Judge outside his territory: irregularity (Navarro v. Domagtoy; Arañes v. Occiano; Beso v. Daguman). Arañes was void because there was no license. Uribe: authority ≠ jurisdiction ≠ venue.
  • Presbyterian minister marries two Catholics: valid, irregularity (Uribe). First Officer: no authority. Airplane chief at the Manila Hotel: no authority (only in flight or at stopovers).
  • Art. 8: chambers or open court, church, chapel or temple, consular office, and not elsewhere, except articulo mortis, remote places (Art. 29), or both parties' written request plus a sworn statement. Violation: irregularity.
  • Art. 6: no prescribed form; personal appearance; declaration; ≥2 witnesses of legal age; certificate signed by the parties and witnesses, attested by the officer. In articulo mortis, one of the witnesses writes the dying party's name and the officer attests.
  • Art. 9: LCR where either party habitually resides. Wrong LCR: irregularity. The officer need not investigate. A wrongfully obtained license does not void the marriage (Tolentino; Eigenmann).
  • Art. 11: each party files separately a sworn application; residence certificates cannot be required.
  • Art. 12: birth certificate, else baptismal, else residence certificate or an affidavit of 2 witnesses (nearest of kin preferred) if requested ≥15 days before. Dispensed with if the parents swear personally, or the LCR is convinced by merely looking.
  • Art. 13: previously married applicants present a death certificate or decree; if the death certificate cannot be secured, an affidavit (circumstance, civil status, name and date of death of the deceased spouse).
  • Art. 17: notice posted 10 consecutive days; license issued after completion of publication. Early issuance: irregularity.
  • Art. 18: LCR notes the impediment in the application and still issues, unless a competent court orders otherwise "at his own instance or that of any interested party"; no fee, no bond. The duty is ministerial (Uribe: the LCR cannot refuse a 19-year-old without consent).
  • Arts. 19, 24: only lawful fees; free for indigents (affidavit or oath); LCR documents and oaths without any charge; documentary stamp tax exempt.
  • Art. 20: 120 days from issue ("four months" = TRUE), anywhere in the Philippines; automatically cancelled. Marriage after expiry: void (no license). Using the license in another city: not even an irregularity.
  • Art. 21: foreigners need a certificate of legal capacity from their diplomatic or consular officials before a license. Stateless persons or refugees submit an affidavit in lieu (capacity per Philippine law). Missing certificate: irregularity, not void (DOJ Op. 050 s. 1991).
  • Art. 22: seven contents of the certificate (name, sex, age; citizenship, religion, habitual residence; date and precise time; license; consent; advice; marriage settlement attached).
  • Art. 23: original to either party; duplicate and triplicate to the LCR of the place of solemnization within 15 days; officer keeps the quadruplicate, the license and the Art. 8 affidavit. Non-transmittal: valid (Madridejo; Pugeda).
  • Proof of marriage: the certificate is the best but not the only evidence (Avenido: birth certificates, witnesses). Presumption of marriage: disputable (Rule 131 Sec. 3(aa)).
  • No-license proof: a certification of due search and inability to find, without suspicious circumstances, suffices (Republic v. CA & Castro); a certification that does not categorically establish non-existence does not (Sevilla v. Cardenas); courts take a holistic view (Cariaga). License number belonging to another couple: void (Abbas). Wrong-residence LCR: irregularity (Alcantara).
  • License-exempt: Art. 27 articulo mortis (valid even if the ailing party survives); Art. 28 no means of transportation (either party; the 15-km female rule is gone); Arts. 31, 32; Art. 33 Muslims and ethnic communities per custom (license only; RA 11596 age still applies); Art. 34, 5-year cohabitation.
  • Art. 29 affidavit is by the solemnizing officer (specify barrio or barangay; ages, relationship, no impediment); Art. 30 sends it with a legible copy of the contract within 30 days. Art. 34 affidavit is by the parties, plus the officer's sworn statement.
  • A license IS required for Filipinos married abroad by a consul (the consul issues it).
  • Art. 34: at least 5 years as husband and wife; no impediment; Manzano's five requisites. CC marriages: Niñal (the 5 years immediately before, entirely impediment-free; exclusive, continuous). FC marriages, Uribe's view: no impediment at the time of solemnization suffices (state the strict contrary view). A false affidavit is not an irregularity: void (De Castro; Dayot).
  • Art. 26(1): valid abroad means valid here, except 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37, 38. Authority, license and proxy issues are not exceptions. Art. 26(2): Orbecido (citizenship at divorce), Manalo (any spouse may initiate), Garcia v. Recio (prove the foreign law), judicial recognition required.
  • Outdated: Tolentino's rape or seduction-victim exception (RA 11596); the widow's 300 days (dropped; RA 10655 repealed RPC 351); NCC 54 ages 16/14; NCC 72 15 km; NCC 80(2) no good-faith rule.
