Persons, personality and status: basic concepts

Person

  • Etymology. From the Latin persona, the mask worn by Roman actors. The word later came to mean the character, or the actor who played it (Tolentino).
  • Juridical sense. A person is any being, physical or moral, real or juridical and legal, that is susceptible of rights and obligations, or of being the subject of legal relations (Tolentino).
  • "Person" is wider than "man." It includes entities with no physical existence, such as corporations and partnerships, that the law endows with legal existence. Since slavery was abolished, every human being is a person, whatever his intelligence or will. Infants and the insane are persons. Even twins who are physically joined are two distinct persons (Tolentino).

Personality

  • Concept. Personality is the quality derived from being a person. A person is a being. Personality is that being's aptitude to be the subject, active or passive, of juridical relations. It is born with man and stays until his death, and the law extends it to juridical persons (Tolentino).
  • Characteristics (Tolentino): (1) it is a quality of certain beings, not a being itself; (2) it is a juridical concept, not a physical element; (3) it cannot be the object of a contract or of possession, and cannot be impaired by agreement; (4) it is a matter of public interest.
  • Relation to capacity. Sanchez Roman treats personality as the product of capacity. In the general sense it cannot be limited, because it flows from juridical capacity, which flows from human nature. In a specific sense it may be limited, because it results from capacity to act. Most civil-law writers, however, treat personality as identical to juridical capacity, and Tolentino says the terms are used interchangeably in the law.

Status

  • Definition. Status is the legal condition or class to which one belongs in society, or one's legal position with regard to the rest of the community. It determines the nature and number of a person's rights and obligations.
  • Kinds: political (e.g., citizenship and suffrage) or civil (from private law). Civil status is grouped three ways: (1) as a member of society (resident or non-resident, citizen or alien); (2) as a member of a family (single, married, widowed or divorced; parent, child, sibling; legitimate, illegitimate or adopted); (3) as to the person himself (age, mental condition, sex). Profession is not a status. It depends on an accidental element, the occupation, and is not inherent in the person (Tolentino).
  • Characteristics of status: it is outside the commerce of man. Hence it is (1) inalienable, (2) imprescriptible, (3) not the object of compromise (NCC 2035(1): "the civil status of persons"), and (4) the action to claim it cannot be renounced, and (5) the rights arising from it cannot be exercised by creditors (Tolentino).

Art. 37: juridical capacity vs. capacity to act

Art. 37, NCC. Juridical capacity, which is the fitness to be the subject of legal relations, is inherent in every natural person and is lost only through death. Capacity to act, which is the power to do acts with legal effect, is acquired and may be lost. (n)

Source: Sanchez Roman and Art. 11 of the Swiss Civil Code. The union of the two capacities is full civil capacity; these are the elements of civil capacity (Tolentino).

Arts. 38–39: restrictions vs. modifications or limitations on capacity to act

Art. 38, NCC. Minority, insanity or imbecility, the state of being a deaf-mute, prodigality and civil interdiction are mere restrictions on capacity to act, and do not exempt the incapacitated person from certain obligations, as when the latter arise from his acts or from property relations, such as easements. (32a)
Art. 39, NCC. The following circumstances, among others, modify or limit capacity to act: age, insanity, imbecility, the state of being a deaf-mute, penalty, prodigality, family relations, alienage, absence, insolvency and trusteeship. The consequences of these circumstances are governed in this Code, other codes, the Rules of Court, and in special laws. Capacity to act is not limited on account of religious belief or political opinion.
A married woman, twenty-one years of age or over, is qualified for all acts of civil life, except in cases specified by law. (n)

Rules

  1. Art. 38 circumstances do not touch juridical capacity. They only restrict the exercise of rights. The incapacitated person keeps his rights to support, to inherit, and to damages for injuries (Tolentino).
  2. Incapacity does not exempt from "certain obligations." The codal examples are obligations arising (a) from his acts, and (b) from property relations, such as easements. Tolentino says the incapacitated person may have obligations from all sources except contracts:
    • Law: family obligations, legal easements (he cannot contract a voluntary easement), taxes.
    • Crimes: he is civilly liable even when exempt from criminal liability (RPC 101).
    • Quasi-delicts: NCC 2180–2182. Under Art. 2182, if the minor or insane person has no parents or guardian, he answers with his own property, through a guardian ad litem.
    • Quasi-contracts: no one may unjustly enrich himself at another's expense. A minor who receives money as a loan acquires ownership but no contractual duty to pay. He must still return what he received or the amount of his enrichment (Art. 22; also Arts. 1241, 1399, 1971).
    • Necessaries: Art. 1489 par. 2. He must pay a reasonable price.
  3. The Art. 39 enumeration is not exclusive ("among others"). Arts. 38 and 39 are general statements of principle. The controlling rules are the specific articles in the Code, other codes, the Rules of Court and special laws (Tolentino). Physical condition is one unlisted circumstance: impotence is a ground for annulment (FC 45(5)), and a person who is blind, deaf or dumb cannot witness a will (Art. 820).
  4. Circumstances that do NOT limit capacity: religious belief and political opinion (express). Tolentino adds race and nobility, which no longer affect capacity though they once did.
  5. Sex no longer limits capacity. The last paragraph of Art. 39 should now read "eighteen years" because of RA 6809 (Tolentino). The Family Code abolished the old differences, such as the wife's lower marrying age, the 300-day bar on her remarriage, and the unequal adultery and concubinage grounds for legal separation. See also Const. Art. II Sec. 14 (fundamental equality of women and men).

Restriction vs. modification or limitation

The Code uses "restrictions" for the Art. 38 list and "modify or limit" for the Art. 39 list, but it attaches no different legal effect to the labels. Tolentino treats all of them together as "causes of incapacity." In both cases, what a person may or may not do is found in the specific provisions.

Reintegration of capacity (Tolentino): some incapacities end automatically: minority on reaching majority, and civil interdiction on service of the principal penalty. Others need a judicial declaration of restored competency. A person declared incompetent (e.g., insane or prodigal under guardianship) must petition the court that he is now competent (Rules of Court on guardianship).

Tolentino (omission criticized): Old Civil Code Art. 32 expressly said incapacitated persons "are susceptible of rights." The present Art. 38 kept the reference to their obligations but dropped the reference to their rights. Tolentino calls this "unfortunate and conducive to confusion," since it might suggest the Code meant to suppress their rights. Other provisions show it did not.

Arts. 40–41: commencement of personality and the conceived child

Art. 40, NCC. Birth determines personality; but the conceived child shall be considered born for all purposes that are favorable to it, provided it be born later with the conditions specified in the following article. (29a)
Art. 41, NCC. For civil purposes, the foetus is considered born if it is alive at the time it is completely delivered from the mother's womb. However, if the foetus had an intra-uterine life of less than seven months, it is not deemed born if it dies within twenty-four hours after its complete delivery from the maternal womb. (30a)

Related provisions (syllabus)

1987 Constitution, Art. II, Sec. 12. The State recognizes the sanctity of family life and shall protect and strengthen the family as a basic autonomous social institution. It shall equally protect the life of the mother and the life of the unborn from conception. The natural and primary right and duty of parents in the rearing of the youth for civic efficiency and the development of moral character shall receive the support of the Government.
PD 603 (Child and Youth Welfare Code), Art. 5. Commencement of Civil Personality. - The civil personality of the child shall commence from the time of his conception, for all purposes favorable to him, subject to the requirements of Article 41 of the Civil Code.
Art. 164, FC (par. 1). Children conceived or born during the marriage of the parents are legitimate.
RPC Arts. 256–259 (abortion), in substance. Art. 256, intentional abortion: reclusión temporal if violence is used on the pregnant woman; prisión mayor if, without violence, the offender acts without her consent; prisión correccional (medium and maximum) if she consented. Art. 257, unintentional abortion: prisión correccional (minimum and medium) on one who causes an abortion by violence, but unintentionally. Art. 258: abortion practiced by the woman herself or by her parents, with a lower penalty if done to conceal her dishonor. Art. 259: abortion by a physician or midwife (penalty of Art. 256 in its maximum period), and a pharmacist who dispenses an abortive without prescription (arresto mayor and fine).

The syllabus pairs these with Arts. 40–41 to show that the law protects the unborn although it has no personality yet. Criminal law punishes its destruction as a separate crime (abortion, not homicide or infanticide), and the Constitution protects its life from conception. Civil personality, however, still depends on birth.

Rule, exception and condition

  1. General rule: birth determines personality. "Birth" means the removal of the foetus from the mother's womb, naturally or by surgical means (e.g., caesarean). Before birth the foetus is not a person but part of the mother's internal organs (Tolentino).
  2. Exception (presumptive or provisional personality): the conceived child is considered born "for all purposes that are favorable to it." This makes its legal existence, if it is born alive, retroact to the moment of conception.
  3. Condition: "provided it be born later with the conditions specified in" Art. 41. If it is not so born, its provisional personality disappears as if it had never existed.

Tolentino lists two characteristics of the conceived child's personality: (1) it is essentially limited, because it covers only favorable purposes; and (2) it is provisional or conditional, because it depends on the child being born alive later.

  • Complete delivery. Total separation is produced by cutting the umbilical cord, whether the removal was natural or surgical (Tolentino).
  • Alive. The child must have an independent extra-uterine life of its own, not a mere continuation of intra-uterine life. The general opinion is that independent life is shown by complete respiration. The cry of the child is evidence of it but not a necessary sign. Another indication is the floating of the lungs in water. Pulsations, convulsive movements and incomplete respiration may be mere continuations of intra-uterine life (Tolentino).
  • Viability is not required. French law requires viability; ours, like the German, requires only that the child be alive, even for an instant. In effect, the law conclusively presumes viability if the foetus is alive at complete separation (Tolentino).
  • Premature birth (less than 7 months). The child must live 24 hours. This holds even if it was killed before the 24 hours lapsed and could have survived but for the wilful act, and likewise if death came by accident. Conversely, personality is acquired even if the 24-hour survival was due only to medical or scientific means (Tolentino).
  • Source and change. Old Civil Code Art. 30 required a "human form" and 24 hours' life for every foetus. The Code Commission dropped the human-form requirement (science shows every foetus born alive has human form; monsters are not born alive) and dropped the 24-hour rule for full-term children, because "the fact of being alive is the significant fact."
  • Period of conception. Science cannot fix the exact moment. Legally, in a normal child, it is the first 120 days of the 300 days preceding birth (Tolentino). Art. 41 is hard to apply because conception cannot be dated exactly.
  • Burden of proof. Some writers say the child is presumed alive and the burden is on whoever alleges otherwise; others put it on whoever claims a right through the child's personality. Tolentino reasons that Art. 41 makes being alive at birth a condition, so it cannot be presumed, and whoever claims the condition was fulfilled must prove it. Once live birth is shown, continuity of life is presumed, and for a premature child the burden of proving it did not live 24 hours shifts to whoever alleges it. Art. 43 does not govern this question, because it presumes two living persons who both died.
Uribe (count the intra-uterine life, not the father's death): The 7-month figure refers to the intra-uterine life of the foetus, not to the interval between the father's death and the birth. A child born five months after the father died may have had nine months of intra-uterine life. On "within" 24 hours: "Kapag sinabing less than 24, edi kapag 24 hrs na, okay na." A premature child that lives a full 24 hours is considered born. He also stresses that the second sentence of Art. 41 is only a qualification of the first.
Quiz traps (Uribe keys):
  • "Birth determines personality; but the conceived child shall be considered born for all purposes, provided..." is FALSE. It must be "for all purposes that are favorable to it."
  • "For civil purposes, the foetus is considered born if it is alive at the time it is completely delivered from the mother's womb" (standing alone) is FALSE, because it is not absolutely true. The 24-hour qualifier for an intra-uterine life of less than 7 months is missing.
  • "...it is not deemed born if it dies within 24 hours after complete delivery, if: (a) the child is born less than 7 months from the death of the father; (b) ...6 months...; (c) ...5 months...; (d) all; (e) none": (e) none of the above. The test is intra-uterine life. Variants use 9, 8 and 7 months "from the death of the father," and the answer is still none.
  • Watch "seven" changed to six or eight, "twenty-four hours" changed to 48 or 72, and "completely delivered" changed to "conceived" or "partially delivered."

Rights of the conceived child (favorable purposes)

  • Donations. It may receive donations, accepted by those who would legally represent it if already born (NCC 742).
  • Succession. It may inherit by will or by intestacy. Under NCC 1025, the heir must be living, or at least conceived, at the moment the succession opens.
  • Support. It is entitled to support from its progenitors even while in the womb (Quimiguing v. Icao).
  • Compensation. Its interests "embrace both personal and property rights, including the right to compensation for personal injuries wrongfully inflicted" (Tolentino, citing US cases).
  • Acknowledgment. It may be acknowledged as a child before birth (De Jesus v. Syquia).
  • Insurance. It may be named an insurance beneficiary (Marian bar problem below).
  • Nothing unfavorable. Provisional personality cannot be used to impose obligations or burdens on the unborn.
Geluz v. Court of Appeals (2 SCRA 801, July 20, 1961)

Nita Villanueva had her pregnancy aborted by Dr. Antonio Geluz without the knowledge of her husband, Oscar Lazo. It was her third abortion by the same doctor. The CA awarded the husband ₱3,000 as the minimum indemnity "for the death of a person." Doctrine: Reversed. (1) The minimum award for the death of a person does not cover an unborn foetus, which is not endowed with personality and is incapable of having rights and obligations. (2) Since an action for pecuniary damages for personal injury or death pertains primarily to the one injured, no such right could accrue derivatively to the parents or heirs of an unborn child. The provisional personality of Art. 40 operates only for purposes favorable to the child and only if it is born alive. An aborted foetus never acquired personality, so no right of action ever vested in it to be transmitted. (3) The parents may recover only damages for injury to themselves: moral damages for the illegal arrest of the normal development of the foetus (the distress and anguish of its loss and the disappointment of their parental expectations) and exemplary damages if warranted (Art. 2230). On the facts, the husband showed indifference to the repeated abortions and more concern with damages than with the loss, so moral damages were not justified.

Quimiguing v. Icao (34 SCRA 132, July 31, 1970)

Carmen Quimiguing alleged that Felix Icao, a married man, had carnal intercourse with her through force and intimidation, and that she became pregnant. She sued for support of the unborn child and damages. The trial court dismissed the complaint because it did not allege that the child had been born, reasoning that the support obligation between parents and illegitimate children (old Art. 291) does not cover unborn children. Doctrine: Reversed. "A conceived child, although as yet unborn, is given by law a provisional personality of its own for all purposes favorable to it, as explicitly provided in Article 40." It has a right to support from its progenitors, even while in the womb, enforceable through an action on its behalf. It may also receive donations (Art. 742). The lower court's theory violated Art. 40. The mother may separately recover damages for the injury to herself (the Court cited Arts. 21 and 2219).

De Jesus v. Syquia (58 Phil. 866, November 28, 1933)

Cesar Syquia had a relationship with Antonia Loanco, who became pregnant. Before the birth he wrote a note to the priest asking him to baptize the expected child, referring to it as his, and letters showing paternal interest. After the birth he lived with mother and child for about a year, and left when she became pregnant again. The child was christened Ismael Loanco rather than "Cesar Syquia, Jr." Doctrine: "It is a universal rule of jurisprudence that a child, upon being conceived, becomes a bearer of legal rights and capable of being dealt with as a living person. The fact that it is yet unborn is no impediment to the acquisition of rights." Recognizing an unborn child is no different from recognizing a child already born and named; only the means of identification differ. The note to the priest could not be read two ways. It referred to a child then conceived and expected in June, and the child's identity was clear despite the different name. The writing was sufficient for compulsory acknowledgment.

Tolentino: De Jesus v. Syquia shows that letters written before birth can ground an action for compulsory acknowledgment after birth. Note, however, that actions for compulsory recognition were abolished by the Family Code, which now speaks of establishing filiation (FC 172–175).
Roe v. Wade (410 U.S. 113, US Supreme Court, January 22, 1973)

A challenge to Texas laws criminalizing abortion. Doctrine (as Uribe's outline uses it): Pregnancy litigation is "capable of repetition, yet evading review." The normal 266-day gestation will end before the usual appellate process is complete, so the case is an exception to the rule that an actual controversy must exist at every stage of review. On the merits, the Court recognized a constitutional right to abortion grounded in privacy, with State interests growing through the trimesters, and held that the unborn is not a "person" under the Fourteenth Amendment.

Note: Roe v. Wade was overruled by the US Supreme Court in Dobbs v. Jackson Women's Health Organization (2022). In any case, Roe is foreign law and contrasts with our own framework: the 1987 Constitution "shall equally protect the life of the mother and the life of the unborn from conception" (Art. II Sec. 12), and the RPC criminalizes abortion. Civil personality, however, remains tied to birth (Arts. 40–41), which is why Geluz denies indemnity "for death" of an unborn foetus.
Continental Steel Manufacturing Corp. v. Montaño (October 13, 2009) — related case, not in syllabus

An employee claimed CBA bereavement leave and death benefits after his wife's premature delivery ended in the death of their unborn child. The employer argued that the child never acquired personality and so could not have "died." Doctrine: Benefits granted. The Civil Code does not say that only those who have acquired juridical personality can die. Death is the cessation of life, and life is not synonymous with civil personality. The unborn child was a "dependent" of the parents. Contrast Geluz: Continental Steel concerns a labor contract (CBA) construed in the worker's favor, not indemnity for the death of a "person" under the Civil Code.

(2012 Bar) Ricky donated ₱1 million to the unborn child of his pregnant girlfriend, and she accepted. After six months of pregnancy the foetus was born and baptized Angela, but she died 20 hours after birth. May Ricky recover the money?

Answer: Yes. The donation is favorable, so the conceived child had provisional personality and could be a donee, with acceptance by its would-be representative (Arts. 40, 742). But Angela had an intra-uterine life of less than seven months and died within 24 hours of complete delivery, so she is not deemed born (Art. 41). Her provisional personality never became definite, and she never had juridical capacity to be a donee. The donation never took effect, and retaining the money would be unjust enrichment (Art. 22). Baptism is irrelevant.

(2022 Bar) Gavin's will devised his rest house to his daughter Remy's unborn child. Gavin and Remy, then seven months pregnant, were in a car accident on December 1, 2021. Gavin died instantly, and Remy prematurely delivered the same day. The baby died at 8:30 A.M. on December 3, 2021. Is the devise valid?

Answer: Yes. The devise is favorable, and the child was conceived when Gavin died (Art. 1025). With an intra-uterine life of seven months, the baby was considered born if alive at complete delivery, and it was. The 24-hour rule applies only to a foetus of less than seven months, and in any case the baby lived about two days. It acquired personality and therefore the devise, which passes to its own heirs.

(2011 Bar, MCQ) X's negligence caused a vehicular accident in which Y, who was pregnant, suffered an abortion. Y sued X for ₱1,000,000, among other damages, for the death of a family member. Is Y entitled to indemnity for the death of the foetus?

Answer: No. "Since birth determines personality, the accident did not result in the death of a person" (Geluz). Y may still recover damages for her own injuries, moral damages for her own anguish, and exemplary damages if the negligence was gross.

(Uribe class problem, "Mr. Luna") Mr. Luna died leaving ₱10 million. Four months after his death his widow gave birth to a child, who died five hours after birth. Two days later the widow died of complications. Luna's parents and the widow's parents both claim the estate. Who is entitled?

Answer: It depends on the child's intra-uterine life, which "four months after Luna's death" does not tell you.
(a) Less than seven months, and died within 24 hours (5 hours): the child is not deemed born and never inherited. Luna's heirs are his legitimate parents and his widow, who share the estate (one-half to the parents, one-half to the widow, NCC 997). On the widow's death, her share passes to her own heirs, her parents.
(b) Seven months or more, and alive at complete delivery: the child was born, became Luna's heir, and excluded Luna's parents. It concurs with the widow. When the child died, its estate passed to its mother. When she died, everything she held passed to her parents.
Uribe's critique of the student's answer: it ignored that the persons now fighting are the two sets of parents, because "patay na ang mga tao na yan" and the dead have no personality. Trace the succession from death to death, and remember that "somebody else might have acquired their rights by succession."

(Uribe situationer) X had five children. A became a New York citizen before X died. B is serving a sentence with civil interdiction. C died before the filing of the estate proceedings. D was born six months after X's death and died six hours after birth. Who may inherit from X by intestacy?

Answer (Uribe): A may inherit. Persons not incapacitated by law may succeed (NCC 1024), and alienage is no bar to succession. B may inherit but cannot accept personally; it is done through the guardian (civil interdiction restricts capacity to act, not juridical capacity). For C, it depends on whether C died before or after X. If after, C inherited and transmits to his own heirs. If before, his descendants may represent him. D "MAY inherit, if his intra-uterine life was 7 months." D was conceived when X died, and if D's intra-uterine life was at least seven months, D was born alive and acquired personality despite living only six hours. If it was less than seven months, D died within 24 hours and is not deemed born.

Art. 42: extinction of personality by death

Art. 42, NCC. Civil personality is extinguished by death.
The effect of death upon the rights and obligations of the deceased is determined by law, by contract and by will. (32a)
  • Natural or physical death only. The law no longer recognizes "civil death," under which a living man was considered dead for all purposes on conviction of crime or on taking religious profession (Tolentino). Compare presumptive death (NCC 390–391; FC 41), which is a presumption about a person who may in fact be alive.
  • Effect on rights. On death the subject of the legal relations disappears. Some rights and obligations are extinguished completely; others are transmitted to successors. The extent of transmission depends on law, contract or will (par. 2). Examples: purely personal rights die with the person (e.g., the right to support, parental authority, the right to bring an annulment action personal to a spouse, except as provided by law). Under NCC 1311, rights and obligations arising from a contract are transmissible to heirs unless they are not transmissible by their nature, by stipulation, or by provision of law. The will governs within the limits of the law on legitimes.
  • The estate continues the personality. For certain purposes, after a person's death his personality is deemed to continue in his estate. The estate (the mass of property, rights and assets left by the decedent) is considered as having legal personality independent of the heirs. Rights and obligations that survive death are exercised and fulfilled only by the estate through the executor or administrator (Tolentino; Limjoco).
  • The corpse is not a person. Juridical capacity is extinguished by death, so the corpse cannot be the subject of rights. Upon death it becomes a thing in the juridical sense, though not an ordinary object of ownership. Its custody and disposal are governed by law (NCC 305–310 on funerals) (Tolentino).
Limjoco v. Intestate Estate of Pedro O. Fragante (80 Phil. 776, April 27, 1948) (listed in the syllabus as "Pio Fragante")

Fragante applied to the Public Service Commission for a certificate of public convenience to operate an ice plant, and died while the application was pending. The PSC allowed his intestate estate to be substituted and granted the certificate. A competitor (Limjoco) objected that a dead man's estate could not be granted the certificate, and could not meet the requirement of Filipino citizenship. Doctrine: The right to prosecute the application survived and became part of the assets of the estate. "Under the present legal system, such rights and obligations as survive after death have to be exercised and fulfilled only by the estate of the deceased." Heirs were once regarded as the continuation of the decedent's personality by legal fiction. Without a similar fiction there would be no juridical basis for the estate, represented by the administrator, to exercise those rights. "The underlying reason for the legal fiction by which, for certain purposes, the estate of the deceased person is considered a 'person' is the avoidance of injustice or prejudice resulting from the impossibility of exercising such legal rights and fulfilling such legal obligations of the decedent as survived after his death unless the fiction is indulged." The decedent's citizenship was deemed to continue in the estate for this purpose.

Dumlao v. Quality Plastic Products, Inc. (70 SCRA 472, April 30, 1976)

A judgment on a surety obligation was rendered against several defendants, among them Pedro Oria, who had already died before the action was filed. His land was later levied upon, and his heirs (the Dumlaos) sued to annul the judgment as to him. Doctrine: The judgment was void as to Oria for lack of jurisdiction over his person. "He was not, and he could not have been, validly served with summons. He had no more civil personality. His juridical capacity, which is the fitness to be the subject of legal relations, was lost through death" (Arts. 37 and 42). A dead person cannot be sued. (His obligation survived against his estate, to be pursued in the proper proceedings against the estate or his heirs.)

Eugenio, Sr. v. Velez (185 SCRA 425, May 17, 1990)

The siblings of Vitaliana Vargas petitioned for habeas corpus, alleging that she was being detained against her will by Tomas Eugenio, with whom she lived. It turned out she had already died, and Eugenio kept her body and refused to surrender it. He argued that the court lost jurisdiction because habeas corpus does not lie for a dead body. Doctrine: When the petition was filed, it was not certain whether Vitaliana was dead or alive. Habeas corpus is a writ of right, but it does not issue as a matter of course; it issues where the petition makes a prima facie case. The court correctly proceeded, treated the matter as one for custody of the dead body, and awarded custody to the siblings. Under Art. 305 and related provisions, the duty and right to make funeral arrangements belong to the surviving spouse and then to relatives in the order of support. A "spouse" in the Civil Code means a lawfully wedded spouse unless the law expressly provides otherwise (as in old Art. 144). Eugenio was not her lawful husband, since he was legally married to another and was incapacitated to marry her. Hence her brothers and sisters, the nearest relatives, had the better right.

How the three death cases fit Art. 42: Limjoco concerns rights that survive death and are exercised by the estate, as an extension of personality. Dumlao holds that the dead person himself cannot be a party, because personality is extinguished. Eugenio holds that the corpse is a thing whose custody follows the law on funerals, not the wishes of a non-spouse.
Quiz traps:
  • "The effect of death upon the rights and obligations of the deceased is determined by law and by will only": FALSE. It is "by law, by contract and by will."
  • "Under what circumstance may the personality of a deceased person continue to exist?" Answer: in the settlement of his estate (Limjoco). Not: the reappearance of a person presumed dead (he never died), a declaration of presumptive death (a living person presumed dead), or intellectual property protection (property passing to heirs).
Note (later cases on suing the dead): The B29 repository also lists Berot v. Siapno (2014) and Gaffney v. Butler (2017): a deceased person, and his estate as such, cannot be impleaded as a defendant because neither has legal personality to be sued. The proper parties are the executor or administrator or, where none, the heirs. In Berot, an heir's voluntary appearance waived the objection. These do not contradict Limjoco, which concerns rights the estate exercises through its administrator.

Art. 43: survivorship between persons called to succeed each other vs. Rule 131, Sec. 3(jj)–(kk)

Art. 43, NCC. If there is a doubt, as between two or more persons who are called to succeed each other, as to which of them died first, whoever alleges the death of one prior to the other, shall prove the same; in the absence of proof, it is presumed that they died at the same time and there shall be no transmission of rights from one to the other. (33)
Rule 131, Sec. 3(jj), Rules of Court. That except for purposes of succession, when two persons perish in the same calamity, such as wreck, battle, or conflagration, and it is not shown who died first, and there are no particular circumstances from which it can be inferred, the survivorship is determined from the probabilities resulting from the strength and the age of the sexes, according to the following rules:
1. If both were under the age of fifteen years, the older is deemed to have survived;
2. If both were above the age of sixty, the younger is deemed to have survived;
3. If one is under fifteen and the other above sixty, the former is deemed to have survived;
4. If both be over fifteen and under sixty, and the sex be different, the male is deemed to have survived; if the sex be the same, the older;
5. If one be under fifteen or over sixty, and the other between those ages, the latter is deemed to have survived.
Rule 131, Sec. 3(kk), Rules of Court. That if there is a doubt, as between two or more persons who are called to succeed each other, as to which of them died first, whoever alleges the death of one prior to the other, shall prove the same; in the absence of proof, they shall be considered to have died at the same time.

Requisites for Art. 43 to apply

  1. Two or more persons called to succeed each other (a question of succession between them).
  2. Both are dead, and there is doubt as to which died first.
  3. No proof, direct or circumstantial, of the order of death. The burden is on "whoever alleges the death of one prior to the other."

Effect: they are presumed to have died at the same time, and there is no transmission of rights from one to the other. Each one's estate goes to his own other heirs. The presumption is disputable.

Uribe ("DO NOT SAY OLDER"): There is no general rule that "the older survives." Older wins only when both are under 15, or when both are between 15 and 60 and of the same sex. Joaquin v. Navarro (93 Phil. 257, May 29, 1953)

During the battle for the liberation of Manila in February 1945, Joaquin Navarro, Sr., his wife Angela Joaquin, and their children, including their son Joaquin Navarro, Jr., died in the fighting. Settlement of the estates turned on whether Angela or her son Joaquin, Jr. died first. If the son survived his mother, he would have inherited from her and transmitted to his own heirs. The CA, finding the order uncertain, applied the Rules of Court presumption favoring the son, a male between 15 and 60. Testimony showed that the son was shot and killed when he left the burning building in which the family had taken refuge, while his mother remained inside. Doctrine: The statutory presumptions (Rule 123 Sec. 69(ii), now Rule 131 Sec. 3[jj], and Art. 43) are substitutes for facts and apply only when there are no facts from which the order of death can be inferred. "Where there are facts, known or knowable, from which a rational conclusion can be made, the presumption does not step in, and the rule of preponderance of evidence controls." What must be certain, as tested by the rules of evidence, are "the particular circumstances from which [survivorship] can be inferred." Inference "is never certainty, but it may be plain enough to justify a finding of fact." On the evidence, the mother survived the son, so the son inherited nothing from her.

Tolentino (the Rules of Court presumptions were not repealed): The presumptions of the Rules of Court have been criticized as "absurd, arbitrary and without any real basis," and the Code Commission chairman said the Rule "is repealed because the presumptions therein laid down are sometimes absurd." Tolentino nonetheless argues there was no repeal. (1) There is no express repeal, and no implied repeal, because the two can stand together. Art. 43 applies to persons called to succeed each other. The Rules apply to those who are not (e.g., insured and beneficiary), and even between successors, the Rules can apply to deaths in the same calamity for non-succession purposes. (2) A general statute does not impliedly repeal a special provision. Old Code Art. 33 (the source of Art. 43) had coexisted with the Rules of Court. (3) The codifiers could have copied foreign codes that extend simultaneous death to calamities, and did not. Their oversight left both provisions standing. Note also that the Rules themselves now say "except for purposes of succession" and carry Art. 43's rule in par. (kk).
Tolentino (Art. 43 applies whether death is actual or presumed): If Pedro died in Paris and his son Juan died in London on the same day, and the hours cannot be established, neither inherits from the other. Pedro's property goes to his other heirs, and Juan's to his own heirs (excluding Pedro). The same result follows if both have been absent over ten years and are presumed dead for purposes of succession (Art. 390).

(1999/2012 Bar; Uribe class) Marian, 18, insured her life and named her unborn child as sole beneficiary. When already due to give birth, she and the child's father, Pietro, were kidnapped. A week later the military found them: Marian and Pietro had been hacked with bolos. Marian and the baby she had delivered were both dead, with the baby's umbilical cord already cut. Pietro survived. (a) Can the baby be the beneficiary? (b) Between Marian and the baby, who is presumed to have died first? (c) May Pietro claim the proceeds?

Answer: (a) Yes. Designation as beneficiary is favorable, so the conceived child had provisional personality (Art. 40). That personality becomes definite only if the child is born under Art. 41. Marian was "already due," so intra-uterine life was at least seven months, and the baby needed only to be alive at complete delivery. The cut umbilical cord shows complete delivery. Uribe agreed that it also suggests the baby was alive ("Kasi kung patay na yan, bat ica-cut pa yan?"). Whether it was alive must still be proved.
(b) If the baby was never born alive, it never became a person and there is no survivorship question. If it was born alive, the issue concerns insurance, not succession, so Art. 43 does not apply. The facts give no basis to infer who died first ("both found dead"), so Rule 131 Sec. 3(jj) applies: one under 15 (the baby) and one between 15 and 60 (Marian, 18). The latter is deemed to have survived, so the baby is presumed to have died first. If the question were framed as succession between mother and child, Art. 43 would apply and they would be presumed to have died at the same time, with no transmission.
(c) No. Since the baby is presumed to have died before Marian (the insured), it acquired no right to the proceeds. Under insurance law the beneficiary must survive the insured. The proceeds go to Marian's estate. Pietro, who was not married to Marian, is not her heir, so he has nothing to inherit through the baby.

(Bar) Dr. Lopez, a 70-year-old widower, and his son Roberto died in a fire at their home. Roberto's wife Marilyn and their two children were in the province. Dr. Lopez left a ₱20M estate and a ₱1M life insurance policy naming his three children, including Roberto, as beneficiaries. Marilyn claims Roberto's share in both. Rule on her claims.

Answer: Estate: this is succession between father and son, so Art. 43 applies. Absent proof, they are presumed to have died at the same time, with no transmission, so Roberto inherited nothing from his father. Roberto's children may inherit from their grandfather by right of representation. Marilyn cannot, because a spouse does not represent. Insurance: this is not succession, and the two died in the same calamity, so Rule 131 Sec. 3(jj) applies. Dr. Lopez (over 60) and Roberto (between 15 and 60): Roberto is deemed to have survived. His one-third share of the proceeds vested in him and passes to his heirs, including Marilyn.

Arts. 44–47: juridical persons

Art. 44, NCC. The following are juridical persons:
(1) The State and its political subdivisions;
(2) Other corporations, institutions and entities for public interest or purpose, created by law; their personality begins as soon as they have been constituted according to law;
(3) Corporations, partnerships and associations for private interest or purpose to which the law grants a juridical personality, separate and distinct from that of each shareholder, partner or member. (35a)
Art. 45, NCC. Juridical persons mentioned in Nos. 1 and 2 of the preceding article are governed by the laws creating or recognizing them.
Private corporations are regulated by laws of general application on the subject.
Partnerships and associations for private interest or purpose are governed by the provisions of this Code concerning partnerships. (36 and 37a)
Art. 46, NCC. Juridical persons may acquire and possess property of all kinds, as well as incur obligations and bring civil or criminal actions, in conformity with the laws and regulations of their organization. (38a)
Art. 47, NCC. Upon the dissolution of corporations, institutions and other entities for public interest or purpose mentioned in No. 2 of Article 44, their property and other assets shall be disposed of in pursuance of law or the charter creating them. If nothing has been specified on this point, the property and other assets shall be applied to similar purposes for the benefit of the region, province, city or municipality which during the existence of the institution derived the principal benefits from the same. (39a)

Concept

A juridical person is an abstract being formed to realize collective purposes, to which the law grants capacity for rights and obligations. Not every association is one. The State must recognize in it a separate personality, distinct from its members, with its own rights and obligations and its own property not merged with theirs. Members may change while the entity continues. Its personality operates only in patrimonial relations: it has no family or personal rights (Tolentino).

  • Capacity of juridical persons (Tolentino). They are not at par with natural persons. They cannot exercise rights that presuppose physical existence, such as family rights or making a will. Like natural persons, however, they have a nationality, a domicile (Art. 51), a name, a right to reputation, and capacity for relations that do not require ties of blood (e.g., guardianship). They can own, possess and dispose of property, contract, inherit by will, and incur obligations, and they are liable for the acts of their managers and employees in contractual and extra-contractual fault.
  • Extinguishment of capacity comes with the termination of existence under the governing law. For a corporation, this means repeal of its charter, expiry of its term, failure of members, voluntary surrender, judicial dissolution (quo warranto), or failure to organize and commence business. Insolvency by itself does not dissolve a corporation. A partnership is dissolved by the causes in Art. 1830 (e.g., death, insolvency or civil interdiction of any partner) but continues until winding up is completed (Art. 1829).
  • Associations without personality may exist, since personality is not indispensable to every organization. The Code does not regulate them, and they are governed by the rules on co-ownership (Tolentino).
  • Piercing. The corporation is treated as a legal entity until sufficient reason to the contrary appears. Its separate personality may be disregarded, e.g., where the sole owner of all its stock deals with a third person in his own name as the corporation's agent (Tolentino).
  • Tolentino's criticism of Art. 45. Old Code Art. 37 said that associations' capacity was determined by their articles of association. Dropping this makes the present article inaccurate. Partnerships and associations are governed primarily by their contract and only secondarily by law, since the contract has the force of law between the parties.
  • A government that owns stock in a private corporation does not make it public. It is "public" only if founded by the government for public purposes with the whole interest belonging to the government (Tolentino).
Smith, Bell & Co. v. Natividad (40 Phil. 136, September 17, 1919)

Smith, Bell & Co., a Philippine corporation whose stockholders were British, was denied a certificate of Philippine registry for its vessel under Act No. 2761, which limited such certificates in the coastwise trade to citizens and to corporations wholly owned by citizens. Doctrine: A corporation, even one with alien stockholders, is a "person" entitled to the protection of the due process and equal protection clauses. Act No. 2761, however, is not that "vicious species of class legislation which must always be condemned." It falls within the authorized exceptions, notably the police power, and so is valid. Relevance: constitutional guarantees for "persons" protect juridical persons, but the State may validly distinguish on the basis of alienage (cf. Art. 39).

Barlin v. Ramirez (7 Phil. 41, November 24, 1906)

Father Vicente Ramirez, parish priest of Lagonoy, Camarines, held the church and its properties for the Roman Catholic Church. He later adhered to the Independent Filipino (Aglipayan) Church and refused to turn them over to Bishop Jorge Barlin. He argued that the Roman Catholic Church had no legal personality in the Philippines. Doctrine: The Roman Catholic Church is a juridical entity in the Philippines. Under Art. 38 of the old Civil Code (now Art. 46), juridical persons may acquire and possess property of all kinds, incur obligations, and bring civil or criminal actions in conformity with the laws and regulations of their organization. It need not be shown that the Church owned the buildings. It suffices that it had, before 1898 and the Treaty of Paris, the exclusive right to possess and administer them for the purposes for which they were consecrated. The suggestion that it lacked personality, made "with reference to an institution which antedates by almost a thousand years any other personality in Europe," did "not require serious consideration."

Note (B29 case): Philippine Primark Properties v. China Banking Corp. Trust and Assets Management Group (2024) held that a bank's trust department has no juridical personality. It falls under none of the Art. 44 classes, and nothing in the General Banking Law gives it separate personality. The bank itself is the juridical person and the proper party.

Restrictions on capacity to act: the general rule is a presumption of capacity

Rule: capacity to act is presumed. Whoever alleges incapacity (minority, insanity, etc.) at the time of the act has the burden of proving it. Once a person has been judicially declared incapacitated (e.g., placed under guardianship), incapacity is in turn presumed to continue, but only as a rebuttable presumption (juris tantum).

Standard Oil Co. of New York v. Codina Arenas (19 Phil. 363, July 25, 1911)

On December 15, 1908, Vicente Villanueva signed a bond as surety for Juan Codina Arenas in favor of Standard Oil. Sued on the bond, he was declared in default. When judgment was about to be executed, his wife asked that he be relieved because he was insane. He had been declared insane on July 24, 1909 (after signing), and she had been appointed his guardian. The evidence showed he executed the bond with full understanding of its nature and consequences, although he suffered from a monomania of great wealth. Doctrine: He was held liable. It would have had to be shown that the monomania was habitual and amounted to a veritable mental perturbation, that the bond resulted from it and from no other cause, and that the monomania existed when the bond was executed. "Monomania of wealth does not necessarily imply that the person is incapable of executing a bond such as that in question." Absent a judicial declaration, it must be presumed that he acts without the influence of a perturbed mind. "Capacity to act must be supposed to attach to a person who has not previously been declared incapable, and such capacity is presumed to continue so long as the contrary is not proved," that is, that at the moment he acted he was insane or out of his mind.

Note: Uribe's doctrines outline garbles the holding ("not really insane and therefore is deranged and incapable"). The correct reading is that monomania of wealth did not make the surety incapable, and the bond bound him. The B29 repository's "(2020)" date is also wrong. The case is from 1911.

Related (B29): Dumaguin v. Reynolds (1952): a prior guardianship creates only a presumption juris tantum of incapacity, rebutted by proof that the person understood the transactions when he signed them. Catalan v. Basa (2007): mental illness (schizophrenia) does not automatically deprive a person of capacity to act. The party alleging incapacity must prove that the donor lacked control of his mental faculties at the time of the donation, and a donation made with understanding is valid. Heirs of Favis v. Gonzales (2014): the donor's mental state at the time of execution governs.

Mercado v. Espiritu (37 Phil. 215, December 1, 1917)

The Mercado siblings sold land they had inherited from their mother. Two of them, aged 18 and 19, were minors under the then age of majority. In the deed, and before the notary, they declared that they were of legal age. They later sued to annul the sale for minority. Doctrine: "The sale of real estate, made by minors who pretend to be of legal age, when in fact they are not, is valid, and they will not be permitted to excuse themselves from the fulfillment of the obligations contracted by them, or to have them annulled." The courts have laid down the rule that a sale by minors who have passed the ages of puberty and adolescence, who are near the adult age, and who pretend to have reached majority, is valid. This accords with the law on estoppel (NCC 1431; Rules of Court).

Young v. Tecson (39 O.G. 953, Court of Appeals)

Doctrine (as quoted by Tolentino, and contrary to Mercado): "Misrepresentation made by a party as to his age does not estop him from denying that he was of age or from asserting that he was under age, at the time he entered into the contract, for the breach of which an action is brought. Under the principle of estoppel, the liability resulting from misrepresentation has its juridical source in the capacity of the person making the misrepresentation to bind himself. If the person making the misrepresentation cannot bind himself by a contract, he cannot also be bound by any misrepresentation he may have made in connection therewith. A person entering into a contract must see to it that the other party has sufficient capacity to bind himself."

Note (Young v. Tecson: what to answer): Tolentino cites Young v. Tecson, a Court of Appeals decision, as "authority to the effect that the misrepresentation of an incapacitated person does not bind him." That is the opposite of Mercado. Uribe's doctrines outline instead files under Young v. Tecson the line that a minor's later disclosure of his minority, one month after the contract, "is of no moment" because he is already estopped. That line is actually from Sia Suan v. Alcantara. For the quiz, follow the Supreme Court rule (Mercado, Sia Suan): active misrepresentation of age estops the minor. Cite Young v. Tecson as the contrary CA view that the SC did not adopt. Uribe's own key on "a minor may not annul if he actively misrepresented his age under the principle of ___" is estoppel.
Bambalan v. Maramba (51 Phil. 417, January 30, 1928)

Isidro Bambalan, a minor, executed a deed of sale of land he had inherited. His minority was well known to the buyer. The buyer had herself bought the cedula that the minor used to acknowledge the document before the notary, and she knew his age. Bambalan later sued to annul. Doctrine: "The contract of purchase and sale of real property executed by a minor is vitiated to the extent of being void as regards said minor." Mercado does not apply, because the plaintiff did not pretend to be of age and the defendant knew he was a minor. There is no estoppel without misrepresentation and reliance.

Note: Bambalan (1928) used "void." Under the present Civil Code, a contract where one party is incapable of consent is voidable (NCC 1390(1)), not void. Use the case for its estoppel holding (no misrepresentation, so no estoppel), and classify the contract as voidable.
Sia Suan and Gaw Chiao v. Alcantara (85 Phil. 669, March 4, 1950)

In 1931, Rufino Alcantara and his sons, including Ramon, then a minor, sold parcels of land to Sia Suan to settle a pre-existing debt. The deed stated that Ramon was of legal age. About a month later, Ramon's lawyer informed the buyers of his minority, but Ramon soon ratified the sale on receiving ₱500. Years later he sued to annul the deed as to his share. Doctrine: (1) A conveyance for a pre-existing indebtedness has a valid consideration, and absent any other vice it has full force and effect. (2) "The circumstance that, about one month after the date of the conveyance, the appellee informed the appellants of his minority, is of no moment, because appellee's previous misrepresentation had already estopped him from disavowing the contract." The belated information merely shows that the buyers did not know of the minority when they contracted, and it emphasizes the minor's bad faith, since he then ratified on receiving ₱500. The contract became "fully efficacious as a contract executed by parties with full legal capacity."

De Braganza v. De Villa Abrille (105 Phil. 456, April 13, 1959)

During the Japanese occupation, Rosario de Braganza and her sons Rodolfo and Guillermo borrowed money in Japanese war notes. They promised in writing to pay a larger amount in Philippine currency after the war. The sons were minors when they signed. Sued on the note, they pleaded minority. Doctrine: (1) "The failure of the minor to disclose his minority when making a contract does not per se constitute a fraud which can be made the basis of an action of deceit. In order to hold the minor liable, the fraud must be actual and not constructive." Merely signing, without an active misrepresentation of age, does not estop the minor. The sons were not bound by the contract. (2) Although the written contract is unenforceable against them because of non-age, the minor shall make restitution to the extent that he may have profited by the thing received (old Civil Code Art. 1304, now NCC 1399). Since they had used the money for their support, they profited and had to restore its value. (3) Where minority is set up only as a defense to an action, without the minor seeking positive relief from the contract, the four-year period of Art. 1301 (now 1391) for annulment does not apply.

Tolentino (synthesis): "The unemancipated minor cannot enter into contracts (article 1327, par. 1); but he may be estopped from disavowing his contract if he has misled the other party as to his age" (Mercado). "The mere fact that one month after the execution of the contract, the minor informed the other contracting party of his minority, does not affect the case; such subsequent information is of no moment, because his previous misrepresentation has already estopped him from disavowing the contract. Such belated information merely confirms the fact that the other party did not know of the minor's true age on the date of the contract, and somewhat emphasizes the latter's bad faith in making the misrepresentation" (Sia Suan).

A, 17, sold his inherited lot to B. The deed recites that A is "of legal age," and A showed B a fake ID. A month later A told B his true age. At 19, A sues to annul. Will it prosper?

Answer: Unenforceable, because both parties are incapable of giving consent (NCC 1403(3)). Ratification by the parent or guardian of one party makes it voidable. Ratification by both makes it valid from the start (NCC 1407).

Concepts (Tolentino)

  • Insanity includes the various forms of mental disease, inherited or acquired, in which the mentality is perverted: illusions, hallucinations, delusions, abnormal exaltation or depression, insane ideas of persecution. Examples are dementia praecox, paranoia, schizophrenia, mania and melancholia.
  • Mental deficiency (amentia) is a defect in the quantity of mental development, existing from birth or from arrested development in childhood. It has three grades: the idiot (lowest; never develops beyond a mental age of about two years); the imbecile (mental age up to the completed eighth year); and the feeble-minded or moron (highest; mental ages nine to twelve).
  • An insane person cannot make a valid will (NCC 798) and cannot give valid consent to contracts (NCC 1327(2)).

United States v. Vaquilar (27 Phil. 88, March 13, 1914)

Vaquilar killed his wife and daughter and wounded others. Witnesses described strange behavior before the killings and in jail, where he alternated between silence and hysteria. He pleaded insanity. Doctrine: "One who, possessing a sound mind, commits a criminal act under the impulse of passion or revenge, which may temporarily dethrone reason and for the moment control the will, cannot nevertheless be shielded from the consequences of the act by the plea of insanity. Insanity will only excuse the commission of a criminal act, when it is made affirmatively to appear that the person committing it was insane, and that the offense was the direct consequence of his insanity." The burden of proving insanity rests on the accused who claims it. Without such proof, the presumption of sanity holds.

People v. Rafanan, Jr. (204 SCRA 65, November 21, 1991)

The accused raped Estelita Ronaya and pleaded insanity (schizophrenia), relying on a later psychiatric examination. Doctrine: "The law presumes every man to be sane. A person accused of a crime has the burden of proving his affirmative allegation of insanity." The accused failed to present clear and convincing evidence of his state of mind immediately before and during the assault. Inquiry into the mental state of the accused should relate to the period immediately before or at the very moment the act is committed. Under the test applied, there must be a complete deprivation of intelligence in committing the act, or a total deprivation of the will. Mere abnormality of the mental faculties does not exclude imputability.

Note (current test): In People v. Paña (2020), the Court adopted a three-way test: (1) insanity must be present at the time of the commission of the crime; (2) insanity, as the primary cause of the criminal act, must be medically proven; and (3) its effect is the inability to appreciate the nature and quality or wrongfulness of the act. It also held that the quantum of proof is clear and convincing evidence. Cite Vaquilar and Rafanan for the presumption of sanity and the burden of proof, and Paña for the current standard.
How the three syllabus insanity cases line up: Vaquilar: passion or anger is not insanity, and insanity must cause the act. Standard Oil (civil): monomania does not equal incapacity, and capacity is presumed absent a prior judicial declaration. Rafanan: sanity is presumed, the accused bears the burden, and the relevant time is the moment of the act. Common thread: sanity and capacity are presumed, and the relevant time is the time of the act.

Mental age (B29): Sister Pilar Versoza v. People (2019): a person's capacity to give or withhold consent is determined by mental age, not chronological age. An adult with the mental age of a child is treated like a child for capacity to consent.

Restriction 4: prodigality

Rule 92, Sec. 2, Rules of Court. Meaning of word "incompetent." — Under this rule, the word "incompetent" includes persons suffering the penalty of civil interdiction or who are hospitalized lepers, prodigals, deaf and dumb who are unable to read and write, those who are of unsound mind, even though they have lucid intervals, and persons not being of unsound mind, but by reason of age, disease, weak mind, and other similar causes, cannot, without outside aid, take care of themselves and manage their property, becoming thereby an easy prey for deceit and exploitation.
  • Prodigal (spendthrift): "a person who, by excessive drinking, gambling, idleness or debauchery of any kind shall so spend, waste or lessen his estate as to expose himself or his family to want or suffering." The acts must show a morbid state of mind and a disposition to spend, waste and lessen the estate to the extent likely to expose the family to want of support, or to deprive the compulsory heirs of their legitime (Tolentino, citing Martinez v. Martinez, 1 Phil. 182).
  • Effect: prodigality in itself does not limit capacity. The prodigal may still contract and make wills, since no provision incapacitates him for any particular act. Only when he is placed under guardianship as an incompetent is his capacity restricted. From then on he can bind himself only through his guardian.
Tolentino (criticism): "It seems erroneous ... to include prodigality in the present article as a circumstance which limits capacity, because there is no specific provision of law which implements this general provision. It is not the circumstance of prodigality, but the fact of being under guardianship, that restricts the capacity to act of the spendthrift." The same applies to hospitalized lepers and others under Rule 92. Without guardianship they keep capacity. By contrast, persons under civil interdiction, deaf-mutes who cannot read and write, and the insane have limited capacity even without guardianship, because specific provisions restrict them.

Family relations limit capacity for certain acts (Tolentino):

  • Marriage. Ascendants and descendants, siblings, and collateral relatives within the fourth civil degree cannot validly marry each other (FC 37, 38(1)). Other relationships, by affinity or adoption, are also impediments (FC 38).
  • Donations between spouses during the marriage are void, except moderate gifts on family rejoicing (FC 87). The rule extends to common-law partners.
  • Sale between spouses is void, except when a separation of property was agreed upon in the marriage settlements or there has been a judicial separation of property (NCC 1490).
  • Suits between family members do not prosper unless earnest efforts toward a compromise were made and failed (FC 151). The enumeration in FC 150 is construed strictly: collateral relatives beyond siblings, such as cousins or in-laws, are not "family" for this purpose.
  • Testimony. There is a marital disqualification and a filial privilege (Rules of Court, Rule 130). A descendant cannot be compelled to testify against his parents and ascendants in a criminal case (NCC 315, now FC 215).
  • Criminal law. Relationship may alter, exempt or aggravate liability: e.g., RPC 332 (no criminal liability, only civil, for theft, swindling and malicious mischief between spouses and certain relatives), and RPC 15 (relationship as an alternative circumstance). Modification 7: alienage

  • Rule: citizenship may affect a person's rights in matters where the State may validly discriminate between aliens and citizens for reasons of public policy, without violating equal protection (Tolentino; Smith Bell).

  • Insolvency. When a person has been adjudicated insolvent, his capacity to dispose of his property becomes limited. He cannot dispose of property existing at the commencement of the insolvency proceedings, and no payment of property or credit can be made to him (Tolentino, citing the old Insolvency Law, Act 1956). The present law is the Financial Rehabilitation and Insolvency Act of 2010 (RA 10142). B29 cites Umale v. Quiz trap: The incapacity under Art. 1491 is relative. It bars the trustee from buying that particular property, even at public auction and even through an intermediary. It is not a general incapacity to contract. Classify it under "trusteeship" (Art. 39), not under the Art. 38 restrictions.

Arts. 48–49: citizenship (brief)

Art. 49, NCC. Naturalization and the loss and reacquisition of citizenship of the Philippines are governed by special laws. (n)
  • The enumeration of citizens in NCC Art. 48 has been superseded by Const. Art. IV, Sec. 1: (1) those who are citizens at the time of the adoption of the 1987 Constitution; (2) those whose fathers or mothers are citizens; (3) those born before January 17, 1973, of Filipino mothers, who elect Philippine citizenship on reaching majority; (4) those naturalized according to law. Natural-born citizens are those who are citizens from birth without having to perform any act to acquire or perfect citizenship, and those who elect under (3) are deemed natural-born (Sec. 2). Citizens who marry aliens keep their citizenship unless by their act or omission they are deemed to have renounced it (Sec. 4).

Arts. 50–51, FC 68–69: domicile and residence

Art. 68, FC. The husband and wife are obliged to live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, and render mutual help and support. (109a)

Art. 110, NCC (repealed; for comparison). The husband shall fix the residence of the family. But the court may exempt the wife from living with the husband if he should live abroad unless in the service of the Republic.

Concept of domicile (Tolentino)

  • Definition: the place of a person's habitual residence. It is where he has his true, fixed, permanent home and principal establishment, to which he intends to return whenever absent (animus revertendi), and from which he has no present intention of moving.
  • Two elements: (1) the fact of residing, or physical presence, in a fixed place; and (2) the intention to remain there permanently (animus manendi).
  • Art. 50 defines domicile for civil purposes ("exercise of civil rights and fulfillment of civil obligations"). For political purposes, domicile is determined by the special criteria of political (election) laws.

When "residence" means "domicile"

  • Election law: "residence" as a qualification is synonymous with domicile. It requires residence plus intention to remain, and sojourning in a place, however long, does not establish it (Tolentino; Romualdez-Marcos; Poe-Llamanzares).
  • Venue (Rules of Court): Tolentino states that "residence" in Rule 4 is synonymous with domicile. Later jurisprudence holds that for venue (personal actions; settlement of estates under Rule 73) "residence" means actual, physical residence, not legal domicile (e.g., Garcia Fule v. CA (1976); San Luis v. San Luis (2007, B29)). Follow the later cases.

Kinds of domicile

  1. Domicile of origin: acquired at birth; that of the parents.
  2. Domicile of choice (domicilium voluntarium): acquired voluntarily by a person with capacity, by abandoning the old domicile.
  3. Domicile by operation of law (necessary domicile): fixed by law. The domicile of persons under parental authority is that of the persons exercising it (Tolentino). The family domicile is fixed jointly by the spouses (FC 69).

Change of domicile

Every person has a constitutional right to change his domicile, and the right cannot be limited by contract or by will (Tolentino). Tolentino gives three requisites: (1) capacity to choose a new domicile; (2) actual presence in the new residence chosen; (3) intention to remain there permanently. Romualdez-Marcos gives the accepted formulation:

  1. an actual removal or an actual change of domicile;
  2. a bona fide intention of abandoning the former place of residence and establishing a new one; and
  3. definite acts which correspond with the purpose.
  • Domicile is not lost by temporary absence or by simply living elsewhere. Examples: obtaining a residence certificate elsewhere; a temporary stay for study; building a house and educating one's children in another municipality for business, if conduct shows intent to keep the old domicile; enlisting in the military; a diplomat's foreign assignment (Tolentino).
  • Abandonment requires "a deliberate and provable choice of a new domicile, coupled with actual residence in the place chosen, with a declared or provable intent that it should be one's fixed and permanent place of abode, one's home" (Tolentino).
  • Whoever alleges that a person abandoned his domicile of origin must prove it by preponderance of evidence (Abella).
Abella v. COMELEC (201 SCRA 253, September 3, 1991)

Adelina Larrazabal ran for Governor of Leyte, claiming residence and registration as a voter in Kananga, Leyte. The COMELEC found that she actually resided in Ormoc City, an independent component city whose voters cannot vote for provincial officials, where she lived with her husband. It disqualified her, relying in part on the Family Code provisions that spouses must live together (FC 68) and jointly fix the family domicile (FC 69). She argued that under election law residence is a matter of intention, the animus revertendi, and that she had never abandoned Kananga. Doctrine: Disqualification upheld. (1) "Mere absence from one's residence or origin-domicile to pursue studies, engage in business, or practice his avocation, is not sufficient to constitute abandonment or loss of such residence." (2) "The determination of a person's legal residence or domicile largely depends upon intention which may be inferred from his acts, activities and utterances. The party who claims that a person has abandoned or left his residence of origin must show and prove preponderantly such abandonment or loss." (3) On the facts, there was no evidence that she left Kananga temporarily in 1975 to pursue a calling or business. What was clear was that she established her residence in Ormoc City with her husband and considered herself a resident there, so animus revertendi to Kananga was absent. Occasional visits do not show intent to keep a residence, because "it is common among us Filipinos to often visit places where we formerly resided." (The second-placer, Abella, could not be proclaimed in her place.)

Romualdez-Marcos v. COMELEC (248 SCRA 300, 1995)

Imelda Romualdez-Marcos filed her certificate of candidacy for Representative of the First District of Leyte. She first wrote that she had resided there "seven months," then amended it to "since childhood." She was challenged for failing the one-year residency requirement. Doctrine: (1) For election purposes, "residence" is synonymous with domicile. (2) Her domicile of origin was Tacloban/Tolosa, Leyte, where she was brought as a child and schooled. Her later stays in Manila, San Juan and Batac, Ilocos Norte, as a student, wife of a congressman and President, and later, did not show an intent to abandon it. (3) To change domicile there must concur (a) an actual removal or change of domicile, (b) a bona fide intention of abandoning the former residence and establishing a new one, and (c) definite acts corresponding to the purpose. Absent these, the domicile of origin continues. Domicile, once acquired, is retained until a new one is gained. (4) A wife does not automatically lose her domicile of origin by marriage. Old Art. 110 let the husband fix the family "residence," which means actual residence, not domicile. At most she gained a new residence, and after her husband's death she could freely return to her domicile of origin. (5) The one-year requirement is a separate constitutional qualification. It is not an element of a change of domicile.

Poe-Llamanzares v. COMELEC (2016) — related (B29)

A former US citizen who returned for good in 2005 and reacquired Philippine citizenship in 2006 claimed ten years' residence for the 2016 presidential election. Doctrine: Residence may be reckoned from her return in 2005, as shown by acts evidencing intent to stay (enrolling the children, selling the US home, relocating assets), even before reacquiring citizenship. A new domicile requires (1) residence or bodily presence in the new locality, (2) an intention to remain there, and (3) an intention to abandon the old domicile.

Family domicile (FC 69)

  • General rule: joint. "The husband and wife shall fix the family domicile." In case of disagreement, the court decides (through a summary proceeding, FC 253 in relation to FC 69). Under the old Code (Art. 110) the husband alone fixed the residence. The Family Code reflects the spouses' equality.
  • Exemption (par. 2): the court may exempt one spouse from living with the other (a) if the latter lives abroad, or (b) for other valid and compelling reasons. The exemption is discretionary, not automatic.
  • Exception to the exemption: it "shall not apply if the same is not compatible with the solidarity of the family."
  • Note the change from NCC 110. The old Code exempted only the wife, and only if the husband lived abroad "unless in the service of the Republic." Under FC 69, either spouse may be exempted, there is no "service of the Republic" proviso, and there is the family-solidarity limit.
  • The duty to live together (FC 68) cannot be enforced by compulsion or contempt. Its sanction lies in other remedies, e.g., support, legal separation for abandonment, and separation of property (see Arroyo v. Vasquez de Arroyo in the spouses section).
Quiz traps (Uribe keys):
  • "For the exercise of civil rights and the fulfillment of civil obligations, the domicile of natural persons is the place of their actual residence": FALSE. The codal says "habitual residence."
  • "When the law creating or recognizing them... does not fix the domicile of juridical persons, the same shall be understood to be the place where their legal representation is established or where they exercise their principal functions": TRUE (Art. 51). Watch for "and" swapped in for "or," or "principal office" swapped in for "principal functions."
  • "The family domicile shall be fixed by: (a) the husband; (b) the wife; (c) H and W, and in case of disagreement the husband's decision prevails; (d) ...the family court shall decide; (e) ...the court shall decide": (e), the exact codal wording. Choice (c) states the old Civil Code rule (NCC 110).
  • "In case of disagreement as to the family domicile, the decision of the husband shall prevail, subject to the wife's recourse to the court": FALSE. Contrast FC 96/124 (administration of the community or conjugal property), where the husband's decision prevails, subject to the wife's recourse to the court, and FC 211 (parental authority), where the father's decision prevails unless there is a judicial order to the contrary.
  • "To effect a change of domicile, the following must concur, except: ... continuous physical presence in the new place for at least one year": that is the exception. No minimum period is an element of a change of domicile.

H, a career diplomat, is posted abroad. W refuses to join him because she runs a business in Manila and the children study here. H petitions the court to require W to live with him abroad. Rule.

Answer: The court may exempt W from living with H, since H lives abroad and there are valid and compelling reasons (her business, the children's schooling), unless the exemption is not compatible with the solidarity of the family (FC 69 par. 2). The husband's choice does not prevail, and W cannot be compelled by contempt to cohabit. H's posting abroad does not change H's own domicile (Tolentino: a diplomat's assignment is no abandonment of his Philippine domicile).

B, born and raised in Lucena, studied and worked in Quezon City for eight years, renting a condominium there while keeping his voter registration, his family home and his business permit in Lucena, where he goes home most weekends. He runs for councilor in Lucena. Is he a resident?

Answer: Yes. Lucena is his domicile of origin. Absence to pursue studies or a calling does not constitute abandonment (Abella), and his acts (registration, home, permit, regular returns) show animus revertendi. Whoever claims abandonment must prove an actual removal, a bona fide intent to abandon, and corresponding definite acts (Romualdez-Marcos). Quezon City is merely a residence.

The Family Code: effectivity, retroactivity, repeal and amendments

Enactment and effectivity

  • EO 209 was issued by President Corazon C. Aquino on July 6, 1987, under her legislative powers before the Congress convened. It was drafted by the Civil Code Revision Committee (UP Law Center).
  • EO 227 (July 17, 1987) amended EO 209 before it took effect. Among its changes were the second paragraph of Art. 26 (a foreign divorce obtained by the alien spouse capacitates the Filipino spouse to remarry) and the texts of Arts. 36 and 39.
  • Effectivity: FC Art. 257: "This Code shall take effect one year after the completion of its publication in a newspaper of general circulation, as certified by the Executive Secretary, Office of the President." Publication was completed on August 4, 1987, and the Code took effect on August 3, 1988 (1988 being a leap year, the 365-day year ends a day "early") (Tolentino, citing Memorandum Circular No. 85 of the Executive Secretary).
  • For contrast, the Civil Code (RA 386) took effect on August 30, 1950 (Lara v. Del Rosario).
Note ("year"): The August 3, 1988 date counts a year as 365 days (NCC 13). In CIR v. Primetown (2007), the SC held that the Administrative Code of 1987 (12 calendar months to a year) impliedly repealed NCC 13 on "years." Counted by calendar months under Primetown, the date would be August 4, 1988. The effectivity date of the Family Code, however, is settled as August 3, 1988 (Memorandum Circular No. 85, Tolentino, and Uribe). Use that date, and if asked, explain how it was computed. The difference never changes the answer to the usual problems.

(Bar) The Family Code's publication was completed on August 4, 1987. On September 4, 1987, Junior and Gemma were married before a municipal mayor. Valid?

Answer: Yes. The Family Code was not yet in force (effective August 3, 1988). The Civil Code then governed, and it authorized mayors to solemnize marriages. The marriage is valid if the other requisites were present.

Retroactivity: FC 256

Art. 256, FC. This Code shall have retroactive effect insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws.
  • Rule: the Family Code is retroactive, an express exception to NCC 4 ("Laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided").
  • Limit: it cannot prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights under the Civil Code or other laws. A vested right is one that has become fixed and established and is no longer open to doubt or controversy, as opposed to a mere expectancy.
  • Validity of a marriage is determined by the law in force at the time of celebration. A pre-August 3, 1988 marriage is tested under the Civil Code impediments (e.g., NCC 80(7) step-siblings, void; NCC 80(6) killing a spouse requires a conviction). A marriage on or after that date is tested under the Family Code (Uribe: "it depends on when the marriage was celebrated").
  • Retroactive application illustrated: Art. 36 (psychological incapacity) applies to marriages celebrated before the Family Code, and since RA 8533 the action does not prescribe (Art. 39). Art. 40 (judicial declaration of nullity required for remarriage) applies to a remarriage contracted after August 3, 1988 even if the first marriage was earlier (Atienza v. Brillantes, 1995). Procedural rules apply to pending cases (cf. Pulido). Under FC 105 par. 2, the Family Code provisions on conjugal partnership apply to partnerships established before it, without prejudice to vested rights.
Atienza v. Brillantes, Jr. (A.M. No. MTJ-92-706, March 29, 1995)

Judge Brillantes married his first wife in 1965, before the Family Code, in a ceremony he later claimed lacked a marriage license. Without a judicial declaration of nullity, he married another woman in 1991, after the Family Code took effect. Charged administratively, he argued that the first marriage was void, so no judicial declaration was needed. Doctrine: Art. 40 applies to remarriages entered into after the effectivity of the Family Code, regardless of the date of the first marriage. Under Art. 256 it is given retroactive effect, since it does not prejudice vested rights; it is a procedural rule, and no one has a vested right in procedure.

Bernabe v. Alejo (G.R. No. 140500, January 21, 2002)

A child born in 1981, before the Family Code, sued after the father's death for recognition and a share in his estate. The defense invoked FC 172 and 175, which allow an illegitimate child to prove filiation after the father's death only in limited cases. Doctrine: Under NCC 285, the child had the right to sue for recognition within four years after attaining majority. That right had vested before the Family Code took effect, and FC 256 does not allow the Code to impair it. The action was timely.

Tayag v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 95229, June 9, 1992)

An action for recognition and partition was filed on behalf of a minor illegitimate child before the Family Code took effect. The defense argued that under FC 175 the action, based on open and continuous possession of status, should have been filed during the father's lifetime. Doctrine: The right of action vested when the complaint was filed under the Civil Code (which allowed the action within four years after the child's majority) and cannot be impaired by the Family Code's retroactive application.

Candelario v. Candelario (B29 repository)

A husband married before August 3, 1988 sued for nullity under Art. 36. Doctrine (per B29): Under Art. 256, the Family Code (including Art. 36) applies retroactively to marriages celebrated before its effectivity. If the respondent wife had a vested right that would be prejudiced, she should have raised it at the first opportunity, and she did not.

(Uribe situationer) A and B, with no marriage settlement, married on August 1, 1988. X died at 9 A.M. on the wedding day, leaving A a parcel of land by will. After solemnization A died. Does the land form part of A's estate? Which property regime governs?

Answer: The marriage preceded the Family Code (effective August 3, 1988), so the default regime is conjugal partnership of gains under the Civil Code (NCC 119), not absolute community. Uribe's key adds: "FC provisions will apply as long as vested rights are not impaired" (FC 256; FC 105 par. 2), so the CPG is administered and liquidated under the FC's CPG rules. Property acquired by gratuitous title (by will) is the acquiring spouse's exclusive property under CPG. Ownership vested in A at X's death (NCC 777), whether before or after the wedding, so the land forms part of A's estate as A's separate property. Uribe stresses the timing: under ACP "it does not matter when ownership was vested," but under CPG "it would matter."

Quiz traps:
  • "The Family Code shall have retroactive effect" (standing alone): FALSE, because "insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights" is missing.
  • "The Family Code took effect on August 4, 1988 / fifteen days after publication": FALSE. It took effect one year after the completion of publication in a newspaper of general circulation, on August 3, 1988.
  • "As of August 2, 1988, the following have authority to solemnize: ... city mayor" is TRUE, since the Civil Code still governed. For a date between August 3, 1988 and the effectivity of the LGC, mayors are excluded.
  • "Marriages between step-siblings are void": FALSE as a general statement. It was true under NCC 80(7), but the Family Code dropped this impediment, so validity depends on the date of celebration.

Last-minute checklist

  • Art. 37: juridical capacity is the fitness to be the subject of legal relations; inherent; lost only through death. Capacity to act is the power to do acts with legal effect; acquired and may be lost. Juridical capacity can exist without capacity to act, never the reverse.
  • Art. 38 (six): minority, insanity or imbecility, deaf-mute, prodigality, civil interdiction. They are "mere restrictions" and do not exempt from certain obligations (from his acts, from property relations such as easements).
  • Art. 39 (eleven, "among others"): age, insanity, imbecility, deaf-mute, penalty, prodigality, family relations, alienage, absence, insolvency, trusteeship. Not limited by religious belief or political opinion. In "except" MCQs, the answer is usually none of the above.
  • Art. 40: birth determines personality. The conceived child is considered born for all purposes favorable to it, provided it be born later under Art. 41. Its personality is limited and provisional or conditional.
  • Art. 41: born if alive at complete delivery (umbilical cord cut). If intra-uterine life is less than 7 months, it must not die within 24 hours. Count intra-uterine life, not months since the father's death. Viability is not required.
  • Conception period: the first 120 of the 300 days before birth. Const. Art. II Sec. 12 protects the unborn from conception. PD 603 Art. 5 is the same as Art. 40.
  • Geluz: no indemnity "for death" of an aborted foetus, and no derivative action by the parents. Parents get only moral damages for their own distress and exemplary damages. Quimiguing: the unborn has a right to support. De Jesus v. Syquia: the unborn may be acknowledged. Roe was overruled by Dobbs (2022).
  • Art. 42: extinguished by death (natural only; no civil death). Effect on rights is by law, contract and will. The estate continues personality (Limjoco). A dead person cannot be sued (Dumlao). The corpse is a thing, and its custody goes to the lawful spouse, then relatives (Eugenio v. Velez).
  • Art. 43: persons called to succeed each other. Whoever alleges prior death proves it. Absent proof, presumed (disputable) simultaneous death and no transmission. It applies whatever the cause or place of death, even presumed death.
  • Rule 131 Sec. 3(jj): except for succession; same calamity; no inference possible. Both under 15: older. Both over 60: younger. Under 15 vs. over 60: under 15. Both 15–60: male, or if same sex, older. Under 15 or over 60 vs. between: the one between 15 and 60. (kk) = Art. 43. Joaquin v. Navarro: facts beat presumptions.
  • Arts. 44–47: (1) State and subdivisions; (2) public-interest entities created by law, whose personality begins "as soon as constituted according to law"; (3) private corporations, partnerships and associations. Nos. 1–2 are governed by the laws creating them; private corporations by laws of general application; partnerships by the Code. Juridical persons may bring civil or criminal actions. Art. 47 applies only to No. 2 entities, whose assets go to similar purposes for the benefit of the region, province, city or municipality that derived the principal benefits.
  • Smith Bell: a corporation is a "person" under due process and equal protection, but the alien-registry law is valid police power. Barlin: the Roman Catholic Church is a juridical person.
  • Presumption of capacity: Standard Oil v. Arenas. Monomania of wealth does not equal incapacity, and capacity continues until the contrary is proved.
  • Majority = 18 (RA 6809, Dec. 13, 1989). Parental consent to marry until 21, advice 21–25. Vote at 18. Will at 18 (797). Witness to a will at 18 (820).
  • Minor's contracts: one party incapable means voidable (1390); both means unenforceable (1403(3)). The capable party cannot invoke the other's incapacity (1397). Restitution only to the extent benefited (1399). Necessaries: reasonable price (1489). Arts. 1426–1427 (18–21) are practically inoperative after RA 6809.
  • Estoppel: active misrepresentation of age estops (Mercado; Sia Suan; later disclosure "of no moment"). Mere silence does not (Bambalan; Braganza). Braganza: restitution to the extent profited; minority raised as a defense is not barred by the 4-year period. Young v. Tecson (CA) is the contrary view.
  • Criminal liability: RA 9344 as amended. 15 and below: exempt. Above 15 to below 18: exempt unless with discernment (then privileged mitigating). Civil liability remains. (Syllabus RPC 12(2)–(3): under 9; 9–15 unless discernment. RPC 13(2): under 18 mitigating.)
  • Insanity: voidable marriage (FC 45(2)); no consent to contracts (1327(2)), but a lucid interval is valid (1328); exempting (RPC 12(1)) unless lucid interval. Presumption of sanity; burden on the one alleging; the relevant time is the time of the act (Vaquilar, Rafanan; Paña three-way test, clear and convincing evidence).
  • Deaf-mute: cannot consent only if he does not know how to write (1327(2)). May make a will (807). Cannot witness a will (820).
  • Prodigality: limits capacity only once he is under guardianship (Rule 92 Sec. 2; Tolentino).
  • Civil interdiction: accessory to reclusión temporal and perpetua (RPC 41). Loses parental authority, guardianship, marital authority, management, and disposition inter vivos (RPC 34). May dispose by will and may inherit (not accept personally). FC 79, 135(1), 142(3).
  • Family relations (FC 150): spouses; parents and children; other ascendants and descendants; siblings, full or half blood. FC 87 (donations between spouses void, except moderate gifts on family rejoicing; also common-law partners). NCC 1490 (sale between spouses). FC 151 (earnest efforts at compromise).
  • Alienage: aliens cannot acquire land except by hereditary succession (Uribe: "own and acquire are different"). They can own other real property.
  • Absence: declaration after 2 yrs (5 if he left an administrator); effective 6 months after publication. Presumed dead after 7 yrs except for succession; 10 yrs for succession (5 if he disappeared after 75); 4 yrs in danger of death (all purposes). FC 41: 4 / 2 years for remarriage. A reappearing absentee gets no fruits or rents.
  • Trusteeship: NCC 1491 (fiduciaries cannot buy). NCC 1381(1)–(2) (lesion of more than one-fourth makes it rescissible).
  • Art. 49: naturalization, loss and reacquisition of citizenship are governed by special laws.
  • Art. 50: domicile of natural persons is the place of habitual (not actual) residence. Art. 51: juridical persons: as fixed by law, else where their legal representation is established or where they exercise their principal functions.
  • Domicile = physical presence + animus manendi; one at a time; kept until a new one is acquired. Change requires (1) actual removal, (2) bona fide intent to abandon the old and establish the new, (3) definite corresponding acts (Romualdez-Marcos). No minimum period. Marriage does not automatically change the wife's domicile of origin. Abella: absence for study or business is not abandonment; abandonment must be proved by preponderance; Larrazabal lived in Ormoc with her husband, so she was not a Kananga resident. Election law: residence = domicile. Venue: residence = actual residence.
  • FC 69: H and W fix the family domicile; if they disagree, the court decides (summary, FC 253). The court may exempt a spouse if the other lives abroad or for valid and compelling reasons, but not if incompatible with family solidarity. Old NCC 110: the husband fixed it.
  • Family Code: EO 209 (July 6, 1987), amended by EO 227. Published Aug. 4, 1987. Effective Aug. 3, 1988 (FC 257: one year after completion of publication in a newspaper of general circulation). Civil Code effective Aug. 30, 1950.
  • FC 254 repeals NCC Titles III, IV, V, VI, VIII, IX, XI, XV of Book I and listed PD 603 articles. NCC 1–51 survive. FC 255 separability. FC 253 summary proceedings for Arts. 41, 51, 69, 73, 96, 124, 217.
  • FC 256: retroactive insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights. Marriage validity is judged by the law at celebration. Art. 40 applies to post-FC remarriages (Atienza). Vested rights to sue for recognition are preserved (Bernabe v. Alejo; Tayag). Art. 36 applies to pre-FC marriages (Art. 39 per RA 8533).
  • RA 7160: mayors regained authority to solemnize (LGC Secs. 444, 455). Between Aug. 3, 1988 and the LGC's effectivity they had none.
