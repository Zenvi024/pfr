Civil Register

Civil Code, Title XVI, Arts. 407–413 (syllabus XXII), Rule 108 of the Rules of Court, and R.A. 9048 as amended by R.A. 10172. What must be registered; the evidentiary value of entries; and the three routes to change an entry: the civil registrar (clerical errors, first names), Rule 103 (change of name), and Rule 108 (cancellation or correction, summary or adversarial). Cases: Republic v. Sayo, Republic v. Valencia, Republic v. Marcos, Labayo-Rowe; from the class reviewer, Cagandahan, Braza, Magpayo, Olaybar, Gallo, Bartolome, Felix, Ontuca, Barcelote, Cua Ko.

What is registered (Arts. 407–411, 413)

Art. 407, NCC. Acts, events and judicial decrees concerning the civil status of persons shall be recorded in the civil register. (325a)
Art. 408, NCC. The following shall be entered in the civil register:
(1) Births; (2) marriages; (3) deaths; (4) legal separations; (5) annulments of marriage; (6) judgments declaring marriages void from the beginning; (7) legitimations; (8) adoptions; (9) acknowledgments of natural children; (10) naturalization; (11) loss, or (12) recovery of citizenship; (13) civil interdiction; (14) judicial determination of filiation; (15) voluntary emancipation of a minor; and (16) changes of name. (326a)
Art. 409, NCC. In cases of legal separation, adoption, naturalization and other judicial orders mentioned in the preceding article, it shall be the duty of the clerk of the court which issued the decree to ascertain whether the same has been registered, and if this has not been done, to send a copy of said decree to the civil registry of the city or municipality where the court is functioning. (n)
Art. 410, NCC. The books making up the civil register and all documents relating thereto shall be considered public documents and shall be prima facie evidence of the facts therein contained. (n)
Art. 411, NCC. Every civil registrar shall be civilly responsible for any unauthorized alteration made in any civil register, to any person suffering damage thereby. However, the civil registrar may exempt himself from such liability if he proves that he has taken every reasonable precaution to prevent the unlawful alteration. (n)
Art. 413, NCC. All other matters pertaining to the registration of civil status shall be governed by special laws. (n)
Evidentiary value (Art. 410): entries are public documents and prima facie evidence only. A birth certificate naming a father who did not sign is not competent proof of paternity (Reyes; Vda. de Alberto: see Proof of Filiation). And the status of a child is what the law says, not what the record says: "A child born during a valid marriage is a legitimate child by law. Not even the birth certificate of the child can change her status for the information contained therein are merely supplied by the parents" (Cua Ko v. Republic, class reviewer). Likewise, the mother's declaration against legitimacy does not affect it (Art. 167; Ordoña).
Barcelote v. Republic (G.R. No. 222095, August 7, 2017; class reviewer; see also Paternity)

Jonna Barcelote's two children by a married man were registered by him, without her knowledge or signature, under his surname. Held: the birth certificates are void and cancelled: they violated the FC rule that illegitimate children use the mother's surname and the Civil Registry Law requiring the mother's signature. "It is mandatory that the mother of an illegitimate child signs the birth certificate of her child in all cases, irrespective of whether the father recognizes the child as his or not."

Changing an entry (Art. 412; Rule 108)

Art. 412, NCC. No entry in a civil register shall be changed or corrected, without a judicial order. (n)
Rule 108, Secs. 1–7, Rules of Court (summary). Any person interested in an act, event, order or decree concerning civil status recorded in the civil register may file a verified petition with the RTC of the province where the civil registry is located (Sec. 1), for the cancellation or correction of the entries listed in Sec. 2. The civil registrar and all persons who have or claim any interest which would be affected shall be made parties (Sec. 3). The court fixes the hearing, gives reasonable notice to the persons named, and publishes the order once a week for three consecutive weeks in a newspaper of general circulation in the province (Sec. 4). The civil registrar and any interested person may oppose within 15 days from notice or the last publication (Sec. 5). The court may expedite and issue a preliminary injunction (Sec. 6). After hearing, it dismisses or grants; a certified copy of the judgment is served on the civil registrar, who annotates it (Sec. 7).
Republic v. Valencia (141 SCRA 462, 1986)

Leonor Valencia, a Filipino, petitioned under Rule 108 to correct the birth records of her two children by Go Eng, a Chinese national: their citizenship from "Chinese" to "Filipino" and their parents' status from "married" to "not married", on the ground that she and Go Eng were never married. The Republic argued that such substantial changes cannot be made under Rule 108. Held: the petition may proceed. "It is undoubtedly true that if the subject matter of a petition is not for the correction of clerical errors of a harmless and innocuous nature, but one involving nationality or citizenship, which is indisputably substantial as well as controverted, affirmative relief cannot be granted in a proceeding summary in nature. However, it is also true that a right in law may be enforced and a wrong may be remedied as long as the appropriate remedy is used. This Court adheres to the principle that even substantial errors in a civil registry may be corrected and the true facts established provided the parties aggrieved by the error avail themselves of the appropriate adversary proceeding." Here the petition was published, the registrar and Go Eng were notified, and the Solicitor General opposed: the proceeding was adversarial. "A proceeding under Rule 108 is not necessarily a summary proceeding" (class reviewer).

Labayo-Rowe v. Republic (168 SCRA 294, 1988)

Emperatriz Labayo-Rowe petitioned to correct the birth records of her two children: her first name from "Beatriz" to "Emperatriz", and the entry on the parents' marriage (date and place) to "not married", which would make the children illegitimate. Only the local civil registrar was notified. Held: only the correction of the misspelled name, a clerical error, may be allowed. "A mere petition for correction of entries under Rule 108 of the ROC cannot change a child's status from legitimate to illegitimate. Rule 108 'shall not diminish, increase or modify substantive rights.' If Rule 108 were to be extended beyond innocuous or harmless changes or corrections of errors which are visible to the eye or obvious to the understanding, so as to comprehend substantial and controversial alterations concerning citizenship, legitimacy of paternity or filiation, or legitimacy of marriage, without observing the proper proceedings …, said rule would thereby become an unconstitutional exercise which would tend to increase or modify substantive rights."

Republic v. Marcos (182 SCRA 223, 1990)

A petition sought substantial alterations in a birth record (including the child's citizenship and filiation) in proceedings that did not implead or notify all who would be affected. Held: not allowed as filed. "When a petition for cancellation or correction of an entry in the civil register involves substantial and controversial alterations, including those on citizenship, legitimacy of paternity or filiation, or legitimacy of marriage, a strict compliance with the requirements of Rule 108 of the Rules of Court is mandated. If the entries in the civil register could be corrected or changed through mere summary proceedings and not through appropriate action wherein all parties who may be affected by the entries are notified or represented, the door to fraud or other mischief would be set open." (The class reviewer also cites it for the rule that a change of name should not give a false impression of family relationship.)

Republic v. Sayo (188 SCRA 634, 1990)

A petition for correction involving a person's citizenship was granted in what amounted to a summary proceeding. Held: "If the subject matter of a petition is not for the correction of clerical errors of a harmless and innocuous nature, but one involving nationality or citizenship, which is indisputably substantial as well as controverted, affirmative relief cannot be granted in a proceeding summary in nature." (Your sources give only this doctrine.)

Tolentino (Art. 412): a clerical error is "visible to the eyes or obvious to the understanding; an error made by a clerk or transcriber; a mistake in copying or writing, or some harmless or innocuous change, such as correction of a name that is clearly misspelled or of a misstatement of the occupation of the parents." Substantial changes (citizenship, civil status, sex, paternity and filiation) need the adversary procedure: the civil registrar and all who claim an interest that would be affected must be parties, with notice and publication, so that "all relevant facts are fully and properly developed, opposing counsel are given the opportunity to demolish each other's case, and the evidence is thoroughly considered and weighed." A false entry naming the petitioner as a child's father, made by an interloper without authority, may be ordered struck out and replaced with "unknown" (Alisoso v. Lastimoso).

Administrative correction (R.A. 9048 as amended by R.A. 10172)

Sec. 1, R.A. 9048 (as amended by R.A. 10172). Authority to Correct Clerical or Typographical Error and Change of First Name or Nickname. – No entry in a civil register shall be changed or corrected without a judicial order, except for clerical or typographical errors and change of first name or nickname, the day and month in the date of birth or sex of a person where it is patently clear that there was a clerical or typographical error or mistake in the entry, which can be corrected or changed by the concerned city or municipal civil registrar or consul general in accordance with the provisions of this Act and its implementing rules and regulations.
Sec. 2(3), R.A. 9048 (as amended). "Clerical or typographical error" refers to a mistake committed in the performance of clerical work in writing, copying, transcribing or typing an entry in the civil register that is harmless and innocuous, such as misspelled name or misspelled place of birth, mistake in the entry of day and month in the date of birth or the sex of the person or the like, which is visible to the eyes or obvious to the understanding, and can be corrected or changed only by reference to other existing record or records: Provided, however, That no correction must involve the change of nationality, age, or status of the petitioner.
Sec. 4, R.A. 9048 (grounds for change of first name or nickname). (1) The petitioner finds the first name or nickname to be ridiculous, tainted with dishonor or extremely difficult to write or pronounce; (2) the new first name or nickname has been habitually and continuously used by the petitioner and he has been publicly known by that first name or nickname in the community; or (3) the change will avoid confusion.
Republic v. Cagandahan (G.R. No. 166676, September 12, 2008; class reviewer)

Jennifer Cagandahan, registered as female, had congenital adrenal hyperplasia (intersex anatomy), produced high levels of male hormones, and had matured with male features; she petitioned under Rule 108 to change her name to "Jeff" and her sex to "male". Held: granted. A change of sex is a substantial change under Rule 108, and procedure was substantially complied with. Where a person is biologically or naturally intersex, "the determining factor in gender classification for an individual who has reached the age of majority is what the individual, with good reason, thinks of his/her sex", respecting the person's choice about sexual development. (Contrast Silverio v. Republic, G.R. No. 174689, October 22, 2007, not in your sources: no law allows changing the sex entry on the basis of sex reassignment surgery; the first-name change was likewise denied.)

Braza v. City Civil Registrar of Himamaylan (G.R. No. 181174, December 4, 2009; class reviewer)

The widow and legitimate children of Pablo Braza petitioned under Rule 108 to correct the birth record of Patrick, recorded as Pablo's legitimated son by Lucille Titular, alleging that Pablo's marriage to Lucille was bigamous. Held: dismissed. The petition was really a collateral attack on the validity of a marriage and on legitimation and filiation, which must be raised in a direct action by the proper party (FC 170–171), not through correction of entries. "Substantial or contentious alterations may be allowed only in adversarial proceedings, in which all interested parties are impleaded and due process is properly observed."

Republic v. Olaybar (G.R. No. 189538, February 10, 2014; class reviewer)

Merlinda Olaybar learned from her CENOMAR that she was "married" to a Korean, though she had never married; her identity had apparently been used by a travel agent. She sought cancellation of the "wife" entries in the marriage certificate under Rule 108. The OSG argued she was seeking a declaration of nullity in disguise. Held: Rule 108 is proper. Cancelling the wife's entries does not declare a marriage void, since there was no marriage to speak of; and all parties had the chance to contest. "As long as the procedural requirements in Rule 108 are followed, it is the appropriate adversary proceeding to effect substantial corrections and changes in entries of the civil register."

Republic v. Magpayo (G.R. No. 189476, February 2, 2011; class reviewer)

Julian Coseteng Magpayo, claiming his parents were never married, filed a Rule 103 petition in Quezon City to change his name, which the RTC granted by ordering the Makati civil registrar to delete the parents' marriage date and his father's name. Held: void. Changing one's status from legitimate to illegitimate is not a mere change of name under Rule 103. It requires a Rule 108 petition filed where the registry is (Makati), with the civil registrar and all affected persons (including the parents) impleaded.

Republic v. Ontuca (2020; class reviewer)

A mother sought, under Rule 108, to correct her misspelled names in her daughter's birth record and to change her civil status there from "married" to "not married". Held: the name corrections stand (the RTC may resolve clerical errors to avoid multiplicity of suits), but the marital-status change is void: it affects the child's legitimacy, and the father and the child, indispensable parties, were not impleaded. Substantial errors are those that "establish or affect the substantive rights of a person (civil status, citizenship, nationality, legitimacy or marriage/filiation)."

Republic v. Gallo (G.R. No. 207074, January 17, 2018; class reviewer)

Michelle Soriano Gallo, recorded as "Michael", "male", with no middle name and no parents' marriage date, sought correction under Rule 108. Held: proper under Rule 108, not Rule 103: she was not changing her name but correcting an obvious misspelling (she had always been Michelle), and the other corrections were clerical. A clerical error is one "visible to the eyes or obvious to the understanding, and can be corrected or changed only by reference to other existing record or records."

Bartolome v. Republic (G.R. No. 243288, August 28, 2019; class reviewer)

Ruben Cruz Bartolome, recorded as "Feliciano Bartholome", went to court under Rule 103 to change his name. Held: denied without prejudice. R.A. 9048 governs changes of first name and clerical corrections (including inserting a middle name and correcting a misspelled surname); "typographical or clerical errors in a person's surname must likewise be corrected through the administrative proceeding under R.A. 9048." Judicial remedies are available only after the administrative petition is denied.

Republic v. Felix (2021; class reviewer)

A man with two birth records, one with errors, filed a Rule 108 petition to correct one and cancel the other; the Republic argued the RTC had no jurisdiction because R.A. 9048 covers clerical errors. Held: the RTC had jurisdiction. R.A. 9048 "did not divest RTCs of their jurisdiction to hear and decide petitions for correction of entries"; failure to exhaust the administrative remedy does not affect the court's jurisdiction.

Quiz traps (civil register): "No entry in a civil register shall be changed or corrected without a judicial order" → incomplete today: true as Art. 412 reads, but R.A. 9048/10172 allow administrative correction of clerical errors, first names, the day and month of birth and sex. "The year of birth may be corrected administratively" → false (age). "A Rule 108 proceeding is always summary" → false (Valencia). "Legitimacy may be changed by a petition naming only the civil registrar" → false (Labayo-Rowe; Ontuca). "Entries in the civil register are conclusive" → false: prima facie (Art. 410). "The civil registrar is absolutely liable for unauthorized alterations" → false: he may exempt himself by proving every reasonable precaution (Art. 411). "Rule 108 publication: once a week for two weeks" → false: three consecutive weeks.

Bar-style: Ana's birth certificate lists her as "Anna", born "June 5, 1998", "male", and as the legitimate daughter of spouses Jose and Maria, who in fact never married. What remedies apply to each error?

"Anna" → "Ana" (misspelling) and "male" → "female" (patent clerical error, with a medical certification of no sex change): administrative petition with the local civil registrar under R.A. 9048 as amended by R.A. 10172. A wrong day and month of birth would also go there, but not the year. The parents' marital status (which changes her legitimacy): only through an adversarial Rule 108 proceeding in the RTC of the province where the registry is located, impleading the civil registrar and all affected persons (both parents, and anyone whose rights would be affected), with publication for three consecutive weeks (Labayo-Rowe; Ontuca; Magpayo). If the claim is really an attack on a marriage's validity or on legitimacy, it must be a direct action by the proper party (Braza).
