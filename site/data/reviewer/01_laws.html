I. Introduction to Civil Law (Tolentino pp. 1–17)

Concepts of law

  • General or abstract sense (derecho): "the science of moral rules, founded on the rational nature of man, which govern his free activity, for the realization of the individual and social ends, of a nature both demandable and reciprocal." In short, it is the mass of obligatory rules established to govern the relations of persons in society.
  • Specific or material sense (ley): "a juridical proposition or an aggregate of juridical propositions, promulgated and published by the competent organs of the State in accordance with the Constitution." It is a norm of human conduct in social life, established by a sovereign organization and imposed for the compulsory observance of all. Sánchez Román: "a rule of conduct, just, obligatory, promulgated by legitimate authority, and of common observance and benefit."
  • Foundations: law is a product of social life and a creation of human nature, intended by man to serve man. It rests on order, co-existence and liberty.
  • Characteristics (4): (1) a rule of human conduct; (2) promulgated by competent authority; (3) obligatory; (4) of general observance.

Law and morals

  • Both spring from the same source, the social conscience, and share the same purpose (happiness). Only law has a legal sanction enforceable by public authority.
  • Morals cover a wider field: duties to oneself and to God, not just to others. Law covers only social activities. Conduct may conform to law yet be immoral, and vice versa. Colin & Capitant compare them to two concentric circles. Tolentino says they are more accurately two intersecting circles, with a common zone and some principles at variance.
  • Not all moral duties are made juridical; otherwise morals would lose their essential voluntary character. (This idea returns in Arts. 19–21, Human Relations.)

Divisions of law

  • Divine law (God is the legislator) vs. human law (promulgated by man to regulate human relations).
  • Human law is either general or public law: international, constitutional, administrative, criminal, religious. Or it is individual or private law: civil (relations of individuals with other individuals for purely private ends), mercantile (special relations produced by commercial transactions), procedural (the means by which private rights may be enforced).
  • Kinds of specific law: mandatory (commands something be done), prohibitory (commands something not be done), permissive (commands that what it permits be tolerated or respected). Savigny and others criticize this classification. The "more important" classification is absolute (obligatory character) vs. suppletory (applies only when the will of the individual has not manifested itself). This matters for Art. 5: only mandatory and prohibitory laws are covered, not directory or suppletory ones.

Codification

  • A code is a collection of laws of the same kind, i.e., a body of legal provisions on a particular branch of law. David Dudley Field described codification as: "To reduce the bulk, clear out the refuse, condense and arrange the residuum..."
  • Reasons: (1) to simplify and arrange juridical rules scattered in several laws and customs; (2) to unify legislation in the same country; (3) to introduce reforms occasioned by social change.
  • Modern codification was started by Napoleon (Code Napoléon, 1804). Drafting took 3 years for the French code, 14 years for the first German draft, 8 years each for the Swiss and Chilean codes, and 5 years for the Argentine code. The Philippine Civil Code was completed in seven months.
  • Spain: Fuero Real (Alfonso X, 1255); Siete Partidas (via Ordenamiento de Alcalá, 1384); Leyes de Toro; Nueva and Novísima Recopilación (1805); Constitution of 1812 called for a single Civil Code; Proyecto of 1851 never approved; Ley de Bases (May 11, 1888); the Spanish Civil Code took final form on July 24, 1889.
  • Spanish Civil Code of 1889 in the Philippines: extended by Royal Decree of July 31, 1889, published in the Gaceta de Manila on November 17, 1889. Per Tolentino it took effect December 7, 1889, applying the rule that the first day is excluded and the last included (some cases say December 8).
  • Philippine codification: Pres. Quezon created a Code Committee in 1940 (headed by CJ Avanceña). Its records were destroyed in the battle for the liberation of Manila in 1945. Pres. Roxas created a new Code Commission by E.O. No. 48 (March 20, 1947): Jorge Bocobo (chair), Guillermo Guevara, Pedro Ylagan, Francisco Capistrano; Arturo Tolentino was appointed fifth member on February 29, 1948, resigned in 1949 upon his election to the House of Representatives, and was replaced by Carmelino Alvendia. Work began May 8, 1947. The final draft was completed December 15, 1947, submitted to Congress in January 1948, and approved on June 18, 1949 as R.A. No. 386. It took effect on August 30, 1950 (Lara v. Del Rosario; Tolentino).

Civil law: definition and sources

  • In Roman law, "civil law" had four senses: (1) the national law applicable to the individuals of each city; (2) the body of law (plebiscites, imperial constitutions) as distinguished from the jus honorarium/pretorium; (3) the law applicable to Roman citizens as distinguished from the jus gentium applicable to foreigners; (4) the opinions of authorized jurisconsults.
  • Present concept: the branch of law whose double purpose is the organization of the family and the regulation of property. It is "the mass of precepts which determine and regulate the relations of assistance, authority and obedience among the members of a family, and those which exist among members of a society for the protection of private interests."
  • Immediate sources of Philippine civil law: (1) the Civil Code; (2) special statutes (Copyright, Patent, Law of Waters, labor and social legislation). Indirect or remote sources: the Spanish and American laws from which ours were copied. Anglo-American common law is not in force here, but its doctrines may be applied when founded on sound principles applicable to local conditions and not in conflict with existing law. Post-independence American decisions are persuasive only.
  • Sources of the Civil Code itself (Code Commission): (1) Civil Code of 1889; (2) codes, laws, decisions and works of jurists of other countries (Spain, US states especially California and Louisiana, France, Argentina, Germany, Mexico, Switzerland, England, Italy); (3) Philippine Supreme Court doctrines; (4) Filipino customs and traditions; (5) Philippine statutes (Marriage Law Act 3613, Divorce Law Act 2710, Code of Civil Procedure, Rules of Court); (6) the Code Commission's own amendments and new rules.
  • Arrangement: four books, the same as the 1889 Code. Book I Persons; Book II Property, Ownership and its Modifications; Book III Different Modes of Acquiring Ownership; Book IV Obligations and Contracts. New: Human Relations (Preliminary Title), property relations of spouses moved to Book I, prescription moved to Book III, and many new rights (e.g., Arts. 21, 29, 32, 33). Omitted: dowry, censos, use and habitation.
  • Language: the Code was enacted in English, so the English text prevails over any translation. However, Spanish legal terms translated into English (bienes inmuebles = real property, pago = payment, etc.) must be understood in the light of Spanish-Philippine law, not Anglo-American law.
  • Civil vs. commercial law: Muñoz and Vidari doubt that complete unification is possible. The Civil Code is a partial step: it repealed many Code of Commerce provisions and unified the law on sales, partnership, agency, deposit and guaranty.
Note: Balane's "The Spanish Antecedents of the Philippine Civil Code" (54 PLJ) is on the syllabus, but no text of it was in the review materials. The Spanish codification history above comes from Tolentino. If Uribe asks, the thrust is the same: our Civil Code descends from Roman law through the Spanish Code of 1889, as modified by American influence and Filipino customs.

The rule broken into its elements

  1. Publication is indispensable. No law, whatever its effectivity clause, takes effect without publication (Tañada v. Tuvera, 1986 Resolution; Nagkakaisang Maralita). Publication is the basis of the conclusive presumption that everyone knows the law (Art. 3). It provides constructive notice and satisfies due process.
  2. Where: either the Official Gazette or a newspaper of general circulation in the Philippines (E.O. 200). One is enough. If the law itself requires both, both are needed: that falls under "unless it is otherwise provided."
  3. What: the full text must be published. A summary, or an "incorporation by reference" of an unpublished document, does not suffice (Tañada 1986; see the 2016 Bar item on P.D. 755 below).
  4. When it takes effect: after fifteen days following the completion of publication. Under Uribe's computation this is the 16th day: exclude the day of publication, count 15 days, and the law takes effect on the next day.
  5. "Unless it is otherwise provided" refers only to the fifteen-day period, never to publication. The legislature may shorten the period, lengthen it (the Civil Code: one year; the Family Code: one year), or make the law effective "immediately upon publication". It cannot dispense with publication.
  6. Date of publication means the date the issue is actually released and made available to the public, not the date printed on the Official Gazette issue (Veridiano; Pesigan).

Cases

Pesigan v. Angeles (129 SCRA 174, April 30, 1984)

The Pesigans were transporting carabaos interprovincially on April 2, 1982, with health certificates and permits from local officials. The animals were confiscated under E.O. 626-A, which banned interprovincial transport of carabaos and provided for confiscation. E.O. 626-A was published in the Official Gazette issue dated June 14, 1982, more than two months later. Doctrine: E.O. 626-A could not be enforced against the Pesigans on April 2, 1982. It is a penal regulation (it has a confiscation and forfeiture sanction), and it became effective only fifteen days after publication (Art. 2; Sec. 11, Revised Administrative Code). The word "laws" in Art. 2 includes circulars and regulations that prescribe penalties. Publication is necessary to apprise the public of the contents of the regulations and make the penalties binding. Under C.A. 638, "every order or document which shall prescribe a penalty shall be deemed to have general applicability and legal effect."

Uribe (recitation on Pesigan): He drills the difference between the issue date printed on the OG ("the OG of June 14, 1982") and the date of publication ("on June 14"). Is the printed date necessarily the publication date? "Not necessarily" is the right answer. A law is published when it is made accessible to the public, not merely when it is printed, and printing necessarily comes before publication. He will not ask whether you read the case. He goes straight to the issue: "W/N E.O. 626-A is enforceable even before its publication in the OG of June 14, 1982."
People v. Veridiano (132 SCRA 523, October 12, 1984)

B.P. 22 appeared in the Official Gazette issue dated April 9, 1979, but that issue was actually released to the public only on June 14, 1979 (the OG copy editor so certified). The accused issued the check before B.P. 22 took effect. Doctrine: The date of actual release to the public, not the issue date, governs. A law is deemed published when it is made available to the public. B.P. 22 itself provides that it takes effect fifteen days after publication, so it took effect on June 29, 1979. The offense punished is the issuance of the worthless check, so a check issued before the law took effect is not covered.

Uribe (recitation on Veridiano): "It was published again on June 14, 1979, therefore it took effect 15 days after publication, so June 30. Correct?" NO, June 29. B.P. 22 has its own effectivity clause ("15 days after"), which falls under "unless it is otherwise provided". It does not follow the general "after fifteen days following" rule. He also asked: if the Court had ruled that the dishonor (not the issuance) is what is punished, would the result be the same? No, because the dishonor fell after the effectivity of B.P. 22. Frame issues as "whether or not" to fairly capture both parties' sides ("you formulate the issue based on the petitioner's claim"). Do not treat as a fact something that is itself the issue.
Tañada v. Tuvera (136 SCRA 27, April 24, 1985; Resolution on MR, 146 SCRA 446, December 29, 1986)

Petitioners sought to compel publication in the OG of various presidential decrees, LOIs, proclamations and executive orders. Doctrine (1985): The "shall" in C.A. 638 imposes an imperative duty to publish, which gives substance to the people's right to information on matters of public concern. Presidential issuances "of a public nature" or "of general applicability" must be published. Unpublished issuances of general application have no force and effect. Issuances that apply only to particular persons or a class of persons need not be published, on the assumption that they have been circularized to all concerned. Implementation before publication is an operative fact whose consequences cannot always be ignored, so absolute retroactive invalidity is not justified. Doctrine (1986 Resolution): "Laws" in Art. 2 means all laws. Publication is indispensable in every case, and the legislature may only shorten or extend the 15-day period. All statutes, including those of local application and private laws, must be published. The publication must be in full, or it is no publication at all. It had to be in the Official Gazette, the law then in force; E.O. 200 later allowed a newspaper of general circulation. The exempted classes are those in the table above.

Uribe: "Secret PDs." During martial law, people were arrested or tortured under decrees that were printed but never really published: "METROCOM lang ang may alam sa batas" (only METROCOM knew the law). That is why publication is indispensable.
Tolentino and the B29 commentary: E.O. 200 was issued in response to the limited readership of the Official Gazette that the Court recognized in Tañada. Publication satisfies the basic requirements of due process. Administrative regulations that carry existing statutes into effect and impose obligations on the public acquire force only upon proper publication and filing with the ONAR (cf. PASEI v. Torres; Garcillano v. House Committees: rules of procedure for legislative inquiries must be published; publication is done in print, not merely online).
MRCA, Inc. v. Court of Appeals (180 SCRA 344, December 19, 1989)

Doctrine: Publication in the Official Gazette is not a prerequisite for the effectivity of a court ruling, even one that lays down a new rule of procedure. (Art. 2 covers "laws". Under Admin. Code Sec. 24, decisions are published in the OG only when the courts deem them of sufficient importance, and that publication is not a condition of effectivity.) The syllabus lists MRCA again under Retroactivity ("supra"). The point there is that a ruling laying down a procedural rule binds without publication and is governed by the rules on procedural laws. Uribe's doctrines outline gives only the publication holding.

Yaokasin v. Commissioner of Customs (180 SCRA 591, December 22, 1989)

Doctrine: Art. 2 does not apply to Customs Memorandum Order No. 20-87, an administrative order of the Commissioner of Customs addressed to his subordinates, the customs collectors, requiring strict compliance with Sec. 12 of the Plan. C.A. 638 requires publication of executive and administrative orders "except such as have no general applicability." An issuance addressed only to particular persons or a class of persons need not be published, on the assumption that it has been circularized to all concerned. Uribe's outline: "if a memo is directed to a specific person, no need to publish. This presumes that there is no general applicability of the memo."

Commissioner of Customs v. Hypermix Feeds Corp. (G.R. No. 179579, February 1, 2012)

A Customs Memorandum Order reclassified imported wheat by importer and port of discharge, imposing higher tariffs on Hypermix's feed-grade wheat. It was issued without notice and hearing, and without filing with the U.P. Law Center (ONAR). Doctrine: An administrative rule that is merely interpretative needs nothing more than its bare issuance, because it gives no real consequence beyond what the law already prescribes. But when the rule goes beyond providing the means to facilitate the law's implementation and substantially increases the burden of those governed, the agency must give at least those directly affected a chance to be heard, and then duly inform them (publish and file), before the issuance has the force and effect of law.

Nagkakaisang Maralita ng Sitio Masigasig, Inc. v. Military Shrine Services - PVAO (G.R. No. 187587, June 5, 2013)

Proclamation No. 423 (Pres. Garcia) reserved Fort Bonifacio as a military reservation. Proclamation No. 2476 (Pres. Marcos) excluded certain areas. A handwritten note on 2476 purportedly included Western Bicutan in the exclusion, but the note was not part of the proclamation as published. Doctrine: Under Art. 2, publication is indispensable to give effect to a law. "Unless otherwise provided" refers to an effectivity date different from the generic fifteen days. It does not dispense with publication (settled in Tañada). The Court cannot rely on a handwritten note that was not part of the proclamation as published. Without publication, the note never had legal force and effect.

Uribe (from Nagkakaisang Maralita): He jumps to the vices of consent: mistake, fraud, undue influence, violence, intimidation. Why is the "mistake" vice a mistake of fact and not of law? Because of Art. 3: ignorance of the law excuses no one. (He joked that Marcos was a "fortune-teller" because the area excluded later became the Libingan ng mga Bayani where he is buried.)

Bar and Uribe problems on Art. 2

(1990 Bar; Uribe guide question.) A special relief law provides that it "shall take effect upon approval and after completion of publication in the Official Gazette and a newspaper of general circulation." It was passed July 1, 1990, signed July 3, published in the newspaper July 7 and in the OG July 10. (a) Was publication sufficient? (b) When did it take effect? (c) Can funds be released the day after approval?

Answer: (a) Yes. The law itself required publication in both the OG and a newspaper ("unless it is otherwise provided"), and both were done. (b) It took effect on July 10, 1990, upon completion of the last required publication. It said "upon... completion of publication", so there is no 15-day waiting period. (c) No. On July 4 the law was not yet published and had no force. Publication is indispensable even if the law says "upon approval" (Tañada).

(Uribe Quiz 1, 2025.) A law silent as to effectivity was published in the OG on January 20, 2020. It is effective on: (a) Feb. 4 (b) Feb. 5 (c) Feb. 6 (d) Feb. 20 (e) Feb. 21, 2020.

(Uribe old Quiz 1.) A law silent as to effectivity was published in the OG on February 15, 2016. Effective on: (a) Feb. 15 (b) Mar. 1 (c) Mar. 2 (d) Mar. 3 (e) Mar. 4, 2016.

Answer: (c) March 2, 2016. 2016 is a leap year. Feb. 16–29 is 14 days, so Mar. 1 is the 15th day and Mar. 2 the 16th. (The same method makes a law published Jan. 5, 2016 effective Jan. 21, 2016.)

A law states: "This Act shall take effect on January 1, 2026." It was published in a newspaper of general circulation only on January 10, 2026. When did it take effect?

Answer: Not on January 1. Publication is indispensable, and "unless otherwise provided" refers only to the period. The fixed date had already passed when publication was completed, so the law could take effect no earlier than completion of publication on January 10, 2026. (Some argue that once the stated date fails, the default rule applies, which gives January 26, 2026. Either way, the answer is not January 1.) (Tañada; Uribe Quiz item: "If the law states the specific date for its effectivity, then it shall take effect on such date" is FALSE).

(2016 Bar.) P.D. 755 declared a policy and stated that the "Agreement for the Acquisition of a Commercial Bank for the Benefit of the Coconut Farmers" is "incorporated by reference." The P.D. was published, but the Agreement's text was neither published nor attached. Does the Agreement have the force of law?

Answer: No. Publication of the full text is indispensable. A document merely incorporated by reference, neither published nor annexed, cannot be accorded the status of a law (Cojuangco, Jr. v. Republic, 2012; cf. Nagkakaisang Maralita, unpublished handwritten note).

Which need NOT be published: (a) a P.D. of general application; (b) a letter of instructions by an administrative superior to subordinates on guidelines for their duties; (c) a statute of purely local application; (d) an administrative regulation implementing a statute and imposing penalties; (e) none.

Answer: (b). Tañada (1986) expressly exempts internal regulations and LOIs to subordinates. Statutes of local application must be published. Penal regulations must be published (Pesigan).

Quiz traps (Art. 2): (1) "...publication in the Official Gazette and in a newspaper..." is FALSE: it is either/or. (2) "Laws shall take effect fifteen days after their complete publication in the Official Gazette..." is FALSE: it should be "after fifteen days following", and it omits the newspaper. (3) "If the law states a specific date, it takes effect on such date" is FALSE: publication is still required. (4) "Unless otherwise provided" allows dispensing with publication: FALSE. II.B. Ignorance of the law (Art. 3)

Art. 3, NCC. Ignorance of the law excuses no one from compliance therewith. (2)
Art. 526, NCC. He is deemed a possessor in good faith who is not aware that there exists in his title or mode of acquisition any flaw which invalidates it.
He is deemed a possessor in bad faith who possesses in any case contrary to the foregoing.
Mistake upon a doubtful or difficult question of law may be the basis of good faith.

Rule, basis and scope

  • Conclusive presumption of knowledge of the law (ignorantia legis neminem excusat). Tolentino calls it "very far from reality", but it is necessary because of the obligatory force of law. Once a law is promulgated and effective, everyone has a duty to know it, and no one escapes its effects by alleging, in good or bad faith, that he did not know it.
  • Founded not only on expediency and policy but on necessity (Zulueta v. Zulueta, 1 Phil. 254). This is a favorite Uribe MCQ: "This rule is based on a presumption established because of the obligatory force of law. It is dictated not only by expediency but also by necessity." Answer: Ignorantia legis non excusat, not stare decisis, in pari delicto, estoppel or dura lex.
  • Reasons (Tolentino): (1) if laws bound only those who actually know them, social life would be impossible; (2) it is absurd to absolve those who do not know the law and increase the obligations of those who do; (3) it is almost impossible to prove the contrary when a person claims ignorance; (4) conscience carries norms of right and wrong, and in complicated matters there are lawyers to consult.
  • Laws covered: only Philippine laws, and among local laws only mandatory and prohibitory laws, not merely permissive ones. Foreign laws are not presumed known. Courts cannot take judicial notice of them; they must be alleged and proved. Ignorance of a foreign law is therefore a mistake of fact, not of law (Tolentino; cf. processual presumption under Art. 15–16 below).
  • Application: injuries suffered by contracting parties because of their ignorance of the law are borne by them. Ignorance of the law does not favor or prejudice anyone, nor does it justify the amendment or annulment of a contract (e.g., ignorance of the Usury Law, of an election residence law, or of the procedural law on appeals). An honest belief that one's divorce was valid is no defense to bigamy, and neither is advice of counsel.
  • No exceptions admitted in principle: not even interrupted communications (flood, war) where the OG did not reach a region (contra Toullier and Goyena). The rule is based on public interest. But an Argentine decision admitted ignorance equitably to show good faith when new provisions substantially different from former ones are involved, and our CA held the rule should not apply with equal force to minors (Tolentino).
  • Irrevocability of acts: if, through ignorance of law, a person does something prejudicial to himself and it cannot be remedied without impairing another's rights, he cannot correct it to the latter's prejudice. Example: a teacher who resigned upon marriage under a school regulation cannot rescind the resignation upon learning that an earlier law had nullified the regulation.

Mistake of fact vs. mistake of law; difficult questions of law

  • Ignorance of fact (ignorantia facti) may excuse; ignorance of law does not. If an heir does not know his father died, he is ignorant of a fact. If he knows of the death and of the relationship but not that rights vested in him, he is ignorant of the law.
  • Tolentino's bigamy illustrations: a Moro woman divorced under Mohammedan rites (then void) who remarried believing the divorce valid was guilty of bigamy (mistake of law). A man who, after the revolution, could not find his wife, believed on well-founded grounds that she was dead, and remarried was acquitted (mistake of fact) (People v. Bitdu; U.S. v. Enriquez).
  • Difficult questions of law are given the same effect as a mistake of fact in specific instances: Art. 526 par. 3 (possession in good faith), Art. 1334 (mutual error as to legal effect of an agreement may vitiate consent), Art. 2154 (solutio indebiti) and Art. 2155 (payment by mistake in the construction or application of a doubtful or difficult question of law).
  • Mistake of lawyer: a lawyer cannot be disbarred for an honest mistake or error of law (In re Filart).
Kasilag v. Rodriguez (69 Phil. 217, 1939)

Emiliana Ambrosio mortgaged to Kasilag the improvements on her homestead. The deed was in form a mortgage, but Kasilag took possession and enjoyed the fruits, which in substance made it antichresis. Encumbering the homestead itself within the prohibited period was barred by the Public Land Act (Sec. 116). Doctrine: "Gross and inexcusable ignorance of law may not be the basis of good faith, but possible, excusable ignorance may be such basis." Kasilag was not a lawyer. He accepted the mortgage of improvements on the well-grounded belief that he was not violating the prohibition, and he did not know, "as clearly as a jurist does," that possession and enjoyment of fruits are attributes of antichresis, which as a lien was prohibited by Sec. 116. His ignorance was excusable, so he was a possessor (and builder) in good faith, entitled to reimbursement for the improvements or to the rights of a builder in good faith.

II.C. Retroactivity (Art. 4; Arts. 2252–2269; RPC Art. 22; FC Art. 256)

Art. 4, NCC. Laws shall have no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided. (3)
Admin. Code (E.O. 292) Book I, Sec. 19. Laws shall have prospective effect unless the contrary is expressly provided.
RPC Art. 22. Retroactive Effect of Penal Laws. — Penal laws shall have a retroactive effect in so far as they favor the person guilty of a felony, who is not a habitual criminal, as this term is defined in rule 5 of article 62 of this Code, although at the time of the publication of such laws a final sentence has been pronounced and the convict is serving the same.
FC Art. 256. This Code shall have retroactive effect insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws.

Concept and reason

  • A retroactive law is one intended to affect transactions that occurred, or rights that accrued, before it became operative, and which ascribes to them effects not inherent in their nature in view of the law in force when they occurred. It creates a new obligation, imposes a new duty, or attaches a new disability to past transactions (Tolentino).
  • Reason: Art. 4 is necessarily related to Art. 3. The obligatory force of law presupposes that it has been made known. A law not yet effective cannot be conclusively known, and applying a later inconsistent law to acts done under the former would be unjust, because people would not know which law to follow.
  • Construction: statutes are construed as prospective unless the intent to give retrospective effect is expressly declared or necessarily implied from the language used. In every case of doubt, the doubt is resolved AGAINST retroactivity (Tolentino). (Bank item: "in case of doubt, the doubt must be resolved in favor of retroactivity" is FALSE.) Examples of laws not given retroactive effect: laws increasing the formalities of wills, the Usury Law, Civil Code provisions that prejudice rights acquired before its promulgation, compulsory retirement of justices of the peace at 60, and subsidiary imprisonment for insolvency.

Exceptions: laws that MAY be given retroactive effect (Tolentino's list plus additions)

  1. When the law expressly so provides. Even then, there are two constitutional limits. (a) If retroactivity would make a penal law ex post facto, or the law is a bill of attainder (Const. Art. III, Sec. 22). (b) If retroactivity would impair the obligation of contracts (Art. III, Sec. 10). In these cases the retroactivity clause is inoperative. Neither may it destroy vested rights (Uson; Arts. 2252, 2253; FC 256).
  2. Remedial or procedural statutes: these refer to the method of enforcing rights or obtaining redress. Procedure may be changed at any time, effective at once, and applies to actions pending and undetermined at the time of passage, provided vested rights are not affected. No vested right attaches to procedural rules (Acosta v. Plan; Dacudao). The prescriptive period may be shortened even for actions that have already accrued, provided a reasonable time remains.
  3. Curative statutes: these cure errors and irregularities, validating judicial or administrative proceedings, acts of public officers, or private deeds and contracts that otherwise would not produce their intended effects because of some statutory disability or failure to comply with a technical requirement. They are necessarily retroactive (Puzon v. Abellera; Superiora Locale v. Republic, 2022). Limits: they cannot violate constitutional provisions, destroy vested rights of third persons, or affect final judgments.
  4. Interpretative or declaratory statutes: these clarify doubts in an existing law and are considered incorporated in it from the beginning, but they do not affect final judicial decisions (Chile Civil Code Art. 9, cited by Tolentino). Related: a judicial interpretation of a statute forms part of the law from the date of its original passage (Art. 8; contemporaneous construction).
  5. Laws creating new rights: if a right is declared for the first time by a new law, it takes effect from the time of the declaration even though it arose from acts subject to the former law, provided it does not prejudice another acquired right of the same origin (Art. 2253; Tolentino).
  6. Penal laws favorable to the accused who is not a habitual criminal, even if final sentence has been pronounced and is being served (RPC Art. 22).
  7. Family Code provisions: retroactive insofar as they do not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights (FC Art. 256; Alexander v. Spouses Escalona, 2022: FC Art. 124 applied to a disposition of conjugal property, made after the FC took effect, by a spouse of a pre-FC marriage; Bernabe v. Alejo, 2002: the FC cannot take away the right vested under the Civil Code to prove filiation within four years from attaining majority; Francisco v. CA, 1998).
  8. Tax laws may be retroactive if expressly so provided and not so harsh as to be confiscatory (general statutory construction).

Cases

Uson v. Del Rosario (92 Phil. 530, 1953)

Faustino Nebreda died in 1945, before the new Civil Code. He was survived by his lawful wife Maria Uson and by illegitimate children with his common-law wife Maria del Rosario. Del Rosario's side invoked the new Civil Code, which grants illegitimate children successional rights. Doctrine: The new Civil Code cannot be applied retroactively. The surviving wife's rights to the inheritance vested at the moment of Nebreda's death (succession opens at death), and Art. 2253 itself withholds new rights that "prejudice or impair any vested or acquired right of the same origin." The illegitimate children took nothing. (Related: Maria Uson's earlier waiver, in a deed of separation, of her rights to Faustino's future estate was void, because no one can renounce a future inheritance. Link to Art. 6.)

Uribe (on Uson and Art. 4): "If the law expressly provides for retroactive effect, then it will be given retroactive effect?" NO: ex post facto, impairment of vested rights. "If the law does not expressly provide for retroactive effect, then it cannot be given retroactive effect?" NO: there are exceptions. In formulating the issue in Uson he insisted on the decisive fact: the husband died before the effectivity of the new Code, and the legal wife survived him, so her successional rights vested at death. "Illegitimate children are entitled to successional rights" is insufficient, because the children were also asserting status.
Puzon v. Abellera (169 SCRA 789, January 31, 1989)

Involves P.D. 1271, the decree on titles over lands in the Baguio Townsite Reservation, which declared certain titles void but validated others upon compliance with conditions. Doctrine: Art. 4 prohibits retroactive application unless expressly provided, but the rule admits exceptions. "A statute operates prospectively only and never retroactively, unless the legislative intent to the contrary is made manifest either by the express terms of the statute or by necessary implication." P.D. 1271 falls under one of the exceptions: it is curative and validating in character, so it applies to titles issued before its enactment.

Acosta v. Plan (169 SCRA 591, January 30, 1989)

Doctrine: Under B.P. 129 and Sec. 18 of the Interim Rules and Guidelines (January 11, 1983), a record on appeal is no longer necessary for taking an appeal (except in special cases). Being procedural, these provisions may be applied retroactively for the benefit of appellants whose appeals were pending. "Statutes regulating the procedure of the courts will be construed as applicable to actions pending and undetermined at the time of their passage. Procedural laws are retrospective in that sense and to that extent."

Spouses Dacudao v. Secretary of Justice Gonzalez (G.R. No. 188056, January 8, 2013)

The Dacudaos had filed estafa and syndicated estafa complaints against officers of the Legacy Group with the Davao City Prosecutor. DOJ Order No. 182 (2009) directed that all Legacy cases nationwide be forwarded to the DOJ in Manila for resolution. The Dacudaos assailed its application to their already-pending complaints. Doctrine: As a general rule laws have no retroactive effect, but procedural laws are an exception. A remedial statute does not create new rights or take away vested rights. It only operates in furtherance of the remedy or confirmation of existing rights. A rule regulating procedure applies to actions pending and undetermined at the time of its passage. "All procedural laws are retroactive in that sense and to that extent." This violates no right of a person who feels adversely affected, because no vested right generally attaches to or arises from procedural laws.

MRCA v. CA (supra)

Listed again under retroactivity. See II.A: a court ruling laying down a new procedural rule takes effect without OG publication, and as a procedural rule it governs pending cases subject to the vested-rights limit.

II.D. Acts against mandatory or prohibitory laws (Art. 5)

Art. 5, NCC. Acts executed against the provisions of mandatory or prohibitory laws shall be void, except when the law itself authorizes their validity. (4a)
  • Rule: acts contrary to mandatory or prohibitory laws are VOID, not voidable, unenforceable or rescissible. (Bank pair item: "...shall be voidable, except when the law itself authorizes their validity" is FALSE.)
  • Mandatory vs. directory: there is no fixed test; the object is legislative intent. Provisions that are mere matters of form, not material, affecting no substantial right, and where compliance is a matter of convenience are directory. Provisions relating to matters of substance and the essence of the thing to be done are mandatory. Acts contrary to directory, permissive or suppletory laws are not nullified by Art. 5; it is not possible to "violate" them, since many exist only for private interests and can be abrogated by agreement. Directory provisions are still not meant to be disregarded, and courts determine the consequences of non-compliance (Tolentino).
  • Violation of a mandatory law renders the act illegal and void. Examples: an unregistered sale of large cattle in violation of the registration law transfers no title. A forged deed is an absolute nullity.
  • Exception: "when the law itself authorizes their validity". "Law" here refers to the juridical order in its totality. The legislature may prohibit an act, even penalize it, yet not destroy its validity if done. Intent may be inferred, e.g., from the fact that nullity would bring about harmful consequences the law does not sanction. Manresa's three cases (Tolentino):
    1. The violation does not refer to an essential matter and nullity would be more disadvantageous than validity. Example: a marriage solemnized without one or more formal requirements is valid, although the parties responsible may be criminally liable (today: FC Art. 4 par. 3, irregularity in formal requisites).
    2. The law makes validity depend on the consent of the party directly interested in the nullity. Example: a marriage annullable for force or fraud may be validated by the injured party's free cohabitation after the force ceased or the fraud was discovered (FC Art. 45).
    3. The law declares the act null but recognizes its effects as legally existing. Example: when a marriage is annulled, children conceived or born before the annulment are legitimate (FC Art. 54).
  • Related: Art. 1409 (void contracts), Art. 1411–1412 (in pari delicto), Art. 17 par. 3 (prohibitive laws are not defeated by foreign laws, judgments or conventions).
Bank of the Philippine Islands v. Intermediate Appellate Court (Zshornack) (164 SCRA 630, August 19, 1988)

Zshornack, who alleged he was a Philippine resident, delivered US$3,000 to the bank under a document showing the bank was to safekeep the dollars and return them later. They were not to be sold to the Central Bank. Doctrine: The contract was a depositum for safekeeping. Under C.B. Circular No. 20, foreign exchange received by residents had to be sold to the Central Bank (through authorized agents) within one business day. Mere safekeeping of the dollars without selling them is a transaction not authorized by the Circular, so it falls under the general class of prohibited transactions. Under Art. 5 it is void, having been executed against a mandatory/prohibitory law. It gives neither party a cause of action against the other: when the nullity proceeds from the illegality of the cause or object and the act is a criminal offense, both parties being in pari delicto have no action against each other (Art. 1411). The only remedy is for the State to prosecute both for violating the law.

II.E. Waiver of rights (Art. 6; Art. 301; Art. 2035)

Art. 6, NCC. Rights may be waived, unless the waiver is contrary to law, public order, public policy, morals, or good customs, or prejudicial to a third person with a right recognized by law. (4a)
Art. 301, NCC. The right to receive support cannot be renounced; nor can it be transmitted to a third person. Neither can it be compensated with what the recipient owes the obligor. However, support in arrears may be compensated and renounced.
Art. 2035, NCC. No compromise upon the following questions shall be valid:
(1) The civil status of persons;
(2) The validity of a marriage or a legal separation;
(3) Any ground for legal separation;
(4) Future support;
(5) The jurisdiction of courts;
(6) Future legitime. (1814a)
Note: Art. 301 belongs to Title VIII (Support) of Book I, which FC Art. 254 repealed. Uribe still lists it, and the principle survives. Future support cannot be waived or compromised (Art. 2035(4)). Support in arrears, being already a patrimonial credit, may be renounced or compensated. The FC rules on support are in FC Arts. 194–208.

Elements and kinds of rights (Tolentino)

  • Every right has three elements: (1) subjects: the active subject (entitled to demand) and the passive subject (duty-bound). The passive subject is a determinate person in personal rights, and indeterminate (the whole world) in real rights. (2) Object: things and services. (3) Efficient cause: the fact that gives rise to the legal relation (the will of man, as in a contract, or independent of it, as in a fortuitous event).
  • Rights are political (participation in government) or civil. Civil rights are: (1) rights of personality (human rights from being a man: life, physical security, honor, liberty, to learn, write, speak, work); (2) family rights (all rights of a person as a member of a family); (3) patrimonial rights, which have property as their object and are measurable pecuniarily. Patrimonial rights are either real (ownership, mortgage) or personal (right to collect a debt). A right to indemnity for injury is patrimonial.
  • Rights of personality and family rights are NOT subject to waiver; patrimonial rights generally may be waived (Tolentino). (Bank pair item: "Rights of personality and family rights, like patrimonial rights, are generally subject to waiver" is FALSE.)

Waiver: definition, form and requisites

  • Waiver is the relinquishment of a known right with both knowledge of its existence and an intention to relinquish it. Voluntary choice is its essence.
  • Express or implied. An implied waiver needs a clear, unequivocal and decisive act showing that purpose. It is never presumed from doubtful acts. It may also be implied from failure or neglect to assert a right at the proper time.
  • Requisites: (1) the person must actually have the right he renounces. One cannot renounce liability for future fraud (Art. 1171), a future inheritance (Art. 1347 par. 2; Uson), or the action to revoke a donation for ingratitude in advance. (2) He must have capacity to make the renunciation. (3) The renunciation must be made in a clear and unequivocal manner, with any formality the law requires (otherwise it may be tacit if the intent is clearly established). Renouncing one right does not presume renouncing another, especially where the party did not know he had the other right.
  • Scope: all rights and privileges, whether from contract, statute or Constitution, provided they rest in the individual and are for his sole benefit. Constitutional rights can be waived (e.g., to a preliminary investigation, to counsel, to venue). Obligations generally cannot be renounced, but one may exempt himself from an obligation inherent in a right by renouncing the right (e.g., a co-owner renouncing his share of a servitude to avoid contributing to expenses).
  • Real rights: renunciation is unilateral, depending only on the owner's will. Renunciation of a personal right (remission or condonation) needs the debtor's consent. Effects: a renounced real right distinct from ownership (usufruct, easement, mortgage) merges in the owner. If there are co-holders, the others' shares increase. If full ownership is renounced, the thing becomes res nullius, open to occupation.
  • Prohibited waivers (the five limits of Art. 6): contrary to (1) law, (2) public order, (3) public policy, (4) morals, (5) good customs; or (6) prejudicial to a third person with a right recognized by law. Laws cannot be renounced, though rights arising from them may be. Rights created by laws of a general and mandatory character, and rules on status and capacity, formalities, and privileges granted for incapacity, are of public order and cannot be waived. A waiver void as against public policy cannot be validated by estoppel. As to third persons, the prejudice must be injury to a right they have. Mere actual damage without a right being injured does not void the waiver.
  • Source note (Tolentino): the old code allowed waiver of "rights granted by law". The new Article drops that qualification, and the limits were expanded from public order and third persons to include law, public policy, morals and good customs.

Cases

De Borja v. Vda. de De Borja (46 SCRA 577, 1972)

Francisco de Borja died. His son Jose de Borja (administrator of the estate) and Francisco's widow by his second marriage, Tasiana Ongsingco, entered into a compromise in which Tasiana received a fixed sum (₱800,000) in full settlement of her hereditary share. The compromise was made while the probate of Francisco's will was pending. Tasiana later assailed it as void, arguing the estate could not be disposed of before probate. Doctrine: The compromise was valid. Hereditary rights are transmitted from the moment of death (Art. 777), so as a compulsory heir Tasiana already had a vested share that she could dispose of or waive. There is no prohibition against an heir's disposing of his hereditary share before the will is probated, as long as the probate court's eventual decision is respected. The agreement was not on a future inheritance (the decedent was already dead). The waiver of her share was a valid exercise of Art. 6.

Uribe (De Borja variations): Problem: X died a month ago. Before probate, A (an heir) compromised with the other heirs, waiving his share for ₱10M. A later learns his share is ₱25M and claims the waiver is void. Answer: valid. A was fully aware of his rights, there is no vice of consent in the facts, and the right had already vested at X's death. "The will was not yet probated" does not matter, because the heir in De Borja was a compulsory heir (the wife). Watch the changed fact: if the waiving heir were only an instituted voluntary heir and the will is later denied probate, he may have had nothing to waive. Wrong answers he rejected: "it is an inchoate right that can be waived" and "you can waive your hereditary rights while your grandparents are alive" (both wrong: future inheritance cannot be waived). Old quiz: "A compromise agreement entered into by an heir after the death of the testator may be valid even if the will is denied probate" is TRUE (De Borja), if he is a compulsory or legal heir.
Asian Cathay Finance and Leasing Corp. v. Spouses Gravador (G.R. No. 186550, July 5, 2010)

A loan secured by a real estate mortgage, documented in the lender's pre-printed forms, with onerous interest, penalty and related stipulations. Doctrine (Art. 6): a waiver must be couched in clear and unequivocal terms that leave no doubt of the intention to give up a right or benefit that legally pertains to the waiving party. Waivers of rights in contracts of adhesion are construed strictly against the party that drafted them. A stipulation that is contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy, such as an unconscionable interest or penalty imposed in the lender's form contract, is void and is not cured by the borrower's signature on the form.

Note: Uribe's doctrines outline gives no digest for Asian Cathay, and none appeared in the review materials. The doctrine above is the Art. 6 principle the case is cited for. Read the full text for the specific stipulations struck down before relying on factual details in recitation.
Related (B29, not syllabus)

D.M. Consunji v. CA (2001): waiver is the intentional relinquishment of a known right. A widow who claimed ECC death benefits without knowing she could alternatively sue under the Civil Code did not waive the civil action. Her ignorance of the fact of the employer's negligence, a mistake of fact, prevents a valid waiver. Aujero v. PhilComSat (2012): voluntary, legitimate quitclaims entered into with full understanding by a person of education are respected as the law between the parties.

(2014 Bar; bank P-29.) For a Grade VI field trip, parents signed: "I will not file any claim against the school... in case something happens to my child during the trip." Joey, 7, was bitten by a snake at the zoo. Is the waiver valid?

Answer: No. Valid waiver requires (1) existence of the right, (2) capacity to waive, and (3) that it not be contrary to law, public order, public policy, morals or good customs, or prejudicial to a third person with a right recognized by law (Art. 6). This waiver exonerates the school in advance from liability for future negligence. In effect it allows the school to dispense even with ordinary diligence over pupils in its custody, which is contrary to public policy (cf. Arts. 1171–1172, 2180). Notarization is not a requisite of waiver.

H and W, during a legal separation case, agree that W waives all future support from H in exchange for ₱500,000. Valid?

Answer: The waiver of future support is void (Art. 301 principle; Art. 2035(4): no compromise on future support). The support is a family right founded on need and public policy. Support in arrears may be renounced or compromised. (Note also Art. 2035(2)–(3): no compromise on the validity of a marriage or on grounds for legal separation.)

Son S, while his mother M is alive, signs a deed renouncing his future legitime from M in favor of his sister. Valid?

Answer: Void. One can waive only a right one actually has. A future inheritance is not yet a right (Art. 1347 par. 2; Art. 2035(6); Uson). Contrast De Borja: after death the right is vested and may be waived.

Quiz traps (Art. 6): Check the list of five plus the third-person clause. Dropping "public policy" or "good customs", or saying "prejudicial to any third person" (instead of "a third person with a right recognized by law"), makes the statement false. Future support, future legitime, future fraud and future inheritance: not waivable. Support in arrears: waivable.

II.F. Repeal of laws; supremacy of the Constitution; judicial decisions; doubt (Arts. 7, 8, 10; Const. Art. XVIII Sec. 3)

Art. 7, NCC. Laws are repealed only by subsequent ones, and their violation or non-observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary.
When the courts declare a law to be inconsistent with the Constitution, the former shall be void and the latter shall govern.
Administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations shall be valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution. (5a)
1987 Constitution, Art. XVIII, Sec. 3. All existing laws, decrees, executive orders, proclamations, letters of instructions, and other executive issuances not inconsistent with this Constitution shall remain operative until amended, repealed, or revoked.
Art. 8, NCC. Judicial decisions applying or interpreting the laws or the Constitution shall form a part of the legal system of the Philippines. (n)
Art. 10, NCC. In case of doubt in the interpretation or application of laws, it is presumed that the lawmaking body intended right and justice to prevail. (n)

Art. 7 par. 1: repeal, lapse and desuetude

  • Reason: laws are promulgated by the competent authority of the State, so they cease only by the will of the State. Only the State can abrogate its own acts. A law in the statute books keeps its force notwithstanding any practice or usage to the contrary. Disuse (desuetude), custom or contrary practice never repeals a law.
  • Lapse: some laws cease without repeal by their own terms. They may expressly provide for a fixed period (e.g., a Rental Law in force for four years), or their intent may show a limited duration (e.g., the Emergency Powers Act, C.A. 671, held to have a restricted life).
  • Kinds of repeal: (1) express or declared, contained in a special provision of a subsequent law; (2) implied or tacit, when the provisions of the later law are incompatible or inconsistent with those of the earlier law. A general clause "all laws inconsistent herewith are hereby repealed" (e.g., Art. 2270) is not an express repeal. It adds nothing to the effect of implied repeal.
  • Implied repeal is not favored. It rests only on a presumption of intent. There must be a plain, unavoidable and irreconcilable repugnancy. If both laws can stand together by reasonable construction, both are sustained. Requisites: (1) the laws cover the same subject matter; (2) the latter is repugnant to the earlier. (Thornton v. Thornton, 2004: the Family Courts Act did not impliedly repeal the habeas corpus jurisdiction of the CA and SC.)
  • General vs. special law: a later general law does not repeal a prior special law unless there is a clear and necessary conflict. The special law is taken as an exception to the general law, and the matter is one of legislative intent. A new code covering a whole subject matter may supersede prior laws on it (CIR v. Romig, 2024 restates the special-law rule).
  • Repeal of a repealing law (Admin. Code Secs. 21–22):
    Law 1 was repealed by Law 2...Law 2 is then repealed by Law 3. Is Law 1 revived?
    ExpresslyNo, unless Law 3 expressly so provides (Sec. 21)
    ImpliedlyYes, unless Law 3 provides otherwise (Sec. 22). Tolentino's example: repeal of the Perjury Law by the Administrative Code revived the Penal Code provisions on perjury, which had been repealed only by implication.
  • Effect of repeal: governed by the rules on retroactivity. Repeal cannot impair vested rights, acts done, penalties accrued, or judgments already final. But a repeal of a penal law pending prosecution, being favorable to the accused, generally deprives the court of authority to proceed, and the case must be dismissed (Tolentino, citing People v. Tamayo), unless the repealing law provides otherwise.

Art. 7 pars. 2–3: supremacy of the Constitution and validity of executive acts

  • "The former [the law] shall be void and the latter [the Constitution] shall govern." The test of validity is found in the Constitution itself, in a general principle or a specific rule. A law cannot be voided merely because it violates the "spirit" of free institutions or because the court considers it unjust.
  • Administrative or executive acts are valid only when not contrary to the laws or the Constitution. Regulations must be in harmony with the law and are for the sole purpose of carrying it into effect. They cannot extend the law (Teoxon v. Members of the Board of Administrators).
  • Operative fact doctrine: as a rule, nullification of an unconstitutional law carries with it the illegality of its effects. Where nullification of the effects would result in inequity and injustice, the effects of the law before its nullification may be recognized as an operative fact (Tañada 1985; Araullo v. Aquino, 2015).
Guingona, Jr. v. Carague (196 SCRA 221, April 22, 1991)

Senators questioned the automatic appropriation for debt service in the 1990 budget under P.D. 81, P.D. 1177 and P.D. 1967, arguing these decrees were repealed by, or inconsistent with, the 1987 Constitution. Doctrine: The decrees remained operative under Art. XVIII, Sec. 3 because they were not inconsistent with the Constitution and had not been amended, repealed or revoked. "Well-known is the rule that repeal or amendment by implication is frowned upon. Equally fundamental is the principle that construction of the Constitution and law is generally applied prospectively and not retrospectively unless it is so clearly stated."

Art. 8: judicial decisions

  • Not an independent source of law. Courts only apply or interpret the law, so a "judge-made law" is impossible under separation of powers (Tolentino). But the Court's interpretation of a statute constitutes part of the law as of the date it was originally passed, since it merely establishes the contemporaneous legislative intent (doctrine of contemporaneous construction; Pesca v. Pesca, 2001: the Molina guidelines applied to a marriage contracted before Molina). Exception: when a doctrine is overruled and a party relied on the old one, the new doctrine applies prospectively (People v. Jabinal principle).
  • Double function of jurisprudence: (1) to fill deficiencies of legislation and provide a rule for facts with no positive provision or established custom; (2) to adapt rigid provisions, made inadequate by time and circumstances, to changing conditions.
  • Stare decisis (et non quieta movere): adhere to precedents. A Supreme Court decision becomes a precedent to be followed by all courts. Only Supreme Court decisions create binding precedent under stare decisis. It is flexible: when, in the light of changing conditions, a rule has ceased to be of benefit and use to society, or is found contrary to law, courts may and must depart from it (De Castro v. JBC, 2010; 2011 Bar: the doctrine may be abandoned "when the precedent has ceased to be beneficial and useful"). It is a principle of policy, not a mechanical formula, and it does not apply when there is a conflict between the law and the precedent.

Art. 10: presumption that right and justice prevail

  • Source: Swiss Civil Code Art. 4 and the Colombian code. Applied only in case of doubt, when all other rules of interpretation fail. If the law is clear, the judge must apply it even if it does not conform to his idea of right and justice (dura lex sed lex).
  • Equity is an attribute of justice. It may correct the bare written law by limiting its excessive generality, and at times by supplying deficiencies. It is "justice sweetened with mercy" and seeks the intent of the legislator rather than the bare letter (Tolentino). (B29: Alonzo v. Intermediate Appellate Court (Padua), 1987: courts apply the law with justice. Notice of redemption among co-heirs was deemed received through actual knowledge, since years, not days, had passed.)

(Pair, Uribe samplexes.) 1. "Laws are repealed only by existing ones, and their violation or non-observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary." 2. "Laws are repealed only by subsequent ones, and their violation or non-observance shall not be excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary."

Answer: No. 1 FALSE ("existing"), No. 2 TRUE.

(Pair.) 1. "When the courts declare a law to be inconsistent with the Constitution, the former shall be void and the latter shall govern." 2. "...the latter shall be void and the former shall govern."

Answer: No. 1 TRUE, No. 2 FALSE. "Former" = law, "latter" = Constitution (Floresca v. Philex: the Constitution is the supreme, organic law; no statute can override it).

(Pair.) 1. "Administrative or executive acts, orders and regulations shall be valid when they are not contrary to the existing laws." 2. "...shall be valid only when they are not contrary to the laws or the Constitution."

Answer: No. 1 FALSE (omits "only" and the Constitution). No. 2 TRUE.

A municipal ordinance has not been enforced for 30 years; everyone keeps pigs in town despite its prohibition. X, prosecuted, pleads desuetude.

Answer: No defense. Violation or non-observance is not excused by disuse, or custom or practice to the contrary (Art. 7 par. 1). The ordinance stands until repealed by a subsequent ordinance or law, or declared invalid. (Cf. Art. 11: customs contrary to law are not countenanced.)

II.G. Duty to render judgment (Art. 9; RPC Art. 5)

Art. 9, NCC. No judge or court shall decline to render judgment by reason of the silence, obscurity or insufficiency of the laws. (6)
RPC Art. 5. Duty of the court in connection with acts which should be repressed but which are not covered by the law, and in cases of excessive penalties.—Whenever a court has knowledge of any act which it may deem proper to repress and which is not punishable by law, it shall render the proper decision, and shall report to the Chief Executive, through the Department of Justice, the reasons which induce the court to believe that said act should be made the subject of penal legislation.
In the same way the court shall submit to the Chief Executive, through the Department of Justice, such statement as may be deemed proper, without suspending the execution of the sentence, when a strict enforcement of the provisions of this Code would result in the imposition of a clearly excessive penalty, taking into consideration the degree of malice and the injury caused by the offense.
  • Three situations, one duty: the law is silent (no provision), obscure (vague or ambiguous), or insufficient (does not cover all aspects). In all three the court must decide. (Bank pair: "...shall not decline by reason of the silence or obscurity of the laws; but he may decline by reason of the insufficiency of the laws" is FALSE.)
  • Obscure law: clarify it in the light of the rules of statutory construction. Silent or insufficient law: fill the deficiency by resorting to customs or general principles of law. Only when all rules of interpretation are exhausted may the court create a rule on the facts within the juridical order. The court's ignorance of the applicable law is no reason to dismiss (e.g., a cockfight dispute over who won cannot be summarily dismissed because the judge is unacquainted with cockfighting rules) (Tolentino).
  • Not applicable to criminal cases in the sense of creating crimes: nullum crimen, nulla poena sine lege. If no law punishes the act, the case must be dismissed or the accused acquitted, however reprehensible the act. That dismissal or acquittal is itself "the proper decision" (RPC Art. 5). The court then reports to the President, through the DOJ, recommending penal legislation, and likewise when a strict application of the RPC yields a clearly excessive penalty (without suspending execution).
  • Unjust laws: a judge must apply the law as it is. He cannot refuse to apply a law because he considers it unjust (dura lex sed lex), and he cannot invade the legislative domain.
  • Suppletory rules (Tolentino): Art. 6 of the old Code said that where there is no statute exactly applicable, the custom of the place shall apply, and in its absence the general principles of law. The Code Commission's draft article on this was eliminated by Congress, but these suppletory rules are still considered existing. Customs, analogy, jurisprudence, opinions of jurisconsults and general principles of law fill gaps.
  • General principles of law: Valverde and Sánchez Román: "universal juridical standards dictated by correct reason... those high standards which serve as a foundation to positive law..., which form a law superior to that which is enacted." Manresa: the principles serving as the basis for the positive law of each country. Since the mission of courts is to do justice, the phrase must be given a broad meaning (equity, natural law, the fundamental principles of juridical science), limited only in that decisions should not establish rules contrary to the essence of the existing social order. Illustration: absent a rule on lease of personal property, the Court applied by analogy the rules on lease of real property. Where a judge doubted which of two claimants had the better right, he applied the principle that the possessor has the better title.

A suit concerns rights over a new kind of digital asset not covered by any statute. The judge dismisses, saying "there is no law on this." Correct?

Answer: No. Art. 9 forbids declining judgment by reason of the silence or insufficiency of the laws. The judge must decide by analogy, custom proved as fact (Art. 12), general principles of law and equity, and, in doubt, presume that right and justice were intended (Art. 10).

An act is morally reprehensible but no law punishes it. The judge convicts under Art. 9, reasoning he cannot decline judgment because of the silence of the law.

Answer: Wrong. Art. 9 does not authorize creating crimes. The proper decision is acquittal or dismissal, plus a report to the President through the DOJ recommending penal legislation (RPC Art. 5).

Quiz traps (Arts. 9–10): "silence, obscurity or insufficiency" (all three). Art. 10 applies "in case of doubt" only. Statements saying the judge may disregard a clear law to do justice are FALSE.

II.H. Applicability of custom (Arts. 11–12; Const. Art. XII Sec. 5; Rule 129 Secs. 2–3)

Art. 11, NCC. Customs which are contrary to law, public order or public policy shall not be countenanced. (n)
Art. 12, NCC. A custom must be proved as a fact, according to the rules of evidence. (n)
1987 Constitution, Art. XII, Sec. 5. The State, subject to the provisions of this Constitution and national development policies and programs, shall protect the rights of indigenous cultural communities to their ancestral lands to ensure their economic, social, and cultural well-being.
The Congress may provide for the applicability of customary laws governing property rights or relations in determining the ownership and extent of ancestral domain.
Rules of Court, Rule 129, Sec. 2. Judicial notice, when discretionary. — A court may take judicial notice of matters which are of public knowledge, or are capable to unquestionable demonstration, or ought to be known to judges because of their judicial functions.
Sec. 3. Judicial notice, when hearing necessary. — During the trial, the court, on its own initiative, or on request of a party, may announce its intention to take judicial notice of any matter and allow the parties to be heard thereon.
After the trial, and before judgment or on appeal, the proper court, on its own initiative or on request of a party, may take judicial notice of any matter and allow the parties to be heard thereon if such matter is decisive of a material issue in the case.
  • Custom is a juridical rule that results from a constant and continued uniform practice by the members of a social community, with respect to a particular state of facts, and observed with a conviction that it is juridically obligatory (Tolentino).
  • Requisites (Tolentino, 6): (1) plurality of acts, i.e., various resolutions of a juridical question raised repeatedly in life; (2) uniformity or identity of the acts or solutions; (3) general practice by the great mass of the social group; (4) continued performance for a long period of time; (5) general conviction that the practice corresponds to a juridical necessity or is obligatory (opinio juris); (6) the practice must not be contrary to law, morals or public order (Art. 11). Plus Art. 12: it must be proved as a fact.
  • Custom vs. law: in origin, custom comes spontaneously from society while law comes from the governmental power of the State (a conscious creation). In form, custom is tacit and unwritten, manifested in acts and usages, while law is express and written in solemn official form.
  • Art. 11 (contra legem custom): no man or set of men can create a custom for their own benefit or convenience paramount to an express law. Courts will not recognize a custom opposed to positive law. A custom praeter legem (where the law is silent) may supply deficiencies (Art. 9). A custom secundum legem aids interpretation.
  • Art. 12 (proof): customs cannot be presumed. They must be proved as facts by the party relying on them, and courts take no judicial notice of mere customs (Patriarca v. Orate). When an alleged custom is unknown to those who from their business connections are best placed to know it, that ignorance is in a sense positive evidence of its non-existence. Rule 129 Secs. 2–3 allow judicial notice only of matters of public knowledge or capable of unquestionable demonstration, after hearing the parties. A custom of truly public notoriety might be noticed, but the codal rule is proof.
  • Which custom applies: a person performing a juridical act not provided for by law is presumed to act according to the custom of the place. If the parties' domiciles have different customs, Manresa treats it as if there were no custom. Sánchez Román applies the custom of the place of performance or consummation.
  • Illustrations: a contract made with reference to the Saigon–Hongkong trade was governed by that trade's custom. Long acquiescence in an interpretation should be followed. Words in common mercantile use are given their accepted meaning. Compensation for services may be fixed by the custom of the place. Customs are mostly relevant in negligence cases (Martinez v. Van Buskirk) and in family law and succession, where the Code Commission incorporated Filipino customs.
  • Constitution: customary laws of indigenous cultural communities may govern property rights or relations in ancestral domain (Art. XII Sec. 5; IPRA, R.A. 8371). B29: Anaban v. Anaban-Alfiler (2021): a divorce under Ifugao/Kalinga tribal custom after the Family Code did not dissolve the marriage. Local customs cannot supplant the law except where the law allows. In re Sycip, Salazar (1979): keeping a deceased partner's name in a law firm name is not a Philippine custom (only foreign), and a custom must be local and proved.
Martinez v. Van Buskirk (18 Phil. 79, December 27, 1910)

The defendant's cochero left his delivery wagon and horses unattended in the street while helping unload merchandise. A passing vehicle frightened the horses, which ran away and injured Mrs. Martinez in her carromata. Doctrine: The defendant was not negligent. It is a matter of common knowledge, as well as proof, that it was the universal practice of merchants to deliver merchandise in that manner and to leave horses as they were left. That custom obtained in all cities, had not been productive of accidents, and the public had acquiesced in it for years without objection. "Ought the public now, through the courts, without prior objection or notice, to be permitted to reverse the practice of decades and thereby make culpable and guilty one who had every reason and assurance to believe that he was acting under the sanction of the strongest of all civil forces, the custom of a people." Acts that have not proved injurious and have been acquiesced in by society for so long that they have ripened into custom cannot be held unreasonable or imprudent.

Note (commentators, from the class reviewer notes): custom matters most in negligence cases. If, by custom, Manila kalesas are supposed to have rattan baskets to keep people from slipping, a person who slips because there is none may sue for negligence. Custom is relevant only if proved.

In a collection suit, the defendant claims that by "custom in the province" interest of 10% a month on informal loans is normal and binding. Ruling?

Answer: Rejected on two grounds. (1) A custom must be proved as a fact (Art. 12). A bare allegation is not proof, and courts take no judicial notice of local practices. (2) Even if proved, a custom sanctioning unconscionable interest is contrary to law and public policy and shall not be countenanced (Art. 11).

Quiz traps (Arts. 11–12): "A custom need not be proved as a fact, since courts take judicial notice of customs" is FALSE. "Customs contrary to law, public order, public policy or morals shall not be countenanced": the codal text lists only law, public order or public policy. (Tolentino's requisites mention morals, but Art. 11's text does not; on a strict codal item, the added word makes it not "absolutely" the provision. Flag it in your explanation.)

II.I. Legal periods (Art. 13; Admin. Code Sec. 31; Rule 22)

Art. 13, NCC. When the laws speak of years, months, days or nights, it shall be understood that years are of three hundred sixty-five days each; months, of thirty days; days, of twenty-four hours; and nights from sunset to sunrise.
If months are designated by their name, they shall be computed by the number of days which they respectively have.
In computing a period, the first day shall be excluded, and the last day included. (7a)
Administrative Code of 1987 (E.O. 292), Book I, Sec. 31. Legal Periods. - "Year" shall be understood to be twelve calendar months; "month" of thirty days, unless it refers to a specific calendar month in which case it shall be computed according to the number of days the specific month contains; "day," to a day of twenty-four hours; and "night," from sunset to sunrise.
Rules of Court, Rule 22, Sec. 1. How to compute time. — In computing any period of time prescribed or allowed by these Rules, or by order of the court, or by any applicable statute, the day of the act or event from which the designated period of time begins to run is to be excluded and the date of performance included. If the last day of the period, as thus computed, falls on a Saturday, a Sunday, or a legal holiday in the place where the court sits, the time shall not run until the next working day.
Sec. 2. Effect of interruption. — Should an act be done which effectively interrupts the running of the period, the allowable period after such interruption shall start to run on the day after notice of the cessation of the cause thereof.
The day of the act that caused the interruption shall be excluded in the computation of the period.
Note: The syllabus cites "ROC Rule 28". That was the old Rules of Court (1940/1964) provision on computing time, quoted in Armigos and by Tolentino. Under the 1997 Rules it is Rule 22, reproduced above. The old Rule 28 ran the period "until the end of the next day which is neither a Sunday or a legal holiday". The current rule adds Saturdays and says "next working day". The Administrative Code of 1987 (Book I, Chapter 8) similarly allows an act due on a Saturday, Sunday or legal holiday to be done on the next working day (paraphrase).

The three rules of Art. 13 as Uribe tests them

  1. Meaning of the units (par. 1): year = 365 days (Civil Code text); month = 30 days; day = 24 hours; night = sunset to sunrise.
  2. Months designated by name (par. 2): computed by the actual number of days each has (e.g., "the month of February" = 28 or 29 days).
  3. Computation (par. 3): exclude the first day, include the last day. Unlike the Rules of Court, Art. 13 has no Saturday/Sunday/holiday extension.

Tolentino commentary

  • Source: old Civil Code Art. 7, modified to (1) include "year" and (2) add par. 3 from Rule 28 of the Rules of Court. The old Code had "months of thirty days", which Sec. 13 of the Revised Administrative Code changed to calendar months. The present article restores the 30-day month and modifies the RAC. Dean Bocobo thought there was no difference between a legal 30-day month and a calendar month, but Tolentino calls this a mistake (Guzman v. Lichauco). The Code refers to the legal month of 30 days, not the calendar month.
  • Senses of "month": lunar month (28 days); calendar month (as designated in the calendar regardless of the number of days); commercial month (ends on the corresponding day of the next month). The Code uses "month" strictly in the legal sense of 30 days (Namarco v. Tecson).
  • "Week" is not defined. By the calendar it is Sunday to Saturday. As a measure of duration without reference to the calendar it is seven consecutive days.
  • Contractual periods: Rule 28 (now Rule 22) applies only to periods "prescribed or allowed by the Rules of Court, by order of a court, or by any applicable statute." When the act and period are contractual, the Sunday/holiday exception does not apply, and the act must be done on the last day even if it is a Sunday or holiday. The contract is the law between the parties, subject to special laws such as those on negotiable instruments.
  • Date specified: the computation rules apply only when a period must be counted from a date. If the act is to be done on a fixed future date, there is nothing to compute.

Admin. Code vs. Civil Code on "year": CIR v. Primetown

Commissioner of Internal Revenue v. Primetown Property Group, Inc. (G.R. No. 162155, August 28, 2007)

Primetown filed its final adjusted return on April 14, 1998 and its claim for refund on April 14, 2000, within the two-year period under the Tax Code. The CTA dismissed the claim: counting years of 365 days (Art. 13), with 2000 a leap year, the two years (730 days) ended April 13, 2000, so the claim was one day late. Doctrine: Sec. 31, Book I of the Administrative Code of 1987 ("year" = twelve calendar months), the more recent law, governs the computation of legal periods. Art. 13 of the Civil Code and Sec. 31 deal with the same subject matter and are irreconcilable as to "year", so Art. 13 is impliedly repealed as to years. Two years is 24 calendar months. Excluding the first day, the 24th calendar month ended April 14, 2000, so the claim was filed on time. Leap years are no longer considered. (The Court did deny the petition, but the petitioner was the CIR. The B29 repository wrongly says Primetown's claim was late. In fact the Court ruled the refund claim timely and remanded the case to the CTA.)

Armigos v. Court of Appeals (179 SCRA 1, November 6, 1989)

A question of whether an appeal was perfected within the reglementary period. Doctrine: Art. 13's rule "in computing a period, the first day shall be excluded, and the last day included" is similar but not identical to Sec. 4 of the Code of Civil Procedure (exclude the first day and include the last; if the last day is a Sunday or legal holiday it is excluded) and to old Rule 28 of the Rules of Court (exclude the day of the act or event; include the last day unless it is a Sunday or legal holiday, in which case the time runs until the end of the next day that is neither). In applying the rule, the Court considered the "day" as synonymous with the "date", counting calendar dates and not 24-hour periods from the hour of the act. The period does not run from the exact time of receipt.

National Marketing Corporation (NAMARCO) v. Tecson (29 SCRA 70, August 27, 1969)

An action to revive a judgment was filed on the tenth anniversary date of the judgment's finality, and the issue was whether the ten-year period had lapsed. Doctrine: Quoting People v. Del Rosario: "with the approval of the Civil Code of the Philippines (R.A. 386)... we have reverted to the provisions of the Spanish Civil Code in accordance with which a month is to be considered as the regular 30-day month... and not the solar or civil month," with the particularity that whereas the Spanish Code mentioned only "months, days or nights," ours added "years" and explicitly ordains that "years are of three hundred sixty-five days." Since the intervening leap years each have 366 days, ten years of 365 days elapsed before the tenth calendar anniversary, so the action had prescribed.

II.J-1. Territoriality and generality of penal laws (Art. 14; RPC Art. 2)

Art. 14, NCC. Penal laws and those of public security and safety shall be obligatory upon all who live or sojourn in Philippine territory, subject to the principles of public international law and to treaty stipulations. (8a)
RPC Art. 2. Application of its provisions.—Except as provided in the treaties and laws of preferential application, the provisions of this Code shall be enforced not only within the Philippine Archipelago, including its atmosphere, its interior waters and maritime zone, but also outside of its jurisdiction, against those who:
1. Should commit an offense while on a Philippine ship or airship;
2. Should forge or counterfeit any coin or currency note of the Philippine Islands or obligations and securities issued by the Government of the Philippine Islands;
3. Should be liable for acts connected with the introduction into these islands of the obligations and securities mentioned in the preceding number;
4. While being public officers or employees, should commit an offense in the exercise of their functions; or
5. Should commit any of the crimes against national security and the law of nations, defined in Title One of Book Two of this Code.
  • Territoriality: penal laws and laws of public security and safety bind everyone in Philippine territory, citizens and aliens, residents and transients ("live or sojourn"). It is based on territory, not citizenship. (Uribe Quiz item: "...obligatory not only upon all citizens of the Philippines, but also upon all who live or sojourn..." is FALSE. Uribe: "This is the territoriality principle.")
  • Generality: applies to all persons within the territory, save those exempted. Aliens owe local and temporary allegiance. They must obey, may be prosecuted, and may be made to bear their share of public burdens. Conversely, aliens enjoy the civil rights the Constitution guarantees to all inhabitants (Tolentino).
  • Exceptions: "subject to the principles of public international law and to treaty stipulations": (1) offenses by a foreign sovereign (head of state) while in Philippine territory; (2) offenses by diplomatic representatives (ambassadors, ministers, envoys). Consuls have only limited (functional) immunity unless a treaty provides more. (3) Offenses committed on a public or armed vessel of a foreign country. A foreign merchant vessel enjoys no such privilege. An offense aboard it in a Philippine port that breaches public order is triable here. (4) Treaties: e.g., the 1947 Military Bases Agreement (historical; Tolentino), today the Visiting Forces Agreement. (5) International-organization immunity, which is functional only (Westfall v. Locsin, 2024: ADB personnel immune only for acts in official capacity). Members of foreign armed forces are not exempt merely because of their military character unless a treaty so provides.
  • Extraterritoriality: RPC Art. 2 extends Philippine penal law outside the territory in the five cases listed. (AAA v. BBB, 2018: R.A. 9262 psychological violence through a marital infidelity committed abroad is triable here because the mental anguish, an element, is suffered in the Philippines.)
  • Civil vs. criminal: penal laws are territorial. Laws on persons and family follow the Filipino abroad (Art. 15).

A Japanese tourist on a 3-day visit to Cebu commits estafa. He claims Philippine penal laws bind only citizens and residents.

Answer: Wrong. Art. 14 binds all who "live or sojourn" in Philippine territory. A transient sojourner is covered, and no rule of international law or treaty exempts a private tourist.

II.J-2. Nationality principle (Art. 15) and foreign divorce cases

Art. 15, NCC. Laws relating to family rights and duties, or to the status, condition and legal capacity of persons are binding upon citizens of the Philippines, even though living abroad. (9a)
FC Art. 26, par. 2 (as amended by E.O. 227). Where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law.
  • Theories on personal law: the domiciliary theory (US, Anglo-American) holds that a person's personal laws are those of his domicile. The nationality theory (Code Napoléon, strengthened in the Italian unification struggle) holds that they are those of his nationality. Our Civil Code follows the nationality theory (Tolentino).
  • Matters covered: (1) family rights and duties (parental authority, marital authority, support among family members, conjugal partnership); (2) status (married or single, legitimate or illegitimate, citizen); (3) condition; (4) legal capacity (capacity to marry and to contract; age of majority). All questions on marriage, divorce, legal separation, support and marital authority are governed exclusively by Philippine law when Filipinos are involved (Tolentino, citing Barretto v. Gonzales).
  • Who is bound: citizens of the Philippines, even though living abroad. By implication, an alien's status and capacity are governed by his national law (Van Dorn; Recio; Del Socorro v. Van Wilsem). Tolentino: if a party is already of age under the law of the State of which he is a citizen, he cannot set aside a contract on the ground of minority even if he would be a minor under Philippine law (Government v. Frank). Renunciation of allegiance is governed by the national law. A foreign adoption is given the same effect here as the status it creates under the foreign law, unless public policy demands the lex fori.
  • Uribe on exceptions: "Are there any exceptions to this rule? WALA (none)." The Quiz statement "Laws relating to legal capacity of persons are binding upon citizens of the Philippines, even though living abroad" is TRUE: it lists only one item, but it does not say "only". FC Art. 26 par. 2 is not really an exception. It operates through Philippine law, which itself confers capacity to remarry on the Filipino.
  • Uribe on succession: if the problem is the capacity of an heir to succeed, Art. 15 is not the proper basis ("It is about persons: legal capacity to contract marriage, status of the child..."). The correct basis is Art. 1039: "Capacity to succeed is governed by the law of the nation of the decedent" (together with Art. 16 par. 2).

Cases

Barretto Gonzales v. Gonzales (58 Phil. 67, March 7, 1933)

Both spouses were Filipino citizens, married and domiciled in Manila. After separating by agreement, with the husband binding himself to monthly allowances and transfer of properties, the husband went to Reno, Nevada and obtained a divorce for desertion. He then married another Filipina and returned. The wife sued for increased support, and all parties prayed that the Reno divorce be ratified and confirmed. Doctrine: The divorce is not recognized. Under Arts. 9 and 11 of the old Civil Code (now Arts. 15 and 17), the entire conduct of the parties shows a purpose to circumvent Philippine laws on divorce and secure a change of status for reasons and under conditions not authorized by our law. "Litigants by mutual agreement cannot compel the courts to approve of their own actions or permit the personal relations of the citizens of these Islands to be affected by decrees of foreign courts in a manner which our Government believes is contrary to public order and good morals." (Tolentino: it is "a serious question whether any foreign divorce relating to citizens of the Philippine Islands will be recognized... except for a cause and under conditions for which the courts of the Philippines would grant a divorce." That was the Act 2710 era, when absolute divorce existed here only for adultery or concubinage.)

Tenchavez v. Escaño (15 SCRA 355, November 29, 1965)

Pastor Tenchavez and Vicenta Escaño, both Filipinos, secretly married in Cebu in 1948 before a Catholic chaplain. They became estranged. Vicenta went to the US and obtained a divorce decree in Nevada ("extreme cruelty, entirely mental in character") while she was still a Filipino citizen. She later married an American and acquired US citizenship. Doctrine: The divorce is not recognized and gave her no right to remarry. At the time she obtained it she was still bound by Philippine law on family rights and duties and on status, condition and legal capacity (Art. 15). Under Art. 17 par. 3, our prohibitive laws are not rendered ineffective by foreign judgments. Recognizing such divorces would discriminate in favor of wealthy Filipinos able to go abroad. Her later marriage and cohabitation were technically intercourse with a person not her husband, and her refusal to perform her wifely duties and her denial of consortium entitled Tenchavez to legal separation and damages. Her later naturalization did not validate the divorce. (Note: this was a Philippine marriage of two Filipinos. Today's FC Art. 26 par. 2 still requires a mixed marriage, or at least an alien spouse at the time of the divorce.)

Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr. (139 SCRA 139, October 8, 1985)

Alice Reyes Van Dorn, a Filipina, and Richard Upton, an American, married in Hong Kong in 1972 and resided in the Philippines. In 1982 they were divorced in Nevada. Upton later sued in Pasay to have the Galleon Shop business in Manila declared conjugal property, with himself as administrator. Doctrine: The Nevada court had jurisdiction over Alice (who appeared in person) and over Upton (who, through counsel he authorized, agreed to the divorce on the ground of incompatibility, on the understanding that there was no community property). Owing to the nationality principle in Art. 15, only Philippine nationals are covered by the policy against absolute divorce. Aliens may obtain divorces abroad, which may be recognized here provided they are valid under their national law. Under American law the divorce dissolved the marriage, so Upton is no longer Alice's husband. He has no standing to sue as husband to control alleged conjugal assets, and he is estopped by his own representation before the Nevada court (no community property). To hold otherwise would leave the Filipina still bound to a man who is no longer her husband, owing him obedience and fidelity. This is the forerunner of FC Art. 26 par. 2.

Uribe (who obtained the divorce: Van Dorn, Pilapil, Manalo): Statement: "A divorce decree obtained abroad may only be recognized if it is obtained by a Filipino" is incorrect. In Republic v. Manalo (2018), "obtained" in Art. 26 par. 2 does not require that the alien spouse initiated the proceeding. A divorce initiated by the Filipino spouse against the alien spouse is covered. Uribe adds that even before Manalo the Court had recognized a divorce effectively obtained by the Filipina, in Van Dorn. Many say the foreign husband obtained it there, but three facts in the decision show the Filipina initiated it: (1) statements in the special power of attorney; (2) summons was served on the foreign spouse; (3) the foreign spouse was the one who filed the answer, because he was the one summoned. "If you read cases where the Court states that divorce decrees before Manalo [were] recognized [only when] obtained by the foreign spouse, NOT TRUE." He also corrects terminology: a single decision is a "case"; "jurisprudence" refers to the body of decisions or doctrines.
Pilapil v. Ibay-Somera (174 SCRA 653, June 30, 1989)

Imelda Manalaysay Pilapil, a Filipina, married Erich Geiling, a German, in Germany in 1979. Geiling obtained a divorce decree in Germany in January 1986. Months after the divorce, he filed complaints in Manila charging Imelda with adultery committed during the marriage. Doctrine: Under RPC Art. 344, adultery cannot be prosecuted except upon a sworn written complaint by the offended spouse, and the complainant's status as offended spouse must exist at the time of filing. Since Geiling had already obtained a divorce valid under his national law (recognized here per Van Dorn), he was no longer Imelda's husband. He had no legal standing to commence the adultery case "under the imposture that he was the offended spouse at the time he filed suit." The divorce was obtained by the German spouse and is given effect here.

Related

Del Socorro v. Van Wilsem (2014): a foreign national's obligation to support his child is governed by his national law (Art. 15 binds only Filipinos), but foreign law must be pleaded and proved. If it is not, the processual presumption applies and it is presumed the same as Philippine law. In any case, a foreign law that works injustice to Filipinos or is contrary to public policy will not be applied. R.A. 9262 also applies to him under Art. 14 (territoriality). San Luis v. San Luis (2007): a Filipino divorced by his alien spouse abroad may validly remarry. Board of Commissioners v. De la Rosa (1991): valid foreign marriages are valid here (lex loci celebrationis). Yao Kee v. Sy-Gonzales (1988): one who invokes a foreign marriage must prove both the fact of the marriage and its validity under the foreign law and custom.

(1998 Bar.) Juan, a Filipino residing in Tokyo, marries in Japan. What law governs his capacity to contract marriage?

Answer: Philippine law, by the nationality principle (Art. 15). Legal capacity follows Filipinos even when living abroad. Only the forms of the marriage follow Japanese law (lex loci celebrationis, Art. 17; FC Art. 26 par. 1). If Juan is, say, 17, he lacks capacity and the marriage is void under FC Art. 35(1) even if valid in Japan (FC Art. 26 par. 1 exception).

Ana (Filipina) married Ben (Filipino) in Manila. Ana moved to California, obtained a divorce there while still a Filipino, and then became a US citizen. Can Ben remarry? Can Ana's divorce be recognized?

Answer: No. When the divorce was obtained, both were Filipinos bound by Art. 15. The divorce cannot be recognized (Tenchavez; Art. 17 par. 3), so the marriage subsists, and remarriage by either would be bigamous. Contrast: if Ana had first become a US citizen and then obtained the divorce, Republic v. Orbecido (2005) applies Art. 26 par. 2 by reckoning citizenship at the time the divorce was obtained, and Ben could remarry after judicial recognition of the divorce and proof of the foreign law.

(1997 Bar.) Mario and Clara, both US citizens, divorced in the US. Mario returned and married Juana. Is the marriage valid? Is renvoi relevant?

Answer: Valid. The recognition of a foreign absolute divorce rests on the citizenship of the parties at the time it was granted. Both were US citizens, whose national law allows divorce, so the first marriage was validly dissolved (Art. 15 by implication; Van Dorn). Renvoi is not relevant. It arises where one country follows the domiciliary theory and the other the nationality theory on a question such as succession. That is not this case.

II.J-3. Lex rei sitae and the succession exception (Art. 16)

Art. 16, NCC. Real property as well as personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated.
However, intestate and testamentary successions, both with respect to the order of succession and to the amount of successional rights and to the intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions, shall be regulated by the national law of the person whose succession is under consideration, whatever may be the nature of the property and regardless of the country wherein said property may be found. (10a)
Art. 1039, NCC. Capacity to succeed is governed by the law of the nation of the decedent.

Par. 1: lex rei sitae (lex situs)

  • Real property: its legal and actual situs are identical. Every question affecting title to land is governed by the law of the place where it is situated: title, incidents, and the mode in which it may be charged or conveyed (Savigny bases this on a voluntary submission to local laws implied in holding property there).
  • Personal property: the old Code (Art. 10) followed mobilia sequuntur personam (movables follow the owner's nation). The new Code changed this to lex situs for movables too, following the modern tendency to make no distinction between movables and immovables (Tolentino). The old maxim arose when movables were mostly gold and jewels carried on the person. Personal property may be taxed where located even if the owner is not domiciled there. Under the present Code, whether property is movable or immovable is determined by the lex situs, and the right to be respected in possession of movables is governed by the law of the state where the property is found (a matter of public order).
  • (B29) Tayag v. Benguet Consolidated (1968): shares and assets of a Philippine corporation are subject to Philippine courts' orders. A Philippine corporation owes its existence to Philippine law, and a foreign domiciliary administrator's possession of the stock certificates cannot defeat the ancillary administrator's rights here.

Par. 2: succession follows the decedent's national law

  • Four matters governed by the national law of the decedent ("the person whose succession is under consideration"), whatever the nature of the property and wherever it is: (1) order of succession (who are the heirs and in what order); (2) amount of successional rights (shares and legitimes); (3) intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions; plus (4) capacity to succeed (Art. 1039). Tolentino adds questions of preterition, disinheritance and collation.
  • Not covered: the extrinsic validity (forms and solemnities) of wills. That is governed by Art. 17 and Arts. 815–817: the law of the place of execution, or the testator's national law, or the law of his domicile or residence, or Philippine law, depending on the article.
  • Reason: the unity of succession. Dean Capistrano: "there is only one will, express in testamentary and presumed in intestate succession. The oneness and universality of the inheritance cannot be divided or broken up merely because of the different countries where properties of the estate are situated."
  • Foreign law must be alleged and proved, or the processual presumption applies (Philippine law is presumed). A foreigner's testamentary condition that his property be distributed under Philippine law instead of his national law is illegal and considered not written (Miciano v. Brimo, 50 Phil. 867).
Uribe (Quiz 1, 2025 #5): "Real property as well as personal property is subject to the law of the country where it is situated." FALSE, even though it is Art. 16 par. 1 verbatim. It is not absolutely true because of the exception in par. 2 (succession). Explain: "While the statement reproduces par. 1, it is false because par. 2 subjects succession, as to order, amount and intrinsic validity, to the national law of the decedent, regardless of situs." In contrast, "The intrinsic validity of testamentary provisions shall be regulated by the national law of the person whose succession is under consideration" is TRUE (direct translation of par. 2).

Renvoi

  • Renvoi ("referring back") arises when the forum's conflict rule points to a foreign law (e.g., the national law under Art. 16 par. 2), and that foreign law's own conflict rule refers the matter back to the forum (remission, single renvoi) or to a third state (transmission). It typically occurs when one state follows the nationality theory and the other the domiciliary theory.
  • Aznar v. Garcia (1963): Edward Christensen, a citizen of California domiciled in the Philippines, left a will favoring one acknowledged natural daughter and giving only a legacy to another (Helen). Art. 16 points to California law. California's conflict rule for a non-domiciliary refers the matter to the law of the domicile, i.e., the Philippines. The Court accepted the renvoi and applied Philippine law, so Helen was entitled to her legitime.
  • Bellis v. Bellis (1967): Amos Bellis was a citizen and domiciliary of Texas. His illegitimate children claimed legitimes over his Philippine properties. Renvoi did not arise: Texas law applies as his national law, and since he was domiciled in Texas there was no referral back. Texas law has no forced heirship. Art. 16 par. 2 and Art. 1039 make the decedent's national law govern regardless of situs. Art. 17 par. 3 (public policy) does not override the specific rule of Art. 16 par. 2. The illegitimate children took no legitime.
  • Uribe (situationer): in a problem on a foreign decedent with Philippine properties, ask first where he was domiciled before invoking renvoi. In the Cayetano case renvoi was obiter. Renvoi is not always tied to domicile; for example, the lex rei sitae may be the law referred to.

X, a Filipino, died domiciled in New York, leaving land in New York, shares in Makati, and a will giving all to a friend. His legitimate son is preterited. Which law governs the son's legitime and the validity of the institution? Which law governs the New York land?

Answer: Philippine law, the national law of the decedent, governs the order of succession, the amount of successional rights (legitime) and the intrinsic validity of the will (preterition annuls the institution), "whatever may be the nature of the property and regardless of the country wherein said property may be found" (Art. 16 par. 2). The New York land is still subject to New York law on title and transfer formalities (lex situs, par. 1). As a matter of Philippine law, however, the succession to it is governed by Philippine law. (Whether a New York court will honor this is a matter of New York's own conflict rules.)

(2012 Bar, adapted.) A French national revoked his will in Japan, where he was domiciled. He then changed domicile to the Philippines and died. The revocation is valid under Japanese law but invalid under Philippine law. What law governs the revocation?

Answer: Japanese law. Revocation outside the Philippines by a person not domiciled here is valid if done according to the law of the place where the will was made or the law of the place where the testator was domiciled at the time (Art. 829). He was domiciled in Japan when he revoked. (UPLC suggested answer: Japanese law.)

Quiz traps (Art. 16): "...regulated by the national law of the heir" (F); "...law of the country where the decedent died/resided" (F); "...law of the situs if the property is real" (F: "whatever may be the nature of the property"). "The extrinsic validity of the will of X, a Filipino permanent resident of Canada, is governed by the law of the country where the will was executed" (TRUE as the Art. 17 answer; Art. 815 also allows him the forms of the country where he may be).

II.J-4. Forms and solemnities; public-policy limit (Art. 17) and suppletory effect (Art. 18)

Art. 17, NCC. The forms and solemnities of contracts, wills, and other public instruments shall be governed by the laws of the country in which they are executed.
When the acts referred to are executed before the diplomatic or consular officials of the Republic of the Philippines in a foreign country, the solemnities established by Philippine laws shall be observed in their execution.
Prohibitive laws concerning persons, their acts or property, and those which have for their object public order, public policy and good customs shall not be rendered ineffective by laws or judgments promulgated, or by determinations or conventions agreed upon in a foreign country. (11a)
Art. 18, NCC. In matters which are governed by the Code of Commerce and special laws, their deficiency shall be supplied by the provisions of this Code. (16a)

Par. 1: lex loci celebrationis (locus regit actum)

  • The extrinsic validity (forms and solemnities) of contracts, wills and other public instruments is governed by the law of the place of execution. Examples: a power of attorney executed in Germany is tested for formal validity under German law (German & Co. v. Donaldson). For marriage, FC Art. 26 par. 1 applies the same idea: marriages valid where celebrated are valid here, except those void under FC Arts. 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37 and 38.
  • Intrinsic validity of contracts is not expressly covered by the Code. Tolentino and Manresa, following Fiore, would apply: first, the law the parties designated; failing that, their common national law; otherwise the law of the place of perfection for the essence and nature of the obligation, and the law of the place of performance for its fulfillment (lex loci solutionis); and if these cannot be determined, the law of the domicile of the passive subject. Government v. Frank (13 Phil. 236): an American of age under Illinois law who contracted in Illinois with the Philippine Government could not plead minority under Philippine law. Remedies, admissibility of evidence and limitation of actions follow the law of the forum (lex fori).
  • (2012 Bar.) A Japanese and a Filipino contract in Thailand for services to be rendered in Singapore. For breach, the law of the place of performance applies: Singapore law (lex loci solutionis).

Par. 2: the consular exception

  • When the act is executed before Philippine diplomatic or consular officials abroad, Philippine solemnities must be observed. Example: two Filipinos in Tokyo execute a deed before the Philippine Consul, so Philippine law governs the solemnities (bank item P-35). Consular marriages between Filipinos abroad: FC Art. 10 (the consul performs the duties of the solemnizing officer and the local civil registrar).

Par. 3: prohibitive laws and public policy cannot be defeated abroad

  • Philippine prohibitive laws concerning persons, their acts or property, and laws whose object is public order, public policy and good customs, are not rendered ineffective by (a) foreign laws, (b) foreign judgments, or (c) foreign determinations or conventions. This is the public-policy exception to the application of foreign law. Our legislative policy on a subject cannot be defeated by a foreign judgment obtained by Filipinos, whether our law is "too strict or too liberal" (Barretto Gonzales v. Gonzales; Tenchavez).
  • Examples: a foreign divorce obtained by Filipino spouses (not recognized); a joint will executed abroad by Filipinos (prohibited even if allowed where executed, Art. 819); a US custody decree giving a child to a mother living with another man, not given effect here (Querubin v. Querubin); FC Art. 26 par. 1 exceptions (bigamous, incestuous and similar marriages of Filipinos abroad are void here even if valid where celebrated).
  • When Philippine courts refuse to apply an otherwise applicable foreign law (2012 Bar; bank P-31): (1) the foreign law, judgment or contract is contrary to a sound and important public policy of the forum; (2) the foreign law is penal; (3) the foreign law is procedural (procedure follows the lex fori); (4) the foreign law was not pleaded and proved (processual presumption); (5) it would work injustice to Filipinos (Del Socorro v. Van Wilsem). The location of the property outside the Philippines is not a ground; if anything it points to the foreign lex situs.
  • Limit: par. 3 does not override the specific rule of Art. 16 par. 2 on succession (Bellis). Absence of forced heirship in the decedent's national law is not contrary to our public policy for that purpose.

Art. 18: suppletory application of the Civil Code

  • The Code of Commerce and special laws govern their own matters. The Civil Code merely supplies their deficiencies. If the special law is not deficient, the Civil Code does not apply: a special law on a specific matter prevails over a general law, which applies only to fill gaps (Tolentino). Examples: Civil Code rules applied to fill gaps in the Insurance Law and the Carriage of Goods by Sea Act (meaning of "loss"). The Civil Code formalities of sale did not apply to large cattle, which had their own special law.
  • Exceptions (Code made primary, special law suppletory): Art. 1766 (common carriers: the Code governs; the Code of Commerce and special laws apply only to matters not regulated by it); Art. 1961 (usurious contracts governed by the Usury Law and special laws "so far as they are not inconsistent with this Code"); Art. 2237 (insolvency governed by special laws insofar as not inconsistent with the Code).

(Bank P-38.) "In matters governed by the Code of Commerce and special laws, the provisions of the Civil Code shall prevail over the latter in case of conflict." True or false?

Answer: FALSE. The Civil Code only supplies deficiencies (Art. 18). In a conflict, the special law or Code of Commerce prevails, subject to the reverse rule in Arts. 1766, 1961 and 2237.

Two Filipinos domiciled in Nevada marry there. Five years later they obtain a Nevada divorce by mutual consent and execute a property settlement approved by the Nevada court. Back in Manila, the husband asks a Philippine court to enforce the settlement as a final foreign judgment dissolving the marriage.

Answer: The divorce cannot be recognized. They were Filipinos bound by Art. 15, and under Art. 17 par. 3 our prohibitive laws on marriage cannot be rendered ineffective by a foreign judgment or convention (Barretto Gonzales; Tenchavez). The property settlement is enforceable only to the extent it is valid under our law on property relations, e.g., as a judicial separation of property that our court itself approves (FC Arts. 134–136). It cannot rest on a dissolved marriage.

Last-minute checklist

  • Art. 1: "This Act shall be known as the 'Civil Code of the Philippines.'" R.A. 386, approved June 18, 1949, effective August 30, 1950. Code Commission: E.O. 48 (Roxas, March 20, 1947). Spanish Civil Code effective here Dec. 7, 1889. Family Code (E.O. 209) effective Aug. 3, 1988.
  • Art. 2: "after fifteen days following the completion of their publication either in the Official Gazette or in a newspaper of general circulation in the Philippines, unless it is otherwise provided" (E.O. 200). Silent law = 16th day. "15 days after" = 15th day (Veridiano: June 29, 1979). "Unless otherwise provided" covers the period only; publication is indispensable (Tañada; Nagkakaisang Maralita). Publication must be in full.
  • Must publish: all statutes (incl. local and private), P.D.s/E.O.s, implementing rules (plus ONAR filing), city charters, Monetary Board circulars, penal regulations (Pesigan), burden-increasing rules (Hypermix, with notice and hearing). Need not: interpretative, internal, LOIs to subordinates, issuances to a specific class (Yaokasin), court rulings (MRCA).
  • Publication date = actual release to the public, not the printed OG issue date (Veridiano; Pesigan).
  • Art. 3: conclusive presumption; based on expediency and necessity (Zulueta). Only Philippine mandatory/prohibitory laws. Foreign law = fact. Mistake of fact excuses; mistake of law does not. Difficult question of law may be the basis of good faith (Arts. 526, 1334, 2154–2155). Kasilag: only excusable ignorance supports good faith.
  • Art. 4: "no retroactive effect, unless the contrary is provided." Doubt is resolved against retroactivity. Retroactive: procedural (Acosta, Dacudao), curative (Puzon), interpretative, new rights (Art. 2253), favorable penal (RPC 22, not habitual delinquents, even if serving final sentence), FC (Art. 256, no impairment of vested rights). Never: ex post facto laws, bills of attainder, impairment of contracts or vested rights (Uson: widow's rights vested at the 1945 death).
  • Uribe keys: "may be given retroactive effect even if it does not expressly so provide, EXCEPT ..." = none of the above. "cannot be given retroactive effect even if it expressly provides" = ex post facto law. 2025 "may NOT ... even if it expressly so provides: new rights/curative/interpretative/procedural/none" = Uribe's key (a) new rights (Uson); the bank keys (e).
  • Arts. 2266–2267 list specific Civil Code provisions given retroactive effect or applied to pending cases (e.g., Art. 29, Art. 33, compromise articles 2029–2031, quieting of title 476–481, reformation 1359–1369). Art. 2254: no vested right arises from illegal acts. Art. 2263: succession governed by the law at the time of death.
  • Art. 5: VOID (not voidable), "except when the law itself authorizes their validity." Manresa's exceptions: (1) non-essential violation (FC 4 par. 3 irregularity); (2) validity depends on the consent of the interested party (ratification of voidable marriage); (3) nullity declared but effects recognized (FC 54 children). Directory laws not covered. BPI v. IAC (Zshornack): dollar safekeeping in violation of CB Circular 20 is void, and the parties are in pari delicto.
  • Art. 6: waivable unless contrary to law, public order, public policy, morals, good customs, or prejudicial to a third person with a right recognized by law. Requisites: actually has the right, capacity, clear and unequivocal. Personality and family rights are not waivable; patrimonial rights are. Future support, legitime, inheritance and fraud cannot be waived. Support in arrears can (Art. 301; Art. 2035). De Borja: an heir's compromise or waiver after death but before probate is valid (rights vest at death). Watch the variation: a voluntary heir plus probate denied.
  • Art. 2035: no compromise on (1) civil status, (2) validity of marriage or legal separation, (3) any ground for legal separation, (4) future support, (5) jurisdiction of courts, (6) future legitime.
  • Art. 7: repealed only by subsequent laws (not "existing"). Disuse, custom or practice never excuses. Law vs. Constitution: "the former [law] void, the latter [Constitution] governs." Executive acts valid only when not contrary to the laws or the Constitution. Implied repeal is disfavored (same subject plus irreconcilable). A later general law does not repeal a special law. Repeal of an express repealer does not revive the old law; repeal of an implied repealer revives it (Admin. Code Secs. 21–22). Const. Art. XVIII Sec. 3: existing laws not inconsistent remain operative (Guingona v. Carague).
  • Art. 8: decisions "form a part of the legal system", not an independent source. Interpretation is part of the law from its enactment. Stare decisis is abandoned when the precedent has ceased to be beneficial and useful. Only SC decisions bind as precedent.
  • Art. 9: silence, obscurity or insufficiency: the court must still decide (customs, general principles, analogy). Criminal: no law, no crime, so acquit and report to the President through the DOJ (RPC Art. 5); likewise for clearly excessive penalties, without suspending execution.
  • Art. 10: only "in case of doubt" is it presumed the lawmaking body intended right and justice to prevail.
  • Art. 11: customs contrary to law, public order or public policy shall not be countenanced. Art. 12: a custom must be proved as a fact, according to the rules of evidence. Requisites: plurality, uniformity, general practice, long time, opinio juris, not contrary to law. Martinez v. Van Buskirk: long-acquiesced custom of leaving horses unattended while unloading meant no negligence.
  • Art. 13: year = 365 days; month = 30 days; day = 24 hours; night = sunset to sunrise. Months designated by name = actual days. Exclude the first day, include the last. No holiday rule in Art. 13 (Rule 22 and the Admin. Code have it; contractual periods have none). Primetown: Admin. Code Sec. 31, "year" = 12 calendar months (Art. 13 impliedly repealed as to years); "month" = 30 days unless a specific calendar month. Armigos: day = date. Namarco: 30-day month; years of 365 days.
  • Uribe date answers: Jan. 20, 2020 publication → Feb. 5, 2020. Feb. 15, 2016 → Mar. 2, 2016. One month from Feb. 1, 2021 → Mar. 3, 2021. From Feb. 1, 2016 → Mar. 2, 2016. One-month lease from Feb. 15, 2021 → due Mar. 17, so a demand on Mar. 15 is premature, there is no delay, and the lessee is not liable for the fortuitous event.
  • Art. 14: penal and public security laws bind "all who live or sojourn" in Philippine territory (territoriality, not citizenship), "subject to the principles of public international law and to treaty stipulations" (heads of state, diplomats, foreign public vessels, VFA). RPC Art. 2 lists the 5 extraterritorial cases.
  • Art. 15: family rights and duties, status, condition, legal capacity bind citizens, "even though living abroad" (nationality theory). Uribe: no exceptions. Capacity to succeed = Art. 1039, not Art. 15. Barretto Gonzales and Tenchavez: a divorce by Filipinos (or by a spouse still Filipino when it was obtained) is not recognized. Van Dorn: aliens' divorces valid under their national law are recognized; the alien ex-husband is estopped and lacks standing. Pilapil: a divorced German has no standing to file adultery (RPC 344). Manalo: "obtained" includes a divorce initiated by the Filipino spouse (Uribe: even Van Dorn's divorce was initiated by the Filipina).
  • Art. 16: par. 1, lex rei sitae for both real and personal property. Par. 2: order of succession, amount of successional rights, intrinsic validity follow the national law of the decedent, "whatever may be the nature of the property and regardless of the country wherein said property may be found." Uribe: a verbatim par. 1 statement is FALSE (par. 2 exception). Renvoi: Aznar (Californian domiciled in the PH, so Philippine law and the legitime apply); Bellis (Texan domiciled in Texas, so Texas law and no legitime). Miciano v. Brimo: a condition to apply PH law instead of national law is void.
  • Art. 17: forms and solemnities of contracts, wills, public instruments follow the law of the place of execution. Before PH consuls or diplomats abroad, PH solemnities. Prohibitive laws and public order/policy/good customs are not defeated by foreign laws, judgments or conventions. Nationality-principle MCQ "except" = validity of wills or forms and solemnities.
  • Art. 18: the Civil Code only supplies deficiencies of the Code of Commerce and special laws (it does not prevail over them). Reverse cases: Arts. 1766, 1961, 2237.
  • Foreign law must be alleged and proved, otherwise the processual presumption applies (presumed the same as PH law). Courts refuse foreign law that is contrary to public policy, penal, procedural, or unjust to Filipinos. Property abroad is not a ground.
