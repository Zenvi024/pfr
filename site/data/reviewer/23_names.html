Use of Surnames

Civil Code, Title XIII, Arts. 364–380 (syllabus XIX), with FC 176 as amended by R.A. 9255, C.A. 142 as amended by R.A. 6085 (aliases), and R.A. 9048 (administrative change of first name; see Civil Register). Which surname each kind of child and a married woman uses, identical names, judicial change of name, usurpation, pen names and aliases. Cases: Naldoza, Johnston, Llaneta, Tolentino v. CA, Legamia; from the class reviewer, Telmo, Wang, Ursua, Dapar, People v. Estrada, Santos v. Republic.

What a name is

Surnames of children (NCC 364–369; FC 176)

Art. 364, NCC. Legitimate and legitimated children shall principally use the surname of the father.
Art. 365, NCC. An adopted child shall bear the surname of the adopter.
Arts. 366–368, NCC (now read with FC 176). A natural child acknowledged by both parents shall principally use the surname of the father; if recognized by only one, the surname of the recognizing parent (366). Natural children by legal fiction shall principally employ the surname of the father (367). Illegitimate children referred to in article 287 shall bear the surname of the mother (368).
Art. 369, NCC. Children conceived before the decree annulling a voidable marriage shall principally use the surname of the father.
Art. 176, FC (as amended by R.A. 9255). Illegitimate children shall use the surname and shall be under the parental authority of their mother, and shall be entitled to support in conformity with this Code. However, illegitimate children may use the surname of their father if their filiation has been expressly recognized by the father through the record of birth appearing in the civil register, or when an admission in a public document or private handwritten instrument is made by the father. Provided, the father has the right to institute an action before the regular courts to prove non-filiation during his lifetime. The legitime of each illegitimate child shall consist of one-half of the legitime of a legitimate child.
Children of void marriages: they are illegitimate and use the mother's surname (FC 165, 176), except children of marriages void under Art. 36 (psychological incapacity) and of subsequent marriages void under Art. 53, who are legitimate (Art. 54) and use the father's surname. (Class reviewer note.)
Grande v. Antonio (G.R. No. 206248, February 18, 2014; class reviewer)

Grace Grande and Patricio Antonio, not married, had two sons, whom Antonio later acknowledged. He sued for custody and to have the boys' surname changed to Antonio; the lower courts ordered the change. Held: reversed on the surname. Under Art. 176 as amended by R.A. 9255, the illegitimate child "may" use the father's surname: the word is permissive, so the father has no right to compel it, and the choice belongs to the child (through the mother while a minor). The IRR provision making the use of the father's surname mandatory once recognized is void. Parental authority and custody remain with the mother unless she is unfit.

Johnston v. Republic (7 SCRA 1040, 1963)

A married woman adopted a minor alone, her husband (Johnston) merely consenting, and the child was to bear the surname "Johnston". Held: the child bears the adopter's maiden surname, not the husband's. The adopter's surname in NCC 341 (now 365) "refers to the adopter's own original family surname and not to her surname acquired by virtue of marriage. As petitioner-appellant made the adoption without concurrence of her husband, her name as adopter was her maiden name." Using the husband's surname "would mislead the public into believing that the child has also been adopted by the husband, and, later, if questions of successional rights arise, the husband's consent to the adoption might be presented to prove that he had actually joined in the adoption."

Naldoza v. Republic (112 SCRA 568, 1982; see also Paternity)

A mother whose husband had abandoned the family sought to change her legitimate sons' surname to her own. Held: denied. Using only the mother's surname "might create the impression that the minors are illegitimate". "The child should, and in the course of time must, know of his parentage. If, when he fully appreciates the circumstances and is capable of selecting a name for himself, he wants to use his mother's surname only and to avoid using his father's surname, then he should be the one to apply for a change of surname."

Tolentino (Arts. 364–369): a legitimate child has the right to bear the surnames of both parents, but the father's is principally used; the law does not forbid adding the surnames of other ascendants. When both parents recognize a natural child, the father's surname is principally used, even if the mother recognized first, because it hides the illegitimacy; with the FC's abolition of "natural children", this should apply to all illegitimate children. Illegitimate children cannot be restrained from using what may be their father's surname: the legitimate children have a right to it, but not "monopolistic proprietary control" (Osmeña de Valencia). Children of an annulled voidable marriage, being legitimate, may also use the mother's surname.

Married women, widows, and after annulment or legal separation (NCC 370–373)

Art. 370, NCC. A married woman may use:
(1) Her maiden first name and surname and add her husband's surname, or
(2) Her maiden first name and her husband's surname, or
(3) Her husband's full name, but prefixing a word indicating that she is his wife, such as "Mrs."
Art. 371, NCC. In case of annulment of marriage, and the wife is the guilty party, she shall resume her maiden name and surname. If she is the innocent spouse, she may resume her maiden name and surname. However, she may choose to continue employing her former husband's surname, unless:
(1) The court decrees otherwise, or
(2) She or the former husband is married again to another person.
Art. 372, NCC. When legal separation has been granted, the wife shall continue using her name and surname employed before the legal separation.
Art. 373, NCC. A widow may use the deceased husband's surname as though he were still living, in accordance with article 370.
Tolentino v. CA (162 SCRA 66, 1988)

Arturo Tolentino's first wife, Consuelo David, obtained a divorce from him in 1943 under the law then in force during the Japanese occupation, but kept using the surname "Tolentino". His second wife, Constancia, sued to enjoin her. Held: no injunction. The action had prescribed, and in any case "the use of the surname Tolentino does not impinge on the rights of the petitioner." Considering the respondent's age, the burden of reconstituting documents, and that "while still not remarried, [she] will have to use a surname different from the surnames of her own children", the Court found it "just and equitable to leave things as they are, there being no actual legal injury to the petitioner save a deep hurt to her feelings which is not a basis for injunctive relief." The wife's right to the husband's surname is not exclusive; mere use of the surname is not usurpation of the status of wife (distinguishing Silva v. Peralta, where the defendant held herself out as the man's wife).

Telmo v. Republic (class reviewer)

Milagros Llerena Telmo petitioned to change her husband's surname, as she used it, from "Telmo" to "Thelmo"; the husband only executed an affidavit of no objection. Held: denied. A married woman uses her husband's surname by virtue of the marriage; a judicial change of its spelling must be initiated by the husband, the real party in interest, and her reasons were insufficient.

Remo v. Secretary of Foreign Affairs (G.R. No. 169202, March 5, 2010; not in your sources)

A married woman whose passport bore her husband's surname wanted to revert to her maiden name in her renewed passport while the marriage subsisted. Held: denied. Under Art. 370 the use of the husband's surname is optional, so she could have kept her maiden name from the start; but having used his surname in her passport, she may revert only in the cases allowed by the Philippine Passport Act (death of the husband, divorce, annulment or nullity).

Tolentino (Arts. 370–373): in some countries the wife must take the husband's family name; ours says "may", so it is permissive, in line with the principle that surnames indicate descent. The wife cannot be prevented from using the husband's surname, but she cannot restrain others from using it either (Baumann v. Baumann, N.Y.: the second "wife" in a void marriage could keep calling herself Mrs. Baumann; the first wife's hurt feelings are not ground for an injunction). After legal separation she must continue using her married name, because the vinculum is not severed; a widow's use is optional because there is no more vinculum.

Identical names (NCC 374–375)

Art. 374, NCC. In case of identity of names and surnames, the younger person shall be obliged to use such additional name or surname as will avoid confusion.
Art. 375, NCC. In case of identity of names and surnames between ascendants and descendants, the word "Junior" can be used only by a son. Grandsons and other direct male descendants shall either:
(1) Add a middle name or the mother's surname, or
(2) Add the Roman numerals II, III, and so on.
Quiz traps: "A grandson with the same name as his grandfather may use 'Junior'" → false: only a son; grandsons add a middle name or the mother's surname, or Roman numerals. "The older of two persons with identical names must add a name" → false: the younger.

Change of name (NCC 376; Rule 103; R.A. 9048)

Art. 376, NCC. No person can change his name or surname without judicial authority.
Llaneta v. Agrava (57 SCRA 29, 1974)

Teresita Llaneta was born to Atanacia Llaneta after the death of Atanacia's husband, Serafin Ferrer, so she could not be his child; but she was raised by the Ferrers and known all her life as "Teresita Ferrer". She petitioned to change her surname to Ferrer; the judge denied it because it would give the false impression of a family relationship. Held: granted. "The principle that disallows change of name as would give the false impression of family relationship … remains valid but only to the extent that the proposed change of name would in great probability cause prejudice or future mischief to the family whose surname it is that is involved or to the community in general." She had always used "Ferrer"; her records bore it; Serafin's nearest kin approved. "A sudden shift at this time … would result in confusion among the persons and entities she deals with and entail endless and vexatious explanations."

In re Change of Name of Julian Lin Carulasan Wang (G.R. No. 159966, March 30, 2005; class reviewer)

Parents of a minor born to a Filipino mother and a Chinese father, planning to move to Singapore, sought to drop his middle name "Carulasan" because Singaporeans do not use middle names and he would be teased. Held: denied. The reason is not compelling; the middle name identifies his maternal lineage, and the request was a matter of convenience. The Court restated the five characteristics of a name.

Santos v. Republic (2021; class reviewer)

Francis Luigi G. Santos, the illegitimate son of Bong Revilla, was adopted by his stepfather and became a Santos; he petitioned under Rule 103 to change his surname to "Revilla", citing his desire to associate with his biological father's family, his "true identity", and his screen name. Held: denied. "Rule 103 is an action to change a person's whole name, while Rule 108 is to correct errors or cancel entries in the Civil Register." A change of name is a privilege addressed to the court's discretion; it needs a proper and compelling reason, and these reasons do not override the legal effect of the adoption.

Tolentino (Art. 376): sufficient causes: (1) the name is ridiculous, dishonorable or very hard to write or pronounce; (2) the change follows a change of status; (3) it avoids confusion; (4) a sincere desire to adopt a Filipino name. Not enough: mere whim, a condition in a will or donation, or merely having been known by another name. A change of name does not alter family relations, status, capacity or citizenship; it changes only the label. It is a proceeding in rem; strict compliance with publication is jurisdictional, and an error in spelling the petitioner's name in the publication is substantial. When a father changes his name, his children's names are unaffected: adults must petition themselves; minors' names may be changed on the father's petition if the same justification exists.

Usurpation, unauthorized use, pen names, aliases (NCC 377–380; C.A. 142)

Art. 377, NCC. Usurpation of a name and surname may be the subject of an action for damages and other relief.
Art. 378, NCC. The unauthorized or unlawful use of another person's surname gives a right of action to the latter.
Art. 379, NCC. The employment of pen names or stage names is permitted, provided it is done in good faith and there is no injury to third persons. Pen names and stage names cannot be usurped.
Art. 380, NCC. Except as provided in the preceding article, no person shall use different names and surnames.
C.A. 142, Sec. 1 (as amended by R.A. 6085). Except as a pseudonym solely for literary, cinema, television, radio or other entertainment purposes and in athletic events where the use of pseudonym is a normally accepted practice, no person shall use any name different from the one with which he was registered at birth in the office of the local civil registry, or with which he was baptized for the first time, or, in case of an alien, with which he was registered in the bureau of immigration upon entry; or such substitute name as may have been authorized by a competent court…. The name shall comprise the patronymic name and one or two surnames.
Legamia v. IAC (131 SCRA 479, 1984)

Corazon Legamia lived for almost 20 years with Emilio Reyes, who was married to another; he introduced her as his wife and she used the name "Corazon L. Reyes". After his death she signed as "Corazon L. Reyes" in claiming benefits for their son Michael, and was convicted of using an alias under C.A. 142. Held: acquitted. "It is not uncommon in Philippine society for a woman to represent herself as the wife and use the name of the man she is living with despite the fact that the man is married to another woman. The practice, to be sure, is not encouraged but neither is it unduly frowned upon…. None of the women has been charged of violating the C.A. No. 142 because ours is not a bigoted but a tolerant and understanding society." She used the name "without any sinister purpose or personal material gain in mind", for the benefit of her son; "the lawmakers could not have meant to criminalize what Corazon had done especially because some of them probably had their own Corazons."

Ursua v. CA (G.R. No. 112170, April 10, 1996; class reviewer)

Cesario Ursua, asked by his lawyer to get a copy of a complaint against him from the Ombudsman, signed the visitors' logbook as "Oscar Perez" once, out of reluctance to be identified. He was convicted under C.A. 142. Held: acquitted. An alias is a name used publicly and habitually, usually in business transactions, in addition to one's real name; a single isolated use, with no sign of intent to be known by it, is not punishable. "A man's name is simply the sound or sounds by which he is commonly designated by his fellows."

People v. Estrada (G.R. Nos. 164368–69, April 2, 2009; class reviewer)

Former President Estrada was charged with illegal use of the alias "Jose Velarde" for signing bank documents to open a trust account on February 4, 2000. Held: the demurrer to evidence was properly granted. Under Ursua, the use of an alias must be public and habitual; the information limited the charge to a single occasion, and use in a bank transaction covered by the secrecy of bank deposits (R.A. 1405) is not public. Legal names "comprise the patronymic name and one or two surnames."

Dapar v. Biascan (G.R. No. 141880, September 27, 2004; class reviewer)

A man's lawful wife sued his common-law partner, who had used his surname, for usurpation. Held: to be actionable under Art. 377, usurpation requires: (1) actual use of another's name by the defendant; (2) the use is unauthorized; and (3) the use is to designate personality or identify a person.

Tolentino (Arts. 377–380): the right to a name is a right of personality, protected even for a person without a good reputation. A pseudonym is "a conventional fictitious name freely chosen by a person to disguise his personality"; it is protected only when well known as the designation of a particular writer or artist in his field, and may be used only in that activity, never in relations with the State.

Quiz traps (surnames): "A married woman must use her husband's surname" → false (Art. 370: "may"). "A legally separated wife may resume her maiden name" → false (Art. 372: shall continue). "An innocent wife in an annulled marriage must resume her maiden name" → false: the guilty wife must; the innocent wife may choose. "An illegitimate child recognized by the father shall use the father's surname" → false: may (R.A. 9255; Grande). "An adopted child takes the surname the adopter acquired by marriage" → false (Johnston). "Any change of name requires a court order" → false since R.A. 9048 (first names and clerical errors). "A single use of a false name is a violation of the anti-alias law" → false (Ursua; Estrada).
