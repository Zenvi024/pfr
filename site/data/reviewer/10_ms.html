Marriage Settlements, Governing Law and Donations by Reason of Marriage

Title IV, Family Code, Chapters 1–2, Arts. 74–87 (syllabus X.A–C). Covers the order of governing rules (Art. 74), the default regime (Art. 75), the form, timing, parties and registration of marriage settlements (Arts. 76–79, 81), the law that governs the property relations (Art. 80), donations by reason of marriage (Arts. 82–86), and the prohibition on donations between spouses and common-law partners (Art. 87; Matabuena v. Cervantes). Built from Sir Uribe's CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 and its class discussion, his old quizzes, the recitation situationers, the bar questions, and Tolentino's commentary on the Family Code (green boxes; where Tolentino conflicts with Sir's answers, a dashed box says which view to use on his quizzes).

How Uribe tests Arts. 74–87

This chapter supplies most of the "pure codal" items in his property relations quizzes. Every item in Part One of CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 (items 1–10) came from Arts. 74–91. He tests it in four ways:

  1. One-word alterations of the codal. "in writing" becomes "in a public instrument"; "present property" becomes "future property"; "rendered void" becomes "revocable"; "executed before" becomes "may be made before"; "in favor of" becomes "made by"; "other than the absolute community" becomes "absolute community"; "resolutory" becomes "suspensive"; "donee" becomes "donor"; "consent" becomes "advice".
  2. Dropping a qualifier. "The property relations … shall be governed primarily by the Family Code" (no: marriage settlements come first). "In the absence of a marriage settlement … ACP shall govern" (no: only for marriages on or after August 3, 1988).
  3. Date-of-marriage problems. Every facts-based item first asks: when was the marriage celebrated (before or after August 3, 1988), and was there a valid marriage settlement? Before the Family Code, the default was the conjugal partnership of gains (CPG). After it, the default is the absolute community of property (ACP).
  4. Donation classification. Is it a donation propter nuptias (DPN), an ordinary donation, a donation between spouses during the marriage (void), or a donation between common-law partners (void)? The answer decides the form, the grounds for revocation, and validity.
Uribe (Quiz 4 discussion): "If your answer is False, discuss why it is false and not why it is true." A correct answer with a wrong reason gets partial credit at most. In Quiz 4 he asked whether the class agreed with an answer, and then separately whether they agreed with the explanation: "pwedeng tama ang explanation pero mali ang sagot niya."

Art. 74: the order of governing rules

Art. 74, FC. The property relationship between husband and wife shall be governed in the following order:
(1) By marriage settlements executed before the marriage;
(2) By the provisions of this Code; and
(3) By the local custom. (118)

The spouses' own contract (the marriage settlement) comes first. The Family Code supplies whatever the settlement does not cover and fills in when there is no settlement. Local custom applies only when both are silent, and it must be proved as a fact (Art. 12, Civil Code).

Tolentino (definition and theory of the Code): The marriage settlement, also called an ante-nuptial contract, is "an agreement entered into before marriage, and in consideration thereof, between an intended husband and wife, by which the enjoyment or devolution of property is regulated" (Madden). Manresa calls it a contract by those about to marry "to establish the conditions of their conjugal partnership with respect to present and future property." The Code adopts freedom of stipulation: the law is merely suppletory to the will of the parties, so the ACP is only subsidiary. It governs only when the spouses have not provided another system. Stipulations are read together: where the settlement set up a community of all present and future estate, but also provided that if either died without issue his or her contribution would revert to the survivor, the husband took the entire estate when the wife died without issue (Fabre v. Sparks, a Louisiana case).
Quiz trap (CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #2; old Quiz #3 pair; Quiz 3 key #12): "The property relationship between husband and wife shall be governed primarily by the Family Code." False. The marriage settlement executed before the marriage governs first. The mirror statement is true: "The property relation between husband and wife shall be primarily governed by the marriage settlement executed before marriage" (Quiz 3 key #12).
Uribe (Quiz 4 #2 discussion): "If the marriage was solemnized in 1985 without a marriage settlement, what will be the property regime?" CPG. "Which rules on CPG apply, the Civil Code or the Family Code?" The Civil Code, but if the marriage still subsists after August 3, 1988, the Family Code rules on CPG also apply insofar as they do not impair vested rights (Art. 256). In other words, if the property relations are determined today, the Family Code rules on CPG are applied as long as no vested right is impaired.
Art. 256, FC. This Code shall have retroactive effect insofar as it does not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws.
Art. 105, FC (2nd par.). The provisions of this Chapter [CPG] shall also apply to conjugal partnerships of gains already established between spouses before the effectivity of this Code, without prejudice to vested rights already acquired in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws, as provided in Article 256.

UP BOC 2011 Bar MCQ: What happens to the property regimes that were subsisting under the New Civil Code when the Family Code took effect? (A) The original property regimes are immutable and remain effective. (B) Those enjoying specific regimes under the NCC may adopt ACP. (C) Those married under the NCC who did not choose any regime shall now be governed by ACP. (D) They are superseded by the Family Code, which has retroactive effect.

A. A pre-August 3, 1988 marriage without a settlement stays under the CPG. The Family Code does not convert it to ACP (Art. 105, 2nd par.; Art. 256). The regime itself does not change. What changes are the rules applied to it (the Family Code's CPG chapter) where no vested right is impaired. C is the tempting wrong answer.

Art. 75: freedom to choose a regime, and the default regime

Art. 75, FC. The future spouses may, in the marriage settlements, agree upon the regime of absolute community, conjugal partnership of gains, complete separation of property, or any other regime. In the absence of a marriage settlement, or when the regime agreed upon is void, the system of absolute community of property as established in this Code shall govern. (119a)
  • Options: ACP, CPG, complete separation of property, or "any other regime" (a mixed or customized regime), within the limits of the Code (Art. 1: "marriage settlements may fix the property relations during the marriage within the limits provided by this Code").
  • The default applies in two cases: (1) no marriage settlement; (2) the regime agreed upon is void (e.g., an oral settlement; a minor's settlement without the parents as parties; a settlement executed after the wedding).
  • Before August 3, 1988 (Civil Code Art. 119), the default was the conjugal partnership of gains. A void settlement in a 1987 marriage therefore leads to CPG, not ACP.
Tolentino (the principal regimes): No list can be complete, because the spouses can combine regimes, but the main forms are: (1) absorption, where the husband owns all of the wife's present and future property because her personality is absorbed in his ("complete appropriation" or "complete merger"); (2) community, either absolute or universal (all present and future property, however acquired, forms one patrimony) or relative or limited (a community of movables, a community of acquisitions or gains, or a community of future property), with each spouse keeping some exclusive property; and (3) separation, either with a single administration by the husband, the dotal system (a dowry delivered to the husband in administration and usufruct, while the wife keeps her paraphernal property), or absolute separation. Under the "any other regime" clause, the spouses may adopt any of these or a mix.
Tolentino (renouncing the community without naming a regime): The Spanish Civil Code (Art. 1364) imposed the dotal system on spouses who renounced the community without stating another regime. Neither the Civil Code nor the Family Code has an equivalent rule. Tolentino would apply Art. 74: first local custom; failing that, the parties' intention as shown by their conduct during the marriage; failing that, the court adopts the most equitable system closest to the parties' practice, protecting creditors, and may borrow the rules on ordinary partnership. The ACP cannot be imposed, because the spouses expressly renounced it.
Tolentino (void stipulations): Because a settlement is a contract, it is subject to Art. 1306 NCC. Void are stipulations (1) contrary to the nature and purposes of marriage, to individual liberty, or to morals and good customs: e.g., that the union may be dissolved for causes other than death or annulment; relieving a spouse of the moral duties or of the duty to support and educate the children; a money penalty for infidelity; a waiver of the right to sue for legal separation; the "purchase" of a spouse or of a parent's consent; forbidding the survivor to remarry; isolating the wife; (2) contrary to prohibitory or mandatory laws, since laws of public order cannot be modified, though suppletory rules can; and (3) derogatory to the authority of the spouses or to public order: e.g., that the wife need not follow the family domicile, that the wife alone shall exercise parental authority, or that one spouse alone shall support the children. The spouses may agree that the husband or the wife will administer the common property, but they cannot give the administration to a third person (contrary to morals and public policy). A stipulation giving all the profits to one spouse is generally void (cf. Art. 1799 NCC), but Tolentino says it is valid if that spouse also bears all the burdens of the marriage, since Art. 75 allows "any other regime" and the community is not formed for gain. Effect: a void stipulation does not void the whole settlement. Related stipulations fall with it; unrelated ones survive (Manresa).
Quiz trap (CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #3): "In the absence of a marriage settlement, or when the regime agreed upon is void, the system of absolute community of property shall govern." False per the Quiz 4 discussion. The statement drops "as established in this Code", and as stated it is not true of marriages celebrated before August 3, 1988, which default to CPG. Sir: "If your answer is False, discuss why it is false … it is not the system of ACP … if the marriage is solemnized before the effectivity of the FC."
Quiz trap (Quiz 4 #10; old pair item): "X died yesterday survived by Y, his widow. If X and Y did not execute a marriage settlement, the regime that shall govern their property relations shall be absolute community of property regime." False. The date of the marriage is not given. If they married before August 3, 1988, the regime is CPG. Compare the variant "X died in 1987 … the regime … shall be CPG", which is true (a man who died in 1987 must have married under the Civil Code).
Uribe (situation 2, CivRev 2025): the future spouses verbally agreed on ACP. Is it advisable to execute a marriage settlement anyway? Only if they want to stipulate something different from the default, such as excluding specific property. "It is not about what the law provides; it is what the parties would want to exclude." If they want nothing different, there is no need for a settlement, because ACP is the default anyway.

Arts. 76–77: when and how a marriage settlement is made

Art. 76, FC. In order that any modification in the marriage settlements may be valid, it must be made before the celebration of the marriage, subject to the provisions of Articles 66, 67, 128, 135 and 136. (121)
Art. 77, FC. The marriage settlements and any modification thereof shall be in writing, signed by the parties and executed before the celebration of the marriage. They shall not prejudice third persons unless they are registered in the local civil registry where the marriage contract is recorded as well as in the proper registries of properties. (122a)

Requisites for validity (between the parties)

  1. In writing. A private writing is enough. The Family Code does not require a public instrument.
  2. Signed by the parties (and by the persons named in Arts. 78–79 where they apply).
  3. Executed before the celebration of the marriage.
  4. Within the limits of the Code (Art. 1), and not contrary to law, morals, good customs, public order or public policy (Art. 1306, Civil Code).

Requisite for binding third persons

Registration in both (a) the local civil registry where the marriage contract is recorded and (b) the proper registries of property. An unregistered settlement is valid between the spouses but does not prejudice third persons. To be registrable in the registry of property, the settlement must in practice be notarized (a public document).

Tolentino (form and registration): The Spanish Civil Code (Art. 1321) required a public instrument for validity. The Civil Code and the Family Code require only a writing. Tolentino reads this as requiring a private writing "so as to be enforceable between the parties; it may even be oral, in so far as the parties are concerned. But in order to be effective against third persons, it must be in a public instrument to be recorded in the Registry of Property, and in the local civil registry." Registration exists so that third persons who later deal with either spouse know the regime, each spouse's power to dispose, and the extent of the guaranty for obligations. He adds two cautions. Dropping the public-instrument requirement removed the certainty of the date, so a private settlement could be antedated to change the regime after the wedding (heirs are not "third persons" protected by registration). And an evident error in the date (a date after the wedding) does not void a settlement proved to have been executed before the marriage; the exact date need not be proved if it is shown to precede the marriage.
Tolentino vs. the bar/Uribe view on oral settlements. Tolentino's "it may even be oral, in so far as the parties are concerned" treats the writing like a Statute of Frauds requirement. The UP BOC answer and Sir's discussion treat an oral settlement under the Family Code as void (Art. 77 is a requisite for validity), so the default regime applies. For Uribe's quizzes, follow Uribe: after August 3, 1988, an oral settlement is void. Tolentino's view is useful for a pre-FC marriage (Uribe situation 3) and for any guide question on the Statute of Frauds (Art. 1403(2)(c), "an agreement made in consideration of marriage, other than a mutual promise to marry"). Tolentino also notes, citing Arts. 1356–1357 NCC: if the settlement is merely oral, neither party can compel the other to execute a writing if the other objects to oral proof; if it is in a private writing, either party may compel the execution of a public instrument so it can be registered, even during the marriage (he disagrees with Sanchez Roman, who limited this to before the wedding).
Quiz traps (all graded False in his quizzes): (1) "The marriage settlements and any modification thereof shall be in a public instrument" (Quiz comp TF4; Quiz #3 pair 4; TF compilation #27; Quiz 3 key #2). (2) "… shall be in writing and executed during the celebration of the marriage" (pair 4 variant). (3) "… shall be in writing, signed by the parties and may be made before the celebration of the marriage" (CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #5). The faithful version is true: "The marriage settlements and any modification thereof shall be in writing, signed by the parties and executed before the celebration of the marriage" (Quiz #1 TF2).
Uribe (Quiz 4 #5 discussion): one student answered False because a settlement can be modified during the marriage (by judicial separation). Sir agreed it is False but not with that reason: "Ang mali ay marriage settlement. Hindi tungkol sa ibang paraan ng dissolution of property relations." The defect is "may be made", because executing it before the celebration is a mandatory requirement of law. Changes after the wedding (Arts. 66, 67, 128, 135, 136) are not modifications of the settlement under Art. 76. They are judicial remedies that replace the regime.

Modification after the marriage: only by court action

Art. 76 cross-refers to the five exceptions, each of which requires court involvement:

  • Art. 66: reconciliation after legal separation. The spouses may revive their former regime or keep the separation, by agreement and a court order.
  • Art. 67: the agreement to revive the former property regime must be executed under oath and filed with the court.
  • Art. 128: abandonment or failure to comply with obligations; the aggrieved spouse may petition for receivership, judicial separation of property, or sole administration.
  • Art. 135: judicial separation of property for sufficient cause.
  • Art. 136: voluntary judicial separation of property (joint petition approved by the court).
Tolentino (invariability of the regime): Whatever regime the spouses adopt becomes unchangeable once the marriage is celebrated and lasts until it is dissolved. The spouses cannot, during the marriage, drop their settlement and substitute the ACP, because the ACP is only suppletory. The only exceptions are those the law allows: Arts. 66–67 (reconciliation after legal separation, where the separation continues unless the spouses agree to revive the former regime), Art. 128 (abandonment), and Arts. 135–136 (judicial separation of property, for cause or voluntary). After dissolution, the survivor and the heirs may agree to divide the property differently from the settlement. That is valid between them but cannot prejudice third persons, such as the husband's creditors.
Tolentino (effectivity; no period or condition): The settlement is accessory to the marriage, so it takes effect at the moment of the celebration. The regime cannot be subjected to a suspensive or resolutory period or condition. Arts. 88 and 107 make the ACP and the CPG begin at the celebration and void any contrary stipulation, and a period or condition would imply a change of regime during the marriage, which violates invariability. Goyena and De Buen argued that a suspensive condition should be allowed because its fulfillment retroacts to the wedding. Most writers (Manresa, De Diego, Bonet, Castan) reject this: the spouses' property relations, especially as to third persons, cannot be left uncertain even briefly.
Spouses cannot dissolve their regime by private agreement. In the 2005 bar problem, Gabby and Mila married in 1990 under a registered CPG settlement and, in 1995, signed a private document dissolving the CPG and adopting complete separation. The agreement is void. The CPG subsists because "in the absence of an express declaration in the marriage settlements, the separation of property between spouses during the marriage shall not take place except by judicial order" (Art. 134). The properties Gabby later registered in his name alone are presumed conjugal (Art. 116). See also UP BOC 2012 MCQ: separation of property during the marriage may take place only "upon order of the court."

1991 Bar: Patricio and Rowena agreed on a handwritten marriage settlement providing (1) a CPG; (2) each donates to the other 50% of his or her present property; (3) Rowena administers the CPG; (4) neither may sue for annulment or nullity. Both signed before two witnesses, without notarization. (a) Valid as to form? Registrable? (b) Are the stipulations valid?

(a) Valid as to form, because it is in writing and signed (Art. 77). It cannot be registered in the registry of property because it is not a public document; to make it registrable, it must be acknowledged before a notary public. (b) Stipulations 1 and 3 are valid. Stipulation 2 is valid only up to one-fifth of each spouse's present property and void as to the excess (Art. 84; the regime is CPG, "other than ACP"). Stipulation 4 is void, because the validity of marriage is not subject to stipulation (Art. 1; Art. 2035, Civil Code). The settlement only becomes effective upon the celebration of the marriage (Art. 81).

2020–21 Bar: A prenuptial agreement provides that the marriage shall be valid for only five years but may be renewed by mutual consent negotiated at least six months before it expires. Valid?

No. A marriage settlement may fix only the property relations, "within the limits provided by this Code" (Art. 1). A term on the marriage itself contradicts its nature as a "special contract of permanent union" whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation.

UP BOC 2011 MCQ: X and Y agreed verbally before their marriage (a) on the paternity of Y's illegitimate child and (b) on the economic regime. Is the verbal agreement valid?

A: No, because a marriage settlement must be in writing to be valid. Under the Family Code, an oral settlement is void, not merely unenforceable. With no valid settlement, Art. 75 makes ACP the regime.

Uribe (situation 3, CivRev 2025): "The marriage was solemnized in 1988, and days before, they verbally agreed on separation of property." One student said the oral settlement was void (Family Code); another said it was unenforceable (Civil Code statute of frauds). Sir: neither is correct, because the facts do not give the exact date. Before August 3, 1988, the Civil Code governs (CC Art. 122 required writing, and the old rule treated oral agreements in consideration of marriage under the statute of frauds). From August 3, 1988, the Family Code governs, and an oral settlement is void under Art. 77. Lesson: if the date decides the answer and the facts do not give it, answer in the alternative.

Arts. 78–79: settlements by minors and persons under disability

Art. 78, FC. A minor who according to law may contract marriage may also execute his or her marriage settlements, but they shall be valid only if the persons designated in Article 14 to give consent to the marriage are made parties to the agreement, subject to the provisions of Title IX of this Code. (120a)
Art. 79, FC. For the validity of any marriage settlement executed by a person upon whom a sentence of civil interdiction has been pronounced or who is subject to any other disability, it shall be indispensable for the guardian appointed by a competent court to be made a party thereto. (123a)
  • The persons designated in Art. 14 must be made parties to the agreement (they sign). Mere knowledge or consent outside the document is not enough.
  • RA 6809 (effective December 18, 1989) lowered the age of majority to 18. Since then, no one who may marry is a "minor", so Art. 78 operates only on settlements executed before that date by persons 18 to 20 (and on persons under 18 before the FC, when the marrying age was 14/16). Art. 236 as amended by RA 6809 keeps parental consent for marriage at 18 to 21 (Art. 14), but Art. 78 speaks of a "minor".
  • Art. 79: a person under civil interdiction (RPC 34) or under another disability (e.g., insanity, prodigality, deaf-mute who cannot read or write) needs the court-appointed guardian as a party.
Tolentino (Art. 78 after RA 6809): RA 6809 lowered the age of majority to 18 but kept parental consent for marriages of persons 18 to 21. Tolentino concludes that Art. 78 remains in force and that "minor" there must be understood as "below 21 years old". The minor must act personally, not through the parents or guardian; the parents authorize. Parental intervention means signing: it is not enough that the parents consent. In Mirasol v. Lim (59 Phil. 701), the antenuptial contract of an 18-year-old girl was held invalid because her mother (the father being dead) did not sign it, even though the mother was present when it was signed. As to who must sign, Tolentino follows the order in Art. 14 (father, mother, surviving parent, guardian), which the Title IX rules on joint parental authority may modify (e.g., the father has been deprived of parental authority, or a court has designated the mother or a guardian).
Conflict: Tolentino's "below 21" reading would make the December 1989 settlement in Uribe situation 4 void even after RA 6809 took effect. Sir's answer turns on the date: before December 18, 1989, B was a minor and the settlement without the parents is void; on or after it, B was of age and Art. 78 no longer applies. For Uribe's quizzes, follow Uribe, but cite Tolentino as the contrary view if the question invites discussion.
Tolentino (Art. 79 disabilities): The disabilities that restrict capacity to act (Arts. 38–39 NCC and the Rules of Court) include civil interdiction, hospitalized lepers, prodigals, deaf-mutes who cannot read and write, and persons who, though not of unsound mind, cannot take care of themselves and manage their property because of age, disease, weak mind or similar causes, and thus become "an easy prey for deceit and exploitation." Such a person may have the capacity to consent to marriage, but the settlement must be signed by a court-appointed guardian.

1995 Bar: On September 10, 1988, Kevin (26) married Karla (18). The day before, without the knowledge of their parents or guardians, they executed an antenuptial contract adopting CPG. A month later Kevin died. What regime governed?

ACP. Karla was a minor when the settlement was executed (majority was then 21, since RA 6809 took effect only on December 18, 1989). Under Art. 78, the settlement is valid only if the persons who must consent to her marriage are made parties. They were not, so the settlement is void and Art. 75 supplies ACP (the marriage was after August 3, 1988).

Uribe (situation 4, CivRev 2025; also 2018 situationer): "A and B were married in December 1989. Days before, they executed a settlement adopting CPG. B was only 19, and the settlement was executed without the knowledge of B's parents. Status?" The class split between "valid, because RA 6809 already applies" and "void, because Art. 78 requires the parents as parties". Sir: it depends on the exact date. If on or after that date, B was of age, and Art. 78, which speaks only of a "minor", no longer applies, even though parental consent to the marriage itself is still required for ages 18 to 21. Art. 80: which law governs the property relations

Art. 80, FC. In the absence of a contrary stipulation in a marriage settlement, the property relations of the spouses shall be governed by Philippine laws, regardless of the place of the celebration of the marriage and their residence.
This rule shall not apply:
(1) Where both spouses are aliens;
(2) With respect to the extrinsic validity of contracts affecting property not situated in the Philippines and executed in the country where the property is located; and
(3) With respect to the extrinsic validity of contracts entered into in the Philippines but affecting property situated in a foreign country whose laws require different formalities for its extrinsic validity. (124a)

Tolentino reads it the same way: when both spouses are Filipinos, or one is Filipino and the other a foreigner, the settlement governs first, and absent a stipulation, Philippine law applies wherever they live or married. Some commentators apply the husband's national law by analogy to the old NCC 124; note the issue if asked.

Tolentino (property abroad): Art. 16 NCC subjects real and personal property to the law of the place where it is situated. So the settlement must yield to contrary foreign laws on property located abroad, when those laws are invoked. The exception in par. (2) should therefore reach the intrinsic validity of the stipulations on foreign property, not only their extrinsic validity. Par. (3) is the opposite problem: it makes Philippine law yield, as to form, for a contract executed here over property abroad. That runs against Art. 17 NCC (forms are governed by the law of the place of execution). Tolentino says par. (3) "should have been suppressed, or it must be interpreted in the light of conflict of laws principles in relation to real property."
Quiz trap (Quiz #3 pair 2): "The property relations of the spouses, both being Filipinos, shall be governed by Philippine laws, regardless of the place of the celebration of the marriage and their residence." False per the key: it drops the opening qualifier "in the absence of a contrary stipulation in a marriage settlement". Spouses may stipulate otherwise.

UP BOC 2011 MCQ: X and Y, both Filipinos, married and resided in Spain, intending to return to the Philippines someday. No marriage settlement. What law governs their property relations?

B: Philippine law, since they are both Filipinos (Art. 80). Residence does not matter. The UP BOC 2012 MCQ asks the same thing in the abstract: "In the absence of contrary stipulation … property relations of Filipino spouses shall be governed by Philippine laws."

Art. 81: stipulations in consideration of a future marriage

Art. 81, FC. Everything stipulated in the settlements or contracts referred to in the preceding articles in consideration of a future marriage, including donations between the prospective spouses made therein, shall be rendered void if the marriage does not take place. However, stipulations that do not depend upon the celebration of the marriages shall be valid. (125a)
  • A marriage settlement is an accessory contract. It depends on the marriage. If the marriage does not take place, everything in it that was made in consideration of the marriage is void by operation of law. Nothing needs to be revoked.
  • Stipulations that do not depend on the marriage survive, e.g., an acknowledgment of a debt or recognition of a child included in the document.
  • The settlement takes effect only upon the celebration (see the 1991 bar answer: on September 15, 1991, before the wedding, the settlement "is not yet valid and enforceable until the celebration").
Tolentino (reason for Art. 81): The settlement is an accessory contract that depends on the marriage for its existence. If there is no marriage, its terms cannot subsist. Stipulations that do not depend on the marriage remain valid; his example is an acknowledgment of paternity of an illegitimate child, which stands even if the wedding does not happen (Colin & Capitant).
Quiz trap (CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #6; old Quiz #3 pair 2): "Donations between the prospective spouses made in the marriage settlement, in consideration of a future marriage, shall be revocable if the marriage does not take place." False. Under Art. 81, they are rendered void. Sir: "If it is revoked, it presupposes that there is a valid act; there is nothing to revoke if it is void." Revocation (Art. 86(1)) applies to donations propter nuptias made outside the settlement. Art. 86(1) itself carves out "donations made in the marriage settlements, which shall be governed by Article 81".
Uribe (Quiz 4 #6 discussion): a student said it is False "because it is revoked by operation of law, not revocable". Sir: that explanation is also wrong. It is not "revoked by operation of law"; it is void. "What is the difference? If it is void, [it is void] from the very beginning; [if] revoked by operation of law, it is valid but revoked." Do not confuse Art. 81 (void) with Arts. 43(3) and 44 (DPN revoked by operation of law when the donee contracted a subsequent marriage in bad faith, or both spouses acted in bad faith).

Arts. 82–83: donations by reason of marriage (donations propter nuptias)

Art. 82, FC. Donations by reason of marriage are those which are made before its celebration, in consideration of the same, and in favor of one or both of the future spouses. (126)

Requisites of a donation propter nuptias

  1. Made before the celebration of the marriage;
  2. Made in consideration of the marriage; and
  3. Made in favor of one or both of the future spouses.

The donor may be anyone: one future spouse in favor of the other, a parent, a relative, or a stranger, unless the general law on donations disqualifies him (e.g., Art. 739, NCC). The donee must be one or both of the future spouses.

Tolentino (the marriage is the cause, not a condition): Valverde and others treat the marriage as a suspensive condition of the donation. Tolentino disagrees. A condition can be separated from the act without affecting its validity, and a fulfilled condition retroacts; a DPN cannot be conceived without the marriage, and it takes effect only upon the celebration. The marriage is "the purpose and determining cause without which there is no donation." The definition excludes, and leaves to the rules on ordinary donations: (1) donations to the spouses after the celebration; (2) donations to the future spouses before the celebration but not in consideration of the marriage; and (3) donations to persons other than the spouses, even if founded on the marriage. A DPN may be given (1) by the future spouses to each other, (2) by the parents to one or both, or (3) by third persons to either or both.
Quiz trap (CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #9): "Donations by reason of marriage are those which are made before its celebration, in consideration of the same, and made by one or both of the future spouses." False. The law says "in favor of". Sir docked a student to 3.5 points for not explaining who may be the donor: "The problem is not the donee; ang statement is who may be the donor … anyone is allowed to donate unless otherwise prohibited under the law on donations." Also false (Quiz #1 TF3): "Donations by reason of marriage are those which are made before its celebration, in consideration of the same" (incomplete, because it omits the third requisite).

UP BOC 2011 MCQ (and 2018 situationer): In gratitude, the groom's parents donated a property, in writing, to the bride's parents shortly before the wedding. The donation was accepted. Nature?

A: an ordinary donation, because it was not given to the bride or groom. Sir's follow-up: "Why do we have to know what kind of donation this is?" Because the grounds for revocation differ. A DPN is revocable on the Art. 86 grounds. An ordinary donation is revocable under the Civil Code (birth of children, non-fulfillment of charges, ingratitude: Arts. 760, 764, 765).

UP BOC 2012 MCQ: Which of the following is NOT a requisite for a valid DPN? (A) made before the celebration; (B) automatically revoked in case of non-celebration of the marriage; (C) made in consideration of the marriage; (D) made in favor of one or both of the future spouses.

B. Non-celebration is a ground for revocation by the donor (Art. 86(1)), not a requisite, and it is not automatic for DPNs outside the settlement. For donations inside the settlement, Art. 81 makes them void, not "revoked".

Art. 83, FC. These donations are governed by the rules on ordinary donations established in Title III of Book III of the Civil Code, insofar as they are not modified by the following articles. (127a)
Tolentino (how the DPN rules modify ordinary donations): Arts. 725–773 NCC apply insofar as Arts. 82–87 do not modify them. Tolentino lists the principal modifications: (1) a DPN does not require express acceptance; (2) if made by minors (below 21), it needs the consent of those who must consent to the marriage; (3) between the future spouses, it cannot exceed one-fifth of the donor's present property; (4) it may include future property; (5) it is not revoked by the later birth or appearance of children; and (6) it is revoked by the non-celebration of the marriage and the other causes in Art. 86.
Conflict on acceptance: Tolentino's item (1) carries over the Spanish Civil Code rule that a DPN needs no express acceptance (the marriage implies it). Sir's position is that the Family Code changed nothing about formalities, so Arts. 748–749 NCC apply in full, including acceptance for immovables. For Uribe's quizzes, follow Uribe on form; mention Tolentino only as a commentator's view.

Form of a DPN: same as ordinary donations

Art. 748, NCC. The donation of a movable may be made orally or in writing.
An oral donation requires the simultaneous delivery of the thing or of the document representing the right donated.
If the value of the personal property donated exceeds five thousand pesos, the donation and the acceptance shall be made in writing. Otherwise, the donation shall be void. (632a)
Art. 749, NCC. In order that the donation of an immovable may be valid, it must be made in a public document, specifying therein the property donated and the value of the charges which the donee must satisfy.
The acceptance may be made in the same deed of donation or in a separate public document, but it shall not take effect unless it is done during the lifetime of the donor.
If the acceptance is made in a separate instrument, the donor shall be notified thereof in an authentic form, and this step shall be noted in both instruments. (633)
Uribe (Quiz 4 #1 discussion): "Are there modifications as far as the formalities of DPN in relation to ordinary donations?" No. "As far as formalities, exactly the same. Walang binago ang FC." The Family Code modifies only the one-fifth rule (Art. 84), encumbered property (Art. 85) and the grounds for revocation (Art. 86). "If the Civil Code so provides and there is no modification in the FC, then the same rules apply to DPN."
Quiz traps on form: "In order that the donation by reason of marriage involving a movable may be valid, it must be made in writing" (Quiz 4 #1; Quiz #3 pair variant) is False: writing is needed only if the value exceeds ₱5,000; otherwise an oral donation with simultaneous delivery is valid. "… involving an immovable … must be made in a public document" (Quiz #3 pair 3) is True (Art. 749). Quiz 4 #19, "A donation by reason of marriage involving a painting will be valid only if: (a) in writing; (b) in a public instrument; (c) a verbal donation; (d) any of the above; (e) none of the above": E, because it depends on the painting's value, and an oral donation also needs simultaneous delivery. (Sir: "maraming paintings sa tabi-tabi na ₱150 lang.")
Uribe (2018 situationer): A, excited about his wedding in five days, "donated" a house and lot to B by writing her a letter. B died two days before the wedding. Are B's heirs entitled? No. A donation of an immovable requires a public document (Art. 749), so the letter is void as a donation. Even if valid, the marriage never took place, which is a ground for revocation (Art. 86(1)). He also asked whether DPNs are covered by the Statute of Frauds (the class notes record no answer). Arts. 84–85: what the future spouses may donate to each other

Art. 84, FC. If the future spouses agree upon a regime other than the absolute community of property, they cannot donate to each other in their marriage settlements more than one-fifth of their present property. Any excess shall be considered void.
Donations of future property shall be governed by the provisions on testamentary succession and the formalities of wills. (130a)

Breakdown of the one-fifth rule

  • Applies only if the regime is other than ACP (CPG, separation, or any other regime).
  • Applies only to donations between the future spouses, in their marriage settlements. A third person's DPN is not capped by Art. 84 (only by inofficiousness rules).
  • The cap is one-fifth of present property (property owned at the time of the donation).
  • Effect of excess: only the excess is void. The donation is valid up to one-fifth.
  • Future property (property the donor does not yet own, NCC 751) may be donated between future spouses in the settlement only as a mortis causa disposition. It must follow the formalities of wills and the rules on testamentary succession (an exception to NCC 751, which bars donations of future property).
Tolentino (why ACP is exempt, and why the cap exists): If the regime is the ACP, the cap is pointless, because all the property becomes community property anyway; but if the settlement excludes the donated property from the community, Tolentino says it is logical to apply the cap. The law rests on the policy that "no spouse should be allowed to take advantage of the love or tender feelings of the other to acquire property from the latter." That is why Art. 87 bans donations during the marriage altogether. The rule is relaxed before or at the wedding only as a concession to the custom of giving property to the spouses when they marry ("Bigay-Kaya" in Tagalog, "Sab-ong" in Ilocano, "Bugay" in Visayan). Sir asked this in the 2018 situationer: "Is there wisdom to the prohibition on donating more than ⅕ in cases other than ACP? Why was ACP excluded?"
Tolentino vs. the text: a DPN outside the settlement. Art. 84 caps donations "in their marriage settlements". Read literally, a DPN between the future spouses made in a separate deed could exceed one-fifth. Tolentino says the reason of the law gives a negative answer: "whether the donation is in a marriage settlement or not, as long as it is made in view of the marriage, the limitation to one-fifth of present property shall apply." For Uribe's quizzes, follow the codal ("in their marriage settlements"), but this is a good discussion point. Other Tolentino notes: where there is no proof of the donor's total property and the donor was well-off, a DPN of ₱100 was held valid and not inofficious (Adapon v. Adapon); a DPN of present property by a third person is subject only to the limits on ordinary donations (Arts. 750, 752 NCC); a DPN of future property takes effect only at the donor's death, must be made in a will, follows the rules on testamentary succession, and is revocable at any time, and persons other than the future spouses cannot give one (Art. 761 NCC).
Quiz traps (Art. 84 is his favorite one-fifth item):

Art. 85, FC. Donations by reason of marriage of property subject to encumbrances shall be valid. In case of foreclosure of the encumbrance and the property is sold for less than the total amount of the obligation secured, the donee shall not be liable for the deficiency. If the property is sold for more than the total amount of said obligation, the donee shall be entitled to the excess. (131a)
Tolentino (Art. 85): The article changes Art. 131 NCC, which obliged the donor to free the donated property of mortgages and other encumbrances (except easements) unless otherwise stipulated. That duty is gone. A registered encumbrance, or one known to the donee, can be enforced on the property against the donee. The donee may keep the property and prevent foreclosure by paying the secured debt. If the property is foreclosed and the proceeds fall short, the donor, as debtor, pays the deficiency, not the donee; if the proceeds exceed the debt, the excess goes to the donee.
Quiz trap (Quiz #3 pair 3): "In case of foreclosure … sold for less than the total amount of the obligation secured, the donor shall not be liable for the deficiency." False: it is the donee who is not liable. (The donor-obligor remains liable on his own debt for the deficiency.) The variant "… sold for more than the total amount of said obligation, the donee shall be entitled to the excess" is True. Art. 85 departs from the general rule on foreclosure deficiency for an accommodation owner and from the rule (NCC 2115) that the excess goes to the debtor.

Because it is the donor's right, the donor may choose not to exercise it, and the action may be waived. Some DPNs are revoked by operation of law, with no action needed: (1) when a subsequent marriage becomes void under Art. 43 (the absent spouse reappears) and the donee contracted it in bad faith; (2) donations in the marriage settlement when the marriage does not take place (Art. 81); and (3) a donation subject to a suspensive condition that does not happen, in which case it never takes effect. To these add Art. 44 (both spouses of the subsequent marriage in bad faith: all DPNs and testamentary dispositions between them are revoked by operation of law) and Art. 50 (Art. 43(2)–(5) applied to marriages declared void under Art. 40 or annulled). The syllabus groups these as X.C.5.b "By operation of law, FC 43; 44; 81". Note that Sir treats Art. 81 as a case of nullity ("void"), not revocation.
Tolentino (acts of ingratitude, Art. 765 NCC): (1) the donee commits an offense against the person, honor or property of the donor, or of the donor's spouse or children under his parental authority; (2) the donee imputes to the donor a criminal offense or an act involving moral turpitude, even though he should prove it, unless the crime or act was committed against the donee, his spouse or children under his authority; (3) the donee unduly refuses to support the donor when legally or morally bound to do so.

Art. 87: donations between spouses during the marriage

Art. 87, FC. Every donation or grant of gratuitous advantage, direct or indirect, between the spouses during the marriage shall be void, except moderate gifts which the spouses may give each other on the occasion of any family rejoicing. The prohibition shall also apply to persons living together as husband and wife without a valid marriage. (133a)

Elements and scope

  • Covers every donation or grant of gratuitous advantage, direct or indirect (e.g., a donation through a dummy, or condoning a debt).
  • During the marriage. A donation before the marriage, in consideration of it, is a DPN under Art. 82.
  • Sanction: void, so it can be attacked by anyone whose interest is affected (e.g., the donor's heirs), and the action does not prescribe.
  • Exception: moderate gifts on the occasion of family rejoicing (birthdays, anniversaries, Christmas). "Moderate" is relative to the spouses' means.
  • Extension: the prohibition applies to persons living together as husband and wife without a valid marriage, codifying Matabuena.
  • Rationale: (1) to prevent one spouse from exerting undue influence on the other ("lest they deceive each other by stripping themselves for love"); (2) to protect creditors and the donor's family; (3) to keep the regime from being altered during the marriage (Art. 76).
  • Tolentino (cannot be contracted out of): the spouses cannot stipulate in their settlement that donations between them during the marriage will be valid, even under complete separation of property. Art. 1490 NCC allows sales between spouses who have agreed on separation of property, but the reasons of avarice, abuse of affection and undue influence apply to donations whatever the regime.
  • Tolentino (donations mortis causa): the Civil Code expressly excepted donations taking effect after the donor's death. The Family Code omits the exception, but Tolentino says it still applies: the article prohibits donations that take effect immediately. A mortis causa donation is made in a will, is revocable, and may be reduced at the instance of compulsory heirs if inofficious.
  • Tolentino (indirect donations to relatives of the other spouse): Art. 134 NCC made a spouse's donations to the other spouse's children by a prior marriage, or to persons of whom the other spouse is a presumptive heir, merely voidable at the instance of the donor's heirs. The Family Code drops this. Tolentino says such donations are now void under Art. 87, because they are "indirect" donations to the other spouse, who is expected to inherit. Manresa would uphold one where the donor knew the other spouse could not expect to inherit (e.g., the stepson already has legitimate children).
Tolentino (reason and status): Art. 87 covers donations inter vivos. It rests on the unity of the spouses and aims to prevent transfers driven by passion or avarice: the intimacy of marriage places "the weaker spouse under the will of the stronger, whatever the sex," who might extract property by abuse of affection or by threats. Such donations are a "patent nullity"; legally they do not exist (Uy Coque v. Navas, 45 Phil. 430). The nullity may be invoked at any time, by action or defense, by the donor, the donor's heirs, or anyone prejudiced (Manresa). On moderate gifts, the court weighs the family's social position, wealth, usages and circumstances. Scaevola would treat a gift above one-fifth of the giver's present property as immoderate (by analogy to Art. 84); Castan says the court's discretion controls. A husband may validly give his wife, as a manual gift, a car bought with community funds, and the car is then exempt from seizure for a later community debt (a Louisiana ruling Tolentino cites).
Harding v. Commercial Union Assurance Co. (38 Phil. 464, 1918)

(Digest from Tolentino.) With her husband's consent, the wife insured an automobile her husband had given her shortly before. The car was totally destroyed, and the insurer refused to pay, arguing that she had no insurable interest because the gift was void under the old Civil Code prohibition on donations between spouses (now Art. 87). Held: the insurer cannot raise the point. Citing Cook v. McMicking (27 Phil. 10), the Court said the prohibition may be invoked only by persons whose rights or interests are affected by the transfer; the insurer had no right or interest in the car when the transfer occurred. Even if it could invoke the article, it had the burden of showing that the gift was not a moderate one, and the Court could not say as a matter of law that a husband's gift of a car to his wife is not moderate.

Matabuena v. Cervantes (38 SCRA 284, 1971)

Felix Matabuena donated a parcel of land to Petronila Cervantes in 1956, while they were living together as husband and wife without the benefit of marriage. They married in 1962, and Felix died intestate soon after. His sister and only heir, Cornelia, sued to recover the land, invoking Art. 133 NCC, which voided donations between spouses during the marriage. The lower court upheld the donation because the parties were not yet married when it was made. Held: the donation is void. "While Art. 133 of the Civil Code considers as void a 'donation between the spouses during the marriage', policy considerations of the most exigent character as well as the dictates of morality require that the same prohibition should apply to a common-law relationship." If the law prohibits donations between spouses for fear of undue influence, "there is every reason to apply the same prohibitive policy to persons living together as husband and wife without benefit of nuptials," because such a connection "bespeaks greater influence of one party over the other." "So long as marriage remains the cornerstone of our family law, reason and morality alike demand that the disabilities attached to marriage should likewise attach to concubinage." Since the donation was void, the land passed by intestacy: Petronila, as surviving spouse, got one-half and Cornelia, as sister, got the other half.

Checklist: Arts. 74–87 in one page

  1. Order: settlement (executed before the marriage) → Family Code → local custom.
  2. No settlement or void regime → ACP if married on or after August 3, 1988; CPG if married before.
  3. Settlement: writing + signed + executed before the celebration. Not a public instrument. Binds third persons only if registered in the LCR where the marriage is recorded and in the registries of property.
  4. Modifications: only before the celebration; after it, only through Arts. 66, 67, 128, 135, 136, all of which need the court.
  5. Minor (Art. 78): the persons who must consent to the marriage must be parties. RA 6809 took effect December 18, 1989. Civil interdiction or disability (Art. 79): the court-appointed guardian must be a party.
  6. Art. 80: Philippine law for Filipino spouses absent a contrary stipulation, regardless of place of celebration or residence; not if both are aliens; extrinsic validity of contracts over foreign property follows foreign formalities.
  7. Art. 81: no marriage → settlement stipulations in consideration of it, including donations between the prospective spouses made in it, are void, not revocable.
  8. DPN: before, in consideration, in favor of one or both future spouses. Any donor. Same form as ordinary donations (₱5,000 threshold for movables; public document for immovables).
  9. One-fifth of present property, only for regimes other than ACP, only between the future spouses in the settlement; only the excess is void. Future property → rules on wills.
  10. Encumbered property: the donee is not liable for the deficiency and is entitled to the excess.
  11. Art. 86: six grounds for the donor: not celebrated or declared void (except settlement donations); no parental consent; annulled and the donee in bad faith; legal separation and the donee guilty; resolutory condition fulfilled; ingratitude.
  12. Art. 87: donations between spouses during the marriage are void, except moderate gifts on family rejoicing. The rule also covers common-law partners (Matabuena).
