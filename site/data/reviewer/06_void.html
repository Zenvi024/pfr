Concept: what a void marriage is, and why "all requisites present" does not mean "valid"

Art. 4, FC (par. 1). The absence of any of the essential or formal requisites shall render the marriage void ab initio, except as stated in Article 35 (2).
  • A void marriage is inexistent from the beginning. In law it is deemed never to have taken place. A voidable marriage, by contrast, is valid and subsisting until set aside by final judgment (Tolentino).
  • The Family Code proceeds by enumeration (Arts. 35–38, plus Arts. 40, 41, 44, 53). Tolentino warns this may give the wrong idea that every marriage not listed is valid. Some marriages are void because they lack a basic element of marriage even if no article names them: (1) marriages where intent to marry is totally wanting, such as a marriage in a play or film (even if the "priest" is a real priest) or a marriage in jest; (2) marriages not solemnized as the law requires, such as common-law marriages and marriages by proxy; (3) marriages between persons of the same sex. These are legally inexistent.
  • The general American rule is that a void marriage confers no legal rights, as if no marriage had ever been performed. Philippine law is "more humane": it expressly gives some effects to void marriages, on property (Arts. 147–148; Arts. 43(2) and 50) and on the status of children (Art. 54) (Tolentino).
Uribe (2025 recitation): "If all the essential and formal requisites are present, is the marriage valid?" The responsive answer is "No, not necessarily." He penalized a student who instead talked about marriages that are valid even without a license (articulo mortis): "The answer is not responsive." The essential and formal requisites "are minimal requirements lang." A marriage with all of them may still be void under:
  • Art. 36 (psychological incapacity);
  • Art. 37 (incestuous) and Art. 38 (public policy);
  • Art. 35(4) (bigamous, not under Art. 41) and Art. 40 (remarriage without a judicial declaration of nullity of the prior void marriage);
  • Art. 35(5) (mistake as to identity);
  • Art. 44 (subsequent marriage where both spouses acted in bad faith, even with a judicial declaration of presumptive death);
  • Art. 35(6) with Art. 53 (remarriage after annulment or nullity without recording the judgment, partition and delivery of presumptive legitimes).
Start with "No," then give the articles.
Uribe (void plus voidable): When the same marriage has both a ground for nullity and a ground for annulment, its status is void. "Walang saysay ang voidable kung void ang marriage." See the voidable section for the full void vs. voidable comparison (Tolentino's five distinctions). The short version: a void marriage (1) may be attacked collaterally, (2) may be questioned even after the death of a party, (3) cannot be ratified, (4) may be assailed by anyone whose rights it affects when the question becomes material, and (5) the action or defense does not prescribe.

Art. 35: the six void marriages (verbatim)

Art. 35, FC. The following marriages shall be void from the beginning:
(1) Those contracted by any party below eighteen years of age even with the consent of parents or guardians;
(2) Those solemnized by any person not legally authorized to perform marriages unless such marriages were contracted with either or both parties believing in good faith that the solemnizing officer had the legal authority to do so;
(3) Those solemnized without license, except those covered by the preceding Chapter;
(4) Those bigamous or polygamous marriages not falling under Article 41;
(5) Those contracted through mistake of one contracting party as to the identity of the other; and
(6) Those subsequent marriages that are void under Article 53. (80a)

Text note: some printings of par. (4), including the B29 reviewer, read "not failing under Article 41." Some published texts carry this reading too. The sense is plainly "falling," and the rule is the same either way: bigamous marriages are void unless they fall under Art. 41.

Source: NCC Art. 80 (Tolentino). Paragraphs (1), (2) and (3) are void for absence of an essential or formal requisite (Art. 4). Paragraphs (4) and (6) are void because of the impediment of a prior subsisting marriage. Paragraph (5) is void for lack of valid consent (Tolentino).

Art. 80, Civil Code (for marriages before Aug. 3, 1988). The following marriages shall be void from the beginning:
(1) Those contracted under the ages of sixteen and fourteen years by the male and female respectively, even with the consent of the parents;
(2) Those solemnized by any person not legally authorized to perform marriages;
(3) Those solemnized without a marriage license, save marriages of exceptional character;
(4) Bigamous or polygamous marriages not falling under Article 83, number 2;
(5) Incestuous marriages mentioned in Article 81;
(6) Those where one or both contracting parties have been found guilty of the killing of the spouse of either of them;
(7) Those between stepbrothers and stepsisters and other marriages specified in Article 82.

Art. 35(1): below eighteen, "even with the consent of parents or guardians"

  • Age 18 is the minimum for legal capacity (Art. 5). A party below 18 has no legal capacity, so the marriage is void even with parental consent. Consent of parents matters only for ages 18 to below 21 (Art. 14; Art. 45(1), voidable).
  • The Code follows canonical law, which treats a marriage below the age of consent as void. Under the old Marriage Law such a marriage was only voidable (Tolentino).
  • Civil Code: void only if the male was below 16 or the female below 14 (NCC 80(1)). A 1981 marriage of a 16-year-old girl is not void for age. Always check the date of the marriage.
  • FC 234 originally allowed emancipation "by the marriage of the minor." RA 6809 amended it so that emancipation now takes place only by attaining majority at 18; the syllabus calls this provision "repealed." With majority at 18 and marriage below 18 void, a minor can no longer be emancipated by marriage.
  • RA 11596 (2021) declares child marriages void ab initio and penalizes those who facilitate or solemnize them, and the parent or adult who causes or allows them (see the requisites section for detail).
  • Good faith as to age does not save the marriage. In the 2006 Bar problem, Ric married 16-year-old Juliet who showed a falsified birth certificate saying 18. The marriage is void under Art. 35(1), and also bigamous since Ric was still married to Gigi. The good-faith exception of Art. 35(2) is about the officer's authority only.

In 2023 A (25) married B (17) with the written consent of B's parents. All other requisites were complied with. Status?

Answer: Void (Art. 35(1)). Parental consent cannot supply legal capacity. Not voidable, because Art. 45(1) covers only parties 18 to below 21. Cohabitation after B turns 18 does not ratify a void marriage.

Art. 35(2): no authority of the solemnizing officer; the good-faith exception

  • Authority of the solemnizing officer is a formal requisite (Art. 3(1)); who has it is listed in Art. 7 (and Art. 10, and the Local Government Code for mayors). Absence of authority makes the marriage void unless "either or both parties" believed in good faith that the officer had legal authority. This is the only exception named in Art. 4, par. 1.
  • One party's good faith is enough ("either or both"). The good faith must rest on a mistake of fact, e.g., a person posing as a priest or a judge who, unknown to the parties, was dismissed the day before. A mistake of law, e.g., believing that a ship captain may solemnize on land or that a person not listed in Art. 7 may solemnize, does not qualify, because ignorance of the law excuses no one (NCC 3). (Full discussion in the requisites section.)
  • Do not confuse authority (in the person) with jurisdiction and venue. A judge who solemnizes outside his territorial jurisdiction still has authority; the marriage is valid with an irregularity (Navarro v. Domagtoy; Arañes v. Occiano). Uribe: "Authority under Art. 7 nasa tao."

On March 15, 2025 A and B married before Judge C in her chambers. Unknown to them, the Supreme Court had dismissed Judge C on March 14, 2025; she was only collecting her things. Status?

Answer: Valid. Judge C had no authority, but A and B believed in good faith (on a mistake of fact) that she did (Art. 35(2)). Judge C may be liable criminally and administratively, but that does not affect validity.

Art. 35(3): no license, except the marriages in Arts. 27–34

  • The license is a formal requisite. Its absence voids the marriage unless the marriage is one of the exceptional marriages: in articulo mortis (Arts. 27, 29–32), remote places (Art. 28), Muslims and ethnic cultural communities (Art. 33), and five-year cohabitation (Art. 34).
  • Proof of no license: a certification of the local civil registrar of due search and inability to find the license (Republic v. CA and Castro; Abbas v. Abbas; Kho v. Republic). A license number that does not match the registrar's records shows that no license was issued (Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan).
  • Art. 34 requires five years of cohabitation without legal impediment. Under the Civil Code (Niñal) the entire five years must be free of impediment; under the FC as read in Manzano v. Sanchez, the parties must have no impediment at the time of the marriage (Uribe supports Manzano) and the cohabitation must not be in violation of an existing marriage. Full discussion in the requisites section.
Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan (G.R. No. 201061, July 3, 2013)

Benjamin, already married to Azucena, went through a marriage with Sally in 1982 using a license number that did not match any license issued by the Pasig registrar. Doctrine: There is no inconsistency in declaring the Benjamin–Sally marriage both void ab initio and non-existent. It was solemnized without a license (Art. 35(3)), so it is void. It was also non-existent: applying NCC 1409, absolutely simulated or fictitious contracts are "inexistent and void from the beginning." The property relations of such a couple fall under Art. 148.

Art. 35(4): bigamous or polygamous marriages "not falling under Article 41"

  • A subsequent marriage contracted during the lifetime of a spouse is illegal and void from its performance. Under the old law no judicial decree was needed to establish its invalidity (People v. Mendoza; People v. Aragon). Under the FC, a judicial declaration is needed for remarriage purposes (Art. 40).
  • The exception is Art. 41: a subsequent marriage after the prior spouse has been absent for 4 consecutive years (2 years if there was danger of death), with a well-founded belief that the absentee is dead, and a judicial declaration of presumptive death in a summary proceeding. That subsequent marriage is valid (not voidable) until terminated under Art. 42, unless both spouses acted in bad faith (Art. 44).
  • A prior voidable marriage not yet annulled, or a prior void marriage not yet judicially declared void, is a subsisting marriage for purposes of remarriage. A second marriage is therefore void (Tolentino; Wiegel v. Sempio-Diy; Art. 40).
  • A prior marriage that was valid but later dissolved (e.g., by the other spouse's death) before the subsequent marriage is no impediment.
Quiz trap: "Bigamous or polygamous marriages are void" is FALSE (not absolutely true). The codal reads "not falling under Article 41." Likewise, "A marriage contracted by any person during subsistence of a previous marriage shall be null and void" is FALSE, because it copies only the first clause of Art. 41 and drops the "unless" clause.
Uribe ("an answer that deserves a 2"): Asked when a bigamous marriage is valid, a student answered "Art. 41, when the prior spouse is absent for 4 years and there is a well-founded belief that the spouse is dead." Uribe: "THIS IS AN ANSWER THAT DESERVES A 2!" The complete answer has three parts: (1) four consecutive years, or only two where there is danger of death under NCC 391; (2) a well-founded belief that the absentee is already dead; and (3) a judicial declaration of presumptive death in a summary proceeding, which must be for the purpose of remarriage. Art. 35(5): mistake as to the identity of the other party

  • The mistake must be as to the person himself, a substitution of another person for the one desired, without the knowledge of the other party. Example: X and Y are twins; X is to marry A, but Y substitutes for X during the ceremony, unknown to A. The biblical case is Jacob, who married Leah instead of Rachel (Tolentino).
  • If a person merely represents himself to be somebody else, assuming the latter's name, and the other party marries him believing he is that person, there is no mistake as to identity. The very individual intended is the one married. It is only a mistake as to name and perhaps personal qualities, and there is no ground for annulment either (Tolentino).
  • Mistakes as to rank, fortune, character, health or chastity are not mistakes as to identity, and they are not fraud under Art. 46 either (last paragraph of Art. 46).
  • Under the Civil Code (NCC 86(1)), misrepresentation as to identity was fraud and made the marriage voidable. The FC moved mistake as to identity to the void marriages.
Quiz trap: "Mistake ... as to the identity of the other is a ground for annulment" is FALSE; it is a ground for nullity (Art. 35(5)). "A marriage contracted through mistake of one contracting party as to the name of the other is void" is also FALSE.

Art. 35(6): subsequent marriages void under Art. 53

Art. 53, FC. Either of the former spouses may marry again after compliance with the requirements of the immediately preceding Article; otherwise, the subsequent marriage shall be null and void. (n)
  • After a judgment of annulment or absolute nullity, a former spouse may remarry only after the judgment, the partition and distribution of properties, and the delivery of the children's presumptive legitimes are recorded in the appropriate civil registry and registries of property (Art. 52). Otherwise the subsequent marriage is void.
  • Its children are nonetheless legitimate (Art. 54, second sentence).
  • Arts. 52–53 apply only where the prior marriage ended by annulment or judicial declaration of nullity. They do not apply where it was dissolved by death. If a widow(er) remarries without liquidating the prior regime, the subsequent marriage is valid but a mandatory regime of complete separation of property governs it (Arts. 103 and 130).
Uribe (death vs. nullity vs. legal separation): "A died. The surviving spouse remarried. On dissolution of the second marriage the heirs claimed it was void for failure to deliver the presumptive legitimes. Correct?" No. Presumptive legitimes are for nullity and annulment, "presumptive nga kasi walang namatay." If instead the spouses were only legally separated and one remarried, the second marriage is void because it is bigamous: legal separation does not sever the bond. It is not void for failure to deliver presumptive legitimes.

A and B's marriage was annulled for fraud by a judgment final in 2018. Without recording the partition and delivery of presumptive legitimes, A married C in 2020. D was born in 2021. Status of the A–C marriage and of D?

Answer: The A–C marriage is void (Arts. 52–53; Art. 35(6)). D is legitimate (Art. 54, second sentence).

Art. 36: psychological incapacity — text, history, nature

Art. 36, FC (as amended by E.O. No. 227). A marriage contracted by any party who, at the time of the celebration, was psychologically incapacitated to comply with the essential marital obligations of marriage, shall likewise be void even if such incapacity becomes manifest only after its solemnization.
  • New provision, introduced by the FC (Tolentino). It was taken by the Family Code Revision Committee from Canon 1095 of the 1983 Code of Canon Law: "The following are incapable of contracting marriage: ... those who are unable to assume the essential obligations of marriage due to causes of psychological nature" (Molina).
  • Original prescriptive period. As first enacted (E.O. 209), the article provided that the action to declare the nullity of the marriage under it "shall prescribe in ten years after its celebration." E.O. 227 repealed that, even before the FC took effect. E.O. 227 instead amended Art. 39 to give a ten-year period for pre-FC marriages falling under Art. 36. RA 8533 (1998) later deleted that too. Today, Art. 36 actions never prescribe (Art. 39).
  • Elements, read from the text: (1) a party was psychologically incapacitated; (2) the incapacity is to comply with the essential marital obligations; (3) it existed at the time of the celebration, even if it becomes manifest only later.
  • Essential marital obligations: Arts. 68–71 (between spouses: live together, observe mutual love, respect and fidelity, render mutual help and support; fix the family domicile; support the family; manage the household) and Arts. 220, 221 and 225 (parents to children) (Molina guideline 6; Tan-Andal).
Tolentino:
  • Examples: a husband who believes he need not work to support his family because that is his in-laws' duty, or a wife who thinks sexual relations are sinful and dirty and dislikes children. The marriage would be void under Art. 36.
  • Juridical basis, criticized. Incapacity to comply with the essential obligations does not affect consent. It would be different if it were incapacity to understand them, for that would be lack of consent. Incapacity to comply is juridically no different from physical incapacity to consummate, which makes a marriage only voidable (Art. 45(5)). Psychological incapacity may also be cured, but a cure would not validate a marriage that is legally inexistent. Tolentino believes it "should have been a cause for annulment of the marriage only."
  • Retroactivity, criticized. The E.O. 227 ten-year period for pre-FC marriages gave the article retroactive effect, invalidating marriages valid when solemnized under the Civil Code. He calls this "a juridical mistake ... like an ex post facto provision translated into the field of civil law," contrary to Art. 256 (retroactivity only without prejudice to vested rights). A prescriptive period also "seems to betray a sub-consciousness that this marriage should have been made only voidable."
  • Being of unsound mind, drug addiction, habitual alcoholism, homosexuality or lesbianism do not by themselves make a marriage void, but Art. 36 may be invoked if, because of the condition, the spouse cannot comply with the essential obligations.
Note: Tolentino's view that Art. 36 marriages "should be voidable" is criticism, not law. For the quiz: an Art. 36 marriage is void, its action is imprescriptible (Art. 39, RA 8533), it cannot be ratified, and its children conceived or born before finality of the judgment are legitimate (Art. 54).

Art. 36: Santos, Molina and the cases before Tan-Andal

Santos v. Court of Appeals (1995)

Leouel Santos, a soldier, sued to declare his marriage to Julia void because she left for the U.S. to work and never communicated or returned. Doctrine: Psychological incapacity must be characterized by (a) gravity, (b) juridical antecedence, and (c) incurability. It should refer to "no less than a mental (not physical) incapacity" and is confined "to the most serious cases of personality disorders clearly demonstrative of an utter insensitivity or inability to give meaning and significance to the marriage." Being of unsound mind, drug addiction, habitual alcoholism, lesbianism or homosexuality may be indicia of psychological incapacity, depending on their degree of severity; their concealment is a ground for annulment by fraud (Art. 46(4)). Julia's failure to return and communicate did not show incapacity. Petition denied.

Republic v. Court of Appeals and Molina (G.R. No. 108763, 268 SCRA 198, February 13, 1997; the syllabus prints "240 SCRA 198")

The wife sued, citing her husband's immaturity, irresponsibility and dishonesty with money. Doctrine: Mere showing of irreconcilable differences and conflicting personalities is not psychological incapacity. It is not enough to prove that the parties failed to meet their responsibilities; it must be shown that they were incapable of doing so due to some psychological (not physical) illness. The Court laid down eight guidelines:

  1. The burden of proof belongs to the plaintiff. Any doubt is resolved in favor of the existence and continuation of the marriage.
  2. The root cause must be (a) medically or clinically identified, (b) alleged in the complaint, (c) sufficiently proven by experts, and (d) clearly explained in the decision. The incapacity must be psychological, not physical, though its manifestations may be physical.
  3. The incapacity must exist at "the time of the celebration". Its manifestation need not be perceivable then, but the illness must have attached at or before that moment.
  4. It must be medically or clinically permanent or incurable. Incurability may be absolute or relative only to the other spouse. It must be relevant to the assumption of marriage obligations, not to a profession or job (a pediatrician may diagnose children well yet be incapable of raising his own).
  5. It must be grave enough to disable the party from assuming the essential obligations. "Mild characteriological peculiarities, mood changes, occasional emotional outbursts" cannot be root causes. It must be downright incapacity, not a refusal, neglect or difficulty, much less ill will: "a natal or supervening disabling factor ... an adverse integral element in the personality structure."
  6. The essential obligations are those in Arts. 68–71 (spouses) and Arts. 220, 221 and 225 (parents and children). The obligations not complied with must be stated in the petition, proven and included in the decision.
  7. Interpretations of the National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal of the Catholic Church, "while not controlling or decisive, should be given great respect."
  8. The trial court must order the prosecuting attorney and the Solicitor General to appear for the State. No decision shall be handed down without the OSG's certification of its agreement or opposition. The OSG acts as defensor vinculi.
Note on Molina guideline 8: A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC (2003) no longer requires the OSG certification before decision. The State is still represented by the public prosecutor (Art. 48) and the OSG, and the OSG may appeal. Guidelines 2 and 4 were modified by Tan-Andal v. Andal (2021).
Choa v. Choa (392 SCRA 641, November 26, 2002)

Doctrine: A medical examination is not a condition sine qua non to a finding of psychological incapacity, so long as the totality of the evidence adequately establishes it. Here the evidence was completely insufficient, more so without any medical, psychiatric or psychological examination. The Court granted the respondent's demurrer to evidence.

Barcelona v. Court of Appeals and Bengzon (September 24, 2003)

Doctrine: Under the new Rule (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, Sec. 2(d)), the petition must allege the complete facts showing that either or both parties were psychologically incapacitated at the time of the celebration, even if the incapacity becomes manifest only after. The complete facts should allege the physical manifestations, if any, indicative of the incapacity, but expert opinion need not be alleged. The root cause need not be alleged, which modifies Molina guideline 2(b). A second petition does not violate the rule on forum shopping where the first was dismissed without prejudice.

Republic v. Quintero-Hamano (428 SCRA 735, May 20, 2004)

The Japanese husband, Toshio, abandoned his Filipina wife and child a month after the wedding and stopped all support. Doctrine: The three Santos requirements (gravity, juridical antecedence, incurability) apply. The Molina guidelines do not require that a physician examine the person; what matters is evidence that adequately establishes the condition, so actual medical examination may be dispensed with if the totality of evidence suffices. But abandonment, "doubtlessly irresponsible," was never shown to be due to a psychological illness. Abandonment is a ground for legal separation; psychological defect cannot be presumed from it. The norms for determining psychological incapacity apply to any person regardless of nationality; courts cannot be lenient because the spouse is a foreigner.

Ngo Te v. Yu-Te (G.R. No. 161793, February 13, 2009)

Doctrine: The Court criticized the rigid application of Molina, which had become a "strait-jacket" forcing all cases into the same mold. Each case must be judged on its own facts, not on a priori assumptions or generalizations. Courts should give due weight to the findings of psychologists and psychiatrists. On the evidence (expert findings on both spouses), the marriage was declared void.

Kalaw v. Fernandez (G.R. No. 166357; Decision September 19, 2011; Resolution January 14, 2015) — listed in the syllabus as "Kalaw v. Hernandez"

Tyrone Kalaw sought nullity, alleging his wife Malyn neglected the children and spent her time on mahjong and outside the home. The 2011 Decision denied the petition as unproven. Doctrine (2015 Resolution, on reconsideration): The Court reversed itself and declared the marriage void. It gave weight to the expert opinions (a psychologist and a canon law expert), holding that the totality of the evidence established Malyn's incapacity, and reiterated Ngo Te's warning against applying Molina as a strait-jacket. The lack of personal examination of the respondent does not by itself discredit an expert's findings if the totality of evidence supports them.

Republic v. Encelan (G.R. No. 170022, January 9, 2013)

Doctrine: Sexual infidelity and abandonment of the conjugal dwelling, even if true, do not necessarily constitute psychological incapacity. They are grounds for legal separation. It must be shown that they are manifestations of a disordered personality that completely prevented the erring spouse from discharging the essential marital obligations.

Hernandez v. Court of Appeals (1999) (B29 repository; not in the syllabus)

Doctrine: Habitual alcoholism, sexual infidelity and abandonment do not by themselves constitute psychological incapacity. They must be shown to be manifestations of a disordered personality that makes the party unable to discharge the essential obligations.

The 2017–2018 syllabus cases (all decided under Molina; read them now through Tan-Andal)

Most of these cases share one theme: the Court denied nullity where the evidence showed only marital conflict, irresponsibility, infidelity or vices, or where the expert's conclusion about the respondent rested only on the petitioner's side. Republic v. Javier is the exception: nullity was granted, but only on the petitioner's own incapacity. The blocks below state only what the full texts confirm.

Republic v. Mola Cruz (G.R. No. 236629, July 23, 2018)

Doctrine: Mere disagreements and conflicts between spouses do not constitute psychological incapacity. The petitioner must prove that the inability to perform the marital duties is due to a grave, incurable condition deeply rooted in the incapacitated spouse (Uribe's notes).

Espina-Dan v. Dan (G.R. No. 209031, April 16, 2018)

The Filipina wife alleged her Italian husband was immature, lazy and dependent on his parents. Doctrine: A psychologist or other expert may be used to diagnose the allegedly incapacitated spouse, but the court will not accept an expert conclusion drawn solely from the account of the petitioner (or other parties aligned with her) without independent basis. Such a report has little probative value. Petition denied (Uribe's notes).

Lontoc-Cruz v. Cruz (G.R. No. 201988, October 11, 2017)

The wife, Maria Victoria Socorro Lontoc-Cruz, sought nullity alleging that both spouses were psychologically incapacitated. A clinical psychologist who had held sessions with both of them testified. The RTC denied the petition and the CA affirmed. Doctrine: Petition denied. The evidence failed to show that the spouses' personality disorders antedated the marriage, were grave, or were incurable. "Mere showing of irreconcilable differences and conflicting personalities in no wise constitutes psychological incapacity." Note: even an expert who examined both spouses did not carry the day without proof of the three requisites.

Garlet v. Garlet (G.R. No. 193544, August 2, 2017)

Yolanda Garlet alleged that her husband Vencidor had narcissistic personality disorder; she pointed to his irresponsibility, joblessness and infidelity. The RTC granted nullity; the CA reversed. Doctrine: The SC affirmed the CA; the marriage is valid. The psychologist's conclusions rested mainly on information from the petitioner. Irresponsibility, joblessness and infidelity, without proof that they come from a grave psychological incapacity, are not Art. 36 incapacity. (Decided under Molina.)

Bakunawa v. Reyes-Bakunawa (G.R. No. 217993, August 9, 2017)

Manuel Bakunawa III sought nullity, alleging that both he and his wife Nora were psychologically incapacitated. The psychiatrist, Dr. Villegas, diagnosed him with intermittent explosive disorder and Nora with passive-aggressive personality disorder. Her basis was interviews with Manuel and their eldest son only; Nora did not participate, and Manuel was given no psychological tests. The RTC granted nullity; the CA reversed. Doctrine: The SC affirmed the CA; petition denied. The expert evidence was insufficient. The son could not be a reliable source on the spouses' condition at or before the marriage, since he was not yet present then. This applies Uribe's point on children as witnesses to juridical antecedence.

Singson v. Singson (G.R. No. 210766, January 8, 2018)

Maria Concepcion Singson sought nullity on the ground that her husband Benjamin was psychologically incapacitated (pathological gambling and a personality disorder). The RTC granted nullity; the CA reversed. Doctrine: The SC affirmed the CA; petition denied. (1) Gravity was not shown: Benjamin kept his job, provided for the family home, contributed financially and lived with his family. (2) The medical evidence was deficient: the psychiatrist could not identify a definite root cause, and claims about his gambling in adolescence were hearsay. (3) Juridical antecedence was not proven: the wife admitted that she did not know of any gambling before the marriage and that he was kind and caring during courtship.

Republic v. Javier (G.R. No. 210518, April 18, 2018)

Martin Nikolai Javier sought nullity of his marriage to Michelle Mercado-Javier, alleging that both had narcissistic personality disorder. The RTC dismissed the petition; the CA reversed and found both incapacitated. Doctrine: The SC modified the CA's ruling. Castillo v. Republic (G.R. No. 214064, February 6, 2017) — listed in the syllabus as "Mirasol v. Republic" (petitioner Mirasol Castillo)

Doctrine: The SC affirmed the CA; petition denied. The psychologist never interviewed Felipe. Tan-Andal v. Andal (2021): the current standard

Tan-Andal v. Andal (G.R. No. 196359, May 11, 2021, En Banc)

Rosanna sought nullity of her marriage to Mario, citing his drug use, his neglect of his family, and a psychiatrist's finding of narcissistic antisocial personality disorder. Doctrine: The Court declared the marriage void and modified the Molina guidelines:

  1. Burden and quantum. The burden of proof remains on the plaintiff, and the presumption is in favor of the validity of the marriage. The quantum is clear and convincing evidence: more than preponderance, less than proof beyond reasonable doubt.
  2. Legal, not medical. Psychological incapacity is neither a mental incapacity nor a personality disorder that must be proven through expert opinion. What must be proven are durable or enduring aspects of a person's personality structure, existing at the time of the marriage, that manifest through clear acts of dysfunctionality undermining the family and making it impossible for the spouse to understand and comply with the essential marital obligations.
  3. Expert opinion not required. Ordinary witnesses who were present in the spouse's life before the marriage may testify on behaviors they consistently observed. The judge then decides whether those behaviors indicate a genuinely serious psychic cause. Expert opinion is still useful but no longer indispensable. The Molina requirement that the root cause be "medically or clinically identified" and "sufficiently proven by experts" is abandoned.
  4. Juridical antecedence. The incapacity must exist at the time of the celebration, proven by acts and behavior before the marriage, even if it becomes manifest only after.
  5. Incurability in the legal sense. Incurability is not medical permanence. The incapacity must be so enduring and persistent with respect to a specific partner, where the couple's personality structures are so incompatible and antagonistic that the only result of the union would be the inevitable and irreparable breakdown of the marriage.
  6. Gravity. The incapacity must be caused by a genuinely serious psychic cause. It excludes mild characterological peculiarities, mood changes, occasional emotional outbursts, and mere refusal, neglect, difficulty or ill will.
  7. Essential obligations remain those of Arts. 68–71 and Arts. 220, 221 and 225.
  8. Decisions of the National Appellate Matrimonial Tribunal remain persuasive, not binding.

Art. 36 in Uribe's recitations: defenses, evidence, and related distinctions

Counsel for the respondent: the defense sequence

  1. First defense: the respondent complied with the essential marital obligations. "Who cares if incapacitated sya if he was able to perform his essential marital obligations — WILL NEVER PROSPER." The premise of Art. 36 is non-compliance.
  2. If non-compliance is admitted: the failure was not caused by psychological incapacity but by mere refusal, neglect or difficulty. Uribe distinguished "failed to comply" (possibly accidental) from "did not comply" (intentional, "OUT OF SPITE"). "The failure to comply is rooted in incapacity, which is the difficult job of the petitioner's lawyer."
  3. If the failure is due to psychological causes: attack gravity, juridical antecedence and incurability.

Defenses that do not work: prescription (Art. 39 — none), ratification by cohabitation (void marriages cannot be ratified), condonation (a legal-separation defense), in pari delicto.

Uribe (wrong answers he corrected): "In a petition under Art. 36, it is the defendant who will prove that he is able to perform marital obligations" — wrong, "there is no presumption" of incapacity; the burden is on the petitioner. A student who argued the "other spouse" was the incapacitated one was told the defense backfires: if either party is incapacitated, nullity prospers.

Attacking the evidence

  • Psychologist's report: look at the basis of the conclusion. If it rests only on interviews with the petitioner, it is self-serving (Uribe; Espina-Dan). If there were other witnesses, "depende."
  • Children as witnesses: children born during the marriage cannot testify on behavior before the marriage, so they are not credible on juridical antecedence. But Uribe asked: "Hindi ba possible na kinasal sila mga malalaki na ang anak nila?" Qualify: if the children were already adults before the wedding, they could testify on manifestations they observed then.
  • Church annulment: a NAMT decision is persuasive, not binding. The State does not give civil effect to church decrees; a party must still obtain a judicial declaration before remarrying (Art. 40).

Other Uribe points

  • Both spouses incapacitated: the petition prospers. "There is no in pari delicto here." Even one incapacitated spouse suffices, more so two (cf. Valdes: mutual psychological incapacity).
  • Who may file: either spouse, including the incapacitated spouse (A.M. Sec. 2(d): "either or both parties"). Clavecilla v. Clavecilla (2023, B29 repository) confirms the psychologically incapacitated spouse may initiate the action, though the petition there failed for lack of proof. In Republic v. Javier (2018) the petitioner-husband won nullity on the strength of his own incapacity.
  • Remarriage of the incapacitated spouse: is his subsequent marriage likewise void? Not necessarily. Incapacity is relative to the specific partner (Tan-Andal), so he may not be incapacitated as to the new spouse.
  • Status of children: legitimate if conceived or born before the judgment becomes final and executory (Art. 54). Uribe's notes give the reason: the parties entered the marriage believing it valid, and the incapacity shows only later.
  • Property: an Art. 36 marriage never had an ACP or CPG; Art. 147 (or 148) co-ownership governs (Valdes; Diño).

Computing relationship (NCC 963–967): the tool for Arts. 37 and 38

Arts. 963–967, Civil Code. Art. 963. Proximity of relationship is determined by the number of generations. Each generation forms a degree.
Art. 964. A series of degrees forms a line, which may be either direct or collateral. A direct line is that constituted by the series of degrees among ascendants and descendants. A collateral line is that constituted by the series of degrees among persons who are not ascendants and descendants, but who come from a common ancestor.
Art. 965. The direct line is either descending or ascending. The former unites the head of the family with those who descend from him. The latter binds a person with those from whom he descends.
Art. 966. In the line, as many degrees are counted as there are generations or persons, excluding the progenitor. In the direct line, ascent is made to the common ancestor. Thus, the child is one degree removed from the parent, two from the grandfather, and three from the great-grandparent. In the collateral line, ascent is made to the common ancestor and then descent is made to the person with whom the computation is to be made. Thus, a person is two degrees removed from his brother, three from his uncle, who is the brother of his father, four from his first cousin, and so forth.
Art. 967. Full blood relationship is that existing between persons who have the same father and the same mother. Half blood relationship is that existing between persons who have the same father, but not the same mother, or the same mother, but not the same father.

Method: count persons, not counting the common ancestor. In the collateral line, go up to the common ancestor, then down to the other person.

Art. 37: incestuous marriages

Art. 37, FC. Marriages between the following are incestuous and void from the beginning, whether the relationship between the parties be legitimate or illegitimate:
(1) Between ascendants and descendants of any degree; and
(2) Between brothers and sisters, whether of the full or half blood. (81a)
  • Concept: incestuous marriages are those between persons related by blood within the degree prohibited by law. Legitimacy or illegitimacy is immaterial; what matters is consanguinity. Investigation of paternity or maternity is permissible to prove it. Nullity is not affected by the fact that a connecting link was of the half blood. Incest "contravenes the voice of nature, degrades the family, offends against decency and morals, and is absolutely prohibited" (Tolentino).
  • Only two classes: (1) the direct line, any degree; (2) brothers and sisters (collateral 2nd degree), full or half blood.
  • Not every blood-relative marriage is legally incestuous. The Civil Code (NCC 81(3)) treated collaterals within the fourth degree as incestuous; the FC does not, but makes them void for public policy under Art. 38(1) (Tolentino).
  • Art. 37 covers blood relationship only. Affinity and adoption are under Art. 38.
  • Art. 37 marriages are excepted from Art. 26, par. 1: they are void even if valid where celebrated.
Quiz traps:
  • "Marriages between uncles and nephews are incestuous" — FALSE (Art. 38(1), public policy; uncle–niece is 3rd degree collateral).
  • "A marriage solemnized in 1990 between collateral blood relatives, legitimate or illegitimate, up to the fourth civil degree is incestuous" — FALSE under the FC (public policy). For a 1987 marriage the same statement is TRUE (NCC 81(3)).
  • Parties "two degrees" apart: either grandparent–grandchild or brother–sister. Both are incestuous under both codes, so the date does not matter.
  • A and B have the same father, different mothers, both illegitimate: incestuous (Art. 37(2), half blood, "legitimate or illegitimate").

Art. 38: void for reasons of public policy

(2) Between step-parents and step-children;
(3) Between parents-in-law and children-in-law;

Tolentino:
  • Other relationships are not impediments. Other than those in Arts. 37 and 38, no relationship, whatever its nature, is an impediment. There is no legal impediment to a marriage between a guardian and his ward (unless related by blood within the prohibited degree), nor between persons who have been convicted of adultery or concubinage.
  • Collateral blood relatives (nephews and aunts, uncles and nieces, first cousins) are prohibited on scientific and public opinion: children of first cousins are said in many instances to suffer organic defects, and Philippine society frowns on such marriages.

Art. 38(1): collateral blood relatives up to the fourth degree

  • Covers 3rd degree (uncle/aunt–niece/nephew) and 4th degree (first cousins; grand-uncle/aunt–grandniece/nephew). Siblings (2nd degree) are under Art. 37.
  • "Whether legitimate or illegitimate." Adoption does not erase blood relationship.

Who may invoke nullity: A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, the date rules, and collateral attack

A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, Sec. 2 (a)–(d). (a) Who may file. — A petition for declaration of absolute nullity of void marriage may be filed solely by the husband or the wife.
(b) Where to file. — The petition shall be filed in the Family Court.
(c) Imprescriptibility of action or defense. — An action or defense for the declaration of absolute nullity of void marriage shall not prescribe.
(d) What to allege. — A petition under Article 36 of the Family Code shall specially allege the complete facts showing that either or both parties were psychologically incapacitated from complying with the essential marital obligations of marriage at the time of the celebration of marriage even if such incapacity becomes manifest only after its celebration.
The complete facts should allege the physical manifestations, if any, as are indicative of psychological incapacity at the time of the celebration of the marriage but expert opinion need not be alleged.

The rules in order

  1. General rule (direct action): for marriages celebrated under the FC and petitions filed on or after March 15, 2003 (effectivity of the Rule), only the husband or the wife may file a petition for declaration of absolute nullity (Sec. 2(a); Enrico v. Heirs of Medinaceli; Carlos v. Sandoval).
  2. Exception — the prior spouse in a bigamous marriage: the spouse of the prior subsisting marriage may sue to declare the subsequent bigamous marriage void. He or she is the injured party whose rights the bigamous marriage directly threatens (Juliano-Llave v. Republic; Fujiki v. Marinay).
  3. Marriages under the Civil Code, or actions begun before March 15, 2003: the Rule does not apply. Any real party in interest — one who stands to be benefited or injured by the judgment — may sue, e.g., a compulsory or intestate heir (Niñal v. Bayadog; Carlos v. Sandoval; Ablaza v. Republic).
  4. Collateral attack: independently of who may file a direct petition, the nullity of a void marriage may be raised collaterally in any proceeding where it is material, even after a spouse's death, by a party whose rights are affected. The classic forum is the settlement of the estate of the deceased spouse, by compulsory or intestate heirs (Niñal; Carlos v. Sandoval; Enrico; Garcia-Quiazon v. Belen).
  5. Strangers: creditors and others with no material interest cannot sue directly. A stranger claiming an interest in estate property may bring a separate civil action (e.g., partition) against the administrator (B29, citing the collateral-attack rule).

Pepito shot his wife Teodulfa (1985). About a year and eight months later he married Norma without a license, executing an Art. 34 affidavit of five-year cohabitation. After Pepito died, his children by Teodulfa sued to declare the second marriage void. Doctrine: (1) The five-year cohabitation under Art. 34 must be exclusive and continuous and the parties must be capacitated to marry each other during the entire period; Pepito was still married during part of it, so the marriage was void for lack of a license. (2) A void marriage may be questioned by any party whose rights are affected, even after the death of a party, and collaterally. "It is not like a voidable marriage which cannot be collaterally attacked except in direct proceeding instituted during the lifetime of the parties." (3) For purposes other than remarriage, no prior judicial declaration is needed; the court may pass on the validity of the marriage in the case where it is material (e.g., succession, filiation, property). (4) The Civil Code and the FC are silent on who may file; the children, as heirs, had standing (the case predates the 2003 Rule).

Enrico v. Heirs of Sps. Medinaceli (2007)

Eulogio Medinaceli's first wife died in 2004; months later he married Lolita Enrico, claiming the Art. 34 exemption; he died in 2005. His children by the first marriage sued to declare the second marriage void. Doctrine: The second marriage was celebrated under the FC and the action filed after March 15, 2003, so A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC governs: only an aggrieved or injured spouse may file. The heirs cannot file a direct petition. They may, however, question the validity of the marriage collaterally in the proceedings for the settlement of the estate of their father.

Carlos v. Sandoval (2008)

Juan de Dios Carlos sued to declare void the 1962 marriage of his deceased brother Teofilo and Felicidad Sandoval for lack of a license. Doctrine: (1) A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC extends only to marriages covered by the FC and, as a procedural rule, applies only to proceedings begun on or after March 15, 2003. For marriages under the Civil Code, or cases begun earlier, the Rule's "only the husband or wife" limit does not apply. (2) Still, the plaintiff must be a real party in interest. A brother is not a compulsory heir; he is excluded by the decedent's legitimate or illegitimate child. If Teofilo had a son (Teofilo II), the brother has no material interest and no standing. The case was remanded to resolve filiation. (3) Nullity cannot be declared by judgment on the pleadings, summary judgment or confession of judgment. (4) Only the spouse may initiate an action to sever the marital bond for FC marriages; compulsory or intestate heirs may only collaterally attack it in estate proceedings.

Ablaza v. Republic (2010) (B29 repository)

A brother sought nullity of his deceased brother's 1949 marriage. Doctrine: A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC extends only to FC marriages and is prospective (proceedings after March 15, 2003). For a Civil Code marriage, a party who stands to gain or lose from the judgment, such as an heir, may be a proper party.

Juliano-Llave v. Republic (2011)

Sen. Mamintal Tamano, civilly married to Zorayda since 1958, married Estrellita Juliano-Llave in 1993. Zorayda and her son sued to declare the second marriage void. Doctrine: The second marriage was bigamous and void; Tamano's civil marriage was governed by the Civil Code, which does not provide for divorce, and the Muslim Code could not retroactively dissolve it. The prior spouse is "clearly the aggrieved party," as the bigamous marriage threatens the financial and property aspect of the prior marriage and causes her emotional burden. She has standing to sue (the case was also filed before the 2003 Rule).

Fujiki v. Marinay (G.R. No. 196049, June 26, 2013)

Fujiki, a Japanese national, married Marinay. Without dissolving it, Marinay married Maekara. Fujiki obtained a Japanese judgment declaring the Marinay–Maekara marriage void for bigamy and sought its recognition in the Philippines. Doctrine: (1) Sec. 2(a) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC does not apply to a petition for recognition of a foreign judgment; Rule 108 and the rules on foreign judgments apply. (2) When the right of a spouse to protect his marriage is violated, he is an injured party; the prior spouse is a real party in interest entitled to sue to declare a bigamous marriage void, and may petition a court to recognize a foreign judgment nullifying it. Once recognized, the bigamous marriage's entry may be cancelled in the civil registry.

Garcia-Quiazon v. Belen (G.R. No. 189121, July 31, 2013)

Doctrine: Elise, whose successional rights would be prejudiced by her father Eduardo's marriage to Amelia (bigamous, Amelia being still married), may impugn that marriage even after her father's death. A void marriage may be questioned directly by an action attacking it, or collaterally by raising it in a proceeding for the settlement of the estate of the deceased spouse.

Amor-Catalan v. Court of Appeals (2007) (B29 repository)

Doctrine: A petition to declare a marriage void must be prosecuted by a real party in interest with a cause of action. A first wife's standing to attack her former husband's subsequent marriage depends on whether her own marriage to him still subsists (there, a foreign divorce had been obtained); the case was remanded to determine it.

Quirit-Figarido v. Figarido (2024) (B29 repository)

Maria Lina married Edwin in 2003 while her prior marriage subsisted; both knew it. She later sought to declare the Edwin marriage void for bigamy. Doctrine: Only the aggrieved or innocent spouse may file. A party who knowingly contracted the bigamous marriage cannot invoke the court's jurisdiction to benefit from her own wrong ("he who comes to court must come with clean hands").

Note (tension): Sec. 2(a) itself says "solely by the husband or the wife," and A.M. Sec. 2(d) and Clavecilla let even the incapacitated spouse sue under Art. 36. Quirit-Figarido (2024) adds an "innocent spouse" limit for a knowingly bigamous party. Art. 40: judicial declaration of nullity of a prior marriage for purposes of remarriage

Art. 40, FC. The absolute nullity of a previous marriage may be invoked for purposes of remarriage on the basis solely of a final judgment declaring such previous marriage void. (n)
  • New provision (Tolentino). It refers to a void marriage, which in law does not exist and whose nullity can be raised at any time and collaterally. Nevertheless, for purposes of remarriage, the party must first secure a final judgment declaring it void.
  • "For purposes of remarriage" and "solely." For remarriage, the only acceptable proof of nullity is a final judgment. For other purposes (succession, filiation, property, support, a bigamy defense under Pulido), no prior separate action is needed; nullity may be proved by other evidence in the case where it is material (Domingo v. CA; Niñal).
  • Effect of non-compliance: a subsequent marriage contracted without the prior judicial declaration is void (Domingo; Mercado v. Tan; Terre v. Terre). It is void under Art. 35(4) as bigamous, and also, after the judgment, under Arts. 52–53 and 35(6) if the recording requirements are not met.
  • Retroactive: Art. 40 is a rule of procedure and applies even to a first marriage celebrated before the FC, as long as the subsequent marriage is contracted under the FC (Atienza v. Brillantes; Montañez v. Cipriano; Art. 256).
  • Even a church annulment does not satisfy Art. 40. The State does not give civil effect to church decrees.
Tolentino (minority view — rejected by jurisprudence): Without a final judgment, the prior void marriage "would constitute an impediment to the remarriage, and a marriage license may be denied." But if a party conceals the void marriage, gets a license, and remarries, Tolentino submits the second marriage, "if valid in all other respects, cannot be invalidated by want of a final judgment," because a void prior marriage is not a subsisting marriage (Art. 41, first sentence), and in an action questioning the second marriage the first's nullity may be shown collaterally. On his view Art. 40 "applies only when a license is to be obtained." This view does not prevail. Domingo v. CA, Mercado v. Tan and their progeny hold the second marriage void. Answer accordingly.

People v. Mendoza (95 Phil. 845, September 28, 1954)

Mendoza married Jovita (1936), then Olga while Jovita was alive (1941), then, after Jovita died, Carmencita (1949). He was prosecuted for bigamy for the third marriage. Doctrine: Under Sec. 29 of the Marriage Law (Act 3613), a subsequent marriage contracted during the lifetime of the first spouse is illegal and void from its performance, and no judicial decree is necessary to establish its invalidity, as distinguished from annullable marriages. The second marriage being void, there was no subsisting marriage when he married the third time. Acquitted.

People v. Aragon (100 Phil. 1033, February 28, 1957)

Doctrine: Same as Mendoza: a bigamous subsequent marriage is void from its performance and needs no judicial decree to establish its invalidity. The accused, whose second marriage was void, was acquitted of bigamy for the third.

Wiegel v. Sempio-Diy (143 SCRA 499, August 19, 1986)

Karl Wiegel sued to declare his marriage to Lilia void because she had a prior subsisting marriage to Eduardo Maxion. Lilia wanted to prove that her first marriage was void (Maxion was himself previously married) or voidable (force on both parties). Doctrine: No need to admit that evidence. If the first marriage was vitiated by force, it was only voidable (NCC 85), hence valid until annulled; no annulment had been obtained. If it was void because Maxion had a prior marriage, "such a marriage though void still needs, according to this Court, a judicial declaration of such fact." Either way she was still a married woman when she married Karl, so the Karl–Lilia marriage is void (NCC 80).

Terre v. Terre (211 SCRA 6, July 3, 1992)

Jordan Terre, a law student and later a lawyer, persuaded Dorothy that her prior marriage to her first cousin was void, married her, then abandoned her and married Helina. Doctrine (disbarment): His defense that his marriage to Dorothy was void because her prior marriage subsisted was spurious. As a lawyer he knew or should have known that, for purposes of determining whether a person is legally free to contract a second marriage, a judicial declaration that the first marriage was void is essential. Even assuming his good faith, "if we are to hold Jordan Terre to his own argument, his first marriage to complainant Dorothy Terre must be deemed valid, with the result that his second marriage to Helina Malicdem must be regarded as bigamous and criminal in character." Disbarred.

Domingo v. Court of Appeals (1993)

Soledad sued to declare void her marriage to Roberto (he had a prior subsisting marriage) and for separation of property. Roberto moved to dismiss: a void marriage needs no declaration. Doctrine: Under Art. 40, for purposes of remarriage, the only legally acceptable basis for declaring a previous marriage an absolute nullity is a final judgment. For other purposes, such as the separation of property, other evidence is acceptable, but a petition for declaration of nullity may also be filed for those purposes. Even a void bigamous marriage must be declared void by a competent court before a party may remarry; a subsequent marriage without it is void.

Atienza v. Brillantes (1995)

Judge Brillantes married De Castro in 1991 without a judicial declaration of nullity of his 1965 marriage, which he claimed was void for lack of a license. Doctrine: Art. 40 applies to remarriages entered into after the FC took effect, regardless of when the first marriage was celebrated. It is a rule of procedure, so it may be given retroactive effect; no one has a vested right in procedural rules. The judge was dismissed.

Uribe (Olaybar): In Republic v. Olaybar, the woman's signature on a marriage contract was forged; she never married. If she then marries her boyfriend without a declaration of nullity, is that marriage void under Art. 40? No need for a declaration. "The premise of Art. 40 is there is a marriage but a void marriage. In Olaybar, there was no marriage at all." The proper remedy there was correction of entries under Rule 108.
Republic v. Olaybar (G.R. No. 189538, February 10, 2014)

Merlinda Olaybar discovered, when applying for a CENOMAR, that she was registered as married to a Korean national she did not know. Doctrine: A Rule 108 petition may cancel the wife's entries in the marriage certificate where it is established, in an adversarial proceeding (with trial, testimony of the court stenographer and a document examiner), that her signature was forged and she never entered into the marriage. This is not a nullity case; it rectifies a record of a marriage that never took place.

Tolentino v. Paras (122 SCRA 525, May 30, 1983)

Amado Tolentino, married to Serafia, bigamously married Maria Clemente and was convicted of bigamy. On his death, his death certificate named Maria as his spouse. Serafia sued for a declaration that she was the lawful surviving spouse and for correction of the entry. Doctrine: The suit is a proper remedy. It is adversarial (Maria and the civil registrar were parties and contested it), not a summary proceeding. Since the invalidity of the second marriage is not in question, the entry is false and may be corrected. Entries in public documents are only presumptively correct and yield to more positive evidence.

A and B married in 1995 without a license (no exemption). In 2000, without a judicial declaration of nullity, A married C with all requisites. Status of A–C?

Answer: Void. Art. 40 requires a final judgment declaring the A–B marriage void before A may remarry (Domingo; Mercado). Tolentino's contrary view (valid if otherwise complete) is rejected.

James married Mary (1985), then Ophelia (Sept. 1988) while Mary was alive. Mary died in 1989. In 1990 James married Shirley. Is the Shirley marriage valid because the Ophelia marriage was void?

Answer: No. The Ophelia marriage was bigamous and void, and both it and the Shirley marriage fall under the FC. Under Art. 40 James needed a final judgment declaring the Ophelia marriage void before marrying Shirley. Without it, the Shirley marriage is void. Mary's death did not validate the Ophelia marriage. (A suggested answer of "valid" is wrong under Domingo and Mercado.)

Maria and Luis married in 1976. Luis was drunk at the wedding and remained a drunkard. Maria obtained a church annulment and now wants to marry Ronnie. May she?

Answer: Not yet. A church annulment has no civil effect. She must first obtain a final judicial declaration of nullity (e.g., under Art. 36, available for pre-FC marriages without prescription under RA 8533). Art. 40 applies even though the first marriage predates the FC (Atienza v. Brillantes).

Art. 40 and bigamy (RPC 349): Mercado, Tenebro, Morigo, Capili, Montañez, Vitangcol, Pulido

Art. 349, Revised Penal Code. Bigamy. — The penalty of prision mayor shall be imposed upon any person who shall contract a second or subsequent marriage before the former marriage has been legally dissolved, or before the absent spouse has been declared presumptively dead by means of a judgment rendered in the proper proceedings.

Elements (People v. Odtuhan, citing Montañez v. Cipriano):

  1. The offender has been legally married;
  2. The first marriage has not been legally dissolved or, if the spouse is absent, the absent spouse could not yet be presumed dead according to the Civil Code;
  3. He contracts a second or subsequent marriage; and
  4. The second marriage has all the essential requisites for validity, i.e., it would have been valid were it not for the subsistence of the first.
Mercado v. Tan (2000)

Dr. Vincent Mercado, married to Thelma Oliva, married Consuelo Tan. After Tan filed a bigamy complaint, he obtained a declaration of nullity of his first marriage. Doctrine: Under Art. 40, a judicial declaration of nullity of a previous marriage is necessary before a subsequent one can be legally contracted. One who enters into a subsequent marriage without first obtaining it is guilty of bigamy, even if the earlier union is characterized by statute as "void." A declaration obtained after the second marriage is immaterial; the crime was consummated upon the celebration of the second marriage. Conviction affirmed.

Morigo v. People (422 SCRA 376, February 6, 2004)

Lucio Morigo and Lucia Barrete merely signed a marriage contract on their own, with no ceremony before a solemnizing officer. Lucia later obtained a Canadian divorce; Lucio married Maria Lumbago and then sought nullity of the first "marriage." Doctrine: The general rule (Mercado) remains: a judicial declaration of nullity is needed before remarriage, even of a void marriage. But here no marriage ceremony at all was performed. "The mere private act of signing a marriage contract bears no semblance to a valid marriage and thus, needs no judicial declaration of nullity." There was no first marriage to speak of, so the first element of bigamy is missing. Acquitted.

Tenebro v. Court of Appeals (423 SCRA 272, February 18, 2004)

Veronico Tenebro, married to Hilda, married Leticia Ancajas. Charged with bigamy, he argued that his marriage to Hilda did not exist and that his marriage to Ancajas had been declared void under Art. 36. Doctrine: The declaration of nullity of the second marriage on the ground of psychological incapacity is not a defense. It is not an indicator that the second marriage lacked the essential requisites: both parties were over 18, consented freely, and married with a license before a judge and two witnesses. Although the judgment retroacts to the celebration as regards the vinculum, an Art. 36 marriage is "not without legal effects" (e.g., Art. 54 legitimacy), and one of those consequences is criminal liability for bigamy. To hold otherwise would let individuals deliberately flaw each marriage to escape the penal law. The nullity of the second marriage is not per se an argument for the avoidance of criminal liability for bigamy. Conviction affirmed.

Capili v. People (G.R. No. 183805, July 3, 2013)

James Capili, married to Shirley Tismo, married Karla Medina. The Capili–Medina marriage was later declared void, and Capili moved to dismiss the bigamy case. Doctrine: What makes a person liable for bigamy is contracting a second marriage during the subsistence of a valid first marriage. The parties should not judge for themselves the nullity of a marriage; until declared void, it is presumed to exist. He who contracts a second marriage before a judicial declaration "assumes the risk of being prosecuted for bigamy." Criminal culpability attaches at the moment of the offense; the subsequent declaration of nullity of the second marriage does not extinguish it.

People v. Odtuhan (G.R. No. 191566, July 17, 2013)

Odtuhan married Jasmin (1980), then Eleanor (1993); in 1999 his first marriage was declared void for lack of a license. Charged with bigamy in 2008, he moved to quash. Doctrine: The information alleged all four elements of bigamy and was sufficient. The subsequent judicial declaration of nullity of the first marriage is not a ground to quash; it is immaterial, since the crime was already consummated. It is essential that the second marriage, having all the essential requirements, would be valid were it not for the subsistence of the first.

Montañez v. Cipriano (684 SCRA 315, 2012)

Lourdes Cipriano married Socrates Flores (1976), then Silverio Cipriano (1983) while the first marriage subsisted. In 2003 the first marriage was declared void under Art. 36. Charged with bigamy in 2007, she argued that both marriages predated the FC, so no judicial declaration was needed. Doctrine: The RTC erred in quashing. Art. 40 applies retroactively as a procedural rule. The crime was consummated when she contracted the second marriage while the first had not been declared void; the later declaration is immaterial. The second marriage must be one that would be valid but for the first.

Vitangcol v. People (2016)

Norberto Vitangcol married Alice, then Gina. He argued the Alice marriage was void because the civil registrar certified that the marriage license number on the contract did not exist in its records. Doctrine: Conviction affirmed. A certification that a license number could not be found does not by itself prove that no license was issued, and the marriage contract enjoys a presumption of regularity. In any case, "he who contracts a second marriage before the judicial declaration of the first marriage assumes the risk of being prosecuted for bigamy." (Decided before Pulido.)

Pulido v. People (G.R. No. 220149, July 27, 2021, En Banc)

Pulido married Nora (1983), then Rowena (1995). Charged with bigamy (2007), he only later obtained a judgment declaring the first marriage void for lack of a license. Doctrine: Acquitted. The Court abandoned the line (Mercado, Tenebro and their progeny) holding that a judicial declaration of nullity of the first and/or second marriage cannot be raised as a defense in bigamy. (1) Art. 40 was not intended to amend RPC 349; it concerns the civil validity of a remarriage. (2) A void ab initio marriage is never valid; it is wrong to say it is "valid until declared void." A judicial declaration of nullity, even obtained after the second marriage, retroacts to the date of the first celebration. (3) The accused may therefore prove, in the bigamy case itself, that the first marriage was void ab initio, even without a prior judicial declaration; there is then no valid first marriage, and element (1) fails. (4) The accused may likewise show that the second marriage is void on a ground other than its being bigamous; it then would not have been valid even without the first, and element (4) fails. (5) Art. 40 still governs civil consequences: the second marriage, contracted without a prior declaration, remains void, and the parties may face other liabilities.

Donato v. Luna (160 SCRA 441, April 15, 1988)

Donato was charged with bigamy. His second wife, Paz, sued to annul the second marriage for deceit. Donato moved to suspend the criminal case. Doctrine: A prejudicial question is one whose resolution is a logical antecedent of the issue in the criminal case and determines the guilt or innocence of the accused. The nullity of the second marriage, sought by the second wife on the ground of her consent being obtained by deceit, is not determinative of Donato's guilt. Following Landicho v. Relova, an action to annul a marriage is prejudicial to a bigamy case only if the accused proves that his own consent to the second marriage was obtained by duress, violence and intimidation, so that his act was involuntary. Donato had lived with Paz for five years and signed an Art. 76 affidavit with her, belying any duress. He merely sought to evade prosecution.

In 1981 JR married 16-year-old Inka before a mayor. In 1995 he married Elena. Charged with bigamy, JR claims the 1981 marriage lacked a license and the 1995 marriage had no ceremony. Valid defense?

A second marriage void for lack of a ceremony (a ground other than bigamy) means it would not have been valid even without the first. Art. 41, FC. A marriage contracted by any person during subsistence of a previous marriage shall be null and void, unless before the celebration of the subsequent marriage, the prior spouse had been absent for four consecutive years and the spouse present has a well-founded belief that the absent spouse was already dead. In case of disappearance where there is danger of death under the circumstances set forth in the provisions of Article 391 of the Civil Code, an absence of only two years shall be sufficient.

  1. The absent spouse has been absent for four consecutive years, or two years where the disappearance was under danger of death (NCC 391 circumstances);

  3. The present spouse has a well-founded belief that the absentee is already dead; and
  4. The present spouse institutes a summary proceeding for the declaration of presumptive death of the absentee, and obtains the judgment before the subsequent marriage.

Tolentino on Art. 41:
  • Prior voidable marriage: a prior annullable marriage not yet set aside by final judgment (or not yet dissolved by death) is a subsisting marriage; a remarriage is void.
  • Period is mandatory. It cannot be shortened by the present spouse's good faith. A wife whose husband has been away three years with no circumstance suggesting death, who sincerely believes him dead because he always wrote to her and has stopped, contracts a void second marriage "notwithstanding her good faith."
  • The Civil Code also allowed remarriage if the absentee, though absent for less than seven years, "is generally considered as dead and believed to be so by the spouse present." The FC eliminated this; the periods "must be observed."
  • "Absent" spouse: the provision is of American (California) origin. The spouse who has been left or deserted is the "spouse present." It is the deserting spouse's duty to keep the other informed of his whereabouts, so it is the abandoned spouse's remarriage that the article contemplates, not the deserter's. A wife living abroad who deserted her husband to live with her parents was held "absent," though the husband later came home.
  • Burden of proof: where a person has two successive marriages, a presumption arises in favor of the validity of the second; whoever attacks it must prove that the first had not been dissolved when the second was contracted.

The well-founded belief: strict standard

Republic v. Nolasco (1993)

Gregorio Nolasco, a seaman, married Janet Monica Parker, a British national. She left after giving birth while he was at sea. His search consisted of letters (returned), inquiries with friends in her hometown, and asking around while on shipboard. Doctrine: No well-founded belief of death. The present spouse must show a belief founded on diligent, sincere inquiries; a perfunctory search does not suffice. The requirements of the law prevail over special circumstances (e.g., that the child will grow up motherless), since "the law does not view marriage like an ordinary contract." The State's policy is to protect marriage against collusion and against the convenience of the present spouse.

Republic v. Cantor (G.R. No. 184621, December 10, 2013)

Jerry Cantor left the conjugal home after a quarrel in January 1998 and was not heard from. In 2002 Maria Fe petitioned for a declaration of his presumptive death. She had asked his relatives, neighbors and friends and checked hospitals whenever she heard of an unidentified patient. Doctrine: (1) Procedure: the RTC judgment in an Art. 41 summary proceeding is immediately final and executory (Art. 247); the State's remedy is a petition for certiorari under Rule 65 with the CA, which may then be elevated to the SC by Rule 45. (2) Merits: the present spouse bears the burden of proving all the requisites. The well-founded belief must result from "diligent and reasonable efforts and inquiries" to locate the absent spouse, judged by circumstances before and after the disappearance and the nature and extent of the inquiries. Maria Fe's was a "passive search": she did not report to the police or seek help from the media or other agencies. The strict standard guards against spouses using Art. 41 as a shortcut out of a marriage ("to circumvent the policy of the law"). Petition denied.

Republic v. Granada (G.R. No. 187512, June 13, 2012)

Cyrus Granada left for Taiwan in 1991 and lost contact. In 2005 Yolanda obtained a declaration of his presumptive death; the OSG filed an ordinary appeal to the CA. Doctrine: A petition for declaration of presumptive death under Art. 41 is a summary proceeding under the FC (Art. 253 with Arts. 238 et seq.), and the judgment is immediately final and executory under Art. 247. An ordinary appeal does not lie; the CA correctly dismissed it. The aggrieved party's remedy is certiorari under Rule 65 on grave abuse of discretion.

Republic v. Bermudez-Lorino (2005)

Doctrine: In summary proceedings under the FC, there is no reglementary period for appeal; the judgment is immediately final and executory. A notice of appeal is improper. (The Court nonetheless let the CA decision stand because the RTC judgment had become final.)

Republic v. Orcelino-Villanueva (2015)

Edna, working in Japan, learned that her husband Romeo had left their home; her search consisted mainly of asking his relatives. Doctrine: Following Cantor, inquiries limited to relatives, without seeking the help of the authorities or other reasonable means, do not establish a well-founded belief. The declaration of presumptive death was set aside.

Republic v. Fenol; Republic v. Ponce-Pilapil (2020) (B29 repository)

Doctrine: Reiterating Cantor. A declaration of presumptive death requires a well-founded belief supported by diligent, serious and exhaustive efforts; mere absence, however long (eight years in Fenol, six in Ponce-Pilapil), or limited inquiries are insufficient. Without it the prior marriage subsists and any subsequent marriage is void.

Tadeo-Matias v. Republic (2018)

A wife petitioned under Art. 41 to declare her soldier husband, missing since 1979, presumptively dead, in order to claim benefits. Doctrine: A petition for declaration of presumptive death cannot prosper as an independent action for purposes other than remarriage. Art. 41 is available only "for the purpose of contracting the subsequent marriage." For other purposes (benefits, succession), the presumption under NCC 390–391 arises by operation of law and may be invoked in the proper proceeding without a separate declaration.

In re Szatrow (49 O.G. 243)

Doctrine: A petition merely asking that the absent husband be declared presumed dead after seven years cannot be entertained. There is no right to enforce nor remedy prayed for. The presumption is already established by law, and a judicial pronouncement, even if final, would still be only a disputable presumption. The presumption may be invoked in an action or special proceeding where it is material, but cannot itself be the subject of one.

Lukban v. Republic; Gue v. Republic (98 Phil. 574, February 29, 1956; 107 Phil. 381, March 24, 1960)

Doctrine (Civil Code era): A petition for a judicial declaration that an absent spouse is presumptively dead, so that the petitioner may remarry, is not authorized; the law did not require it for remarriage under NCC 83(2), and the presumption arises by law. (The FC reversed this for remarriage: Art. 41 now requires a summary proceeding.)

Jones v. Hortiguela (64 Phil. 179, March 6, 1937)

Doctrine: For purposes of the civil marriage law, it is not necessary to have the former spouse judicially declared an absentee. The declaration of absence under the Civil Code serves only to protect the absentee's estate. For remarriage, the law then required only that the former spouse had been absent seven consecutive years, that the spouse present did not know him to be living, and that he was generally reputed dead and so believed by the spouse present at the time of the second marriage.

Armas v. Calisterio (2000)

Marietta's first husband disappeared in 1947. In 1958, without any judicial declaration, she married Teodorico Calisterio. After Teodorico died (1992), his sister claimed the second marriage was bigamous and void. Doctrine: The validity of a marriage is governed by the law in force at its celebration. Under NCC 83(2), a subsequent marriage after the absent spouse had been absent seven consecutive years (or generally believed dead) was valid without any judicial declaration of presumptive death; that requirement exists only under Art. 41 of the FC and does not apply retroactively. The marriage, presumed valid and never annulled, was valid; Marietta was the surviving spouse with her share in the conjugal property.

SSS v. Vda. de Bailon (2006)

Clemente Bailon had his wife Alice declared presumptively dead (1970) and married Teresita (1983). After his death, Alice's relatives and the SSS questioned Teresita's death benefits, claiming Alice had reappeared. Doctrine: Under the Civil Code (NCC 83(2)), the subsequent marriage was valid until declared null by a competent court in an action by the absentee or either spouse of the subsequent marriage. Under the FC, it is terminated by the recording of an affidavit of reappearance. In either case, the absentee's mere reappearance, even if made known to the spouses of the subsequent marriage, does not terminate it. No step had been taken, so the Clemente–Teresita marriage subsisted until his death, and Teresita was his surviving spouse.

The summary proceeding (FC Arts. 238–253)

  • Art. 253: the rules in Chapters 2 and 3 of Title XI (summary judicial proceedings in the Family Law) govern summary proceedings under Arts. 41, 51, 69, 73, 96, 124 and 217, insofar as applicable.
  • Filed in the court of the place where the petitioner resides; the petition is verified; the court notifies the parties; the case is decided expeditiously without regard to technical rules.
  • Art. 247: "The judgment of the court shall be immediately final and executory." No ordinary appeal; remedy is certiorari (Rule 65) before the CA (Granada; Cantor; Bermudez-Lorino).
  • The declaration is only a prima facie presumption. It is "without prejudice to the effect of reappearance" (Art. 41).
  • If the declaration was obtained by fraud (e.g., the present spouse knew the "absentee" was alive and concealed her whereabouts), the absent spouse's remedy is a petition for annulment of judgment under Rule 47 (Santos v. Santos).
Santos v. Santos (2014)

Ricardo obtained a declaration of the presumptive death of his wife Celerina, falsely alleging that she had left and that he had searched for her and misstating her residence, so she was never notified. He then remarried. Celerina sought annulment of the judgment; the CA said her remedy was an affidavit of reappearance. Doctrine: The proper remedy is a petition for annulment of judgment (Rule 47) on the grounds of extrinsic fraud and lack of jurisdiction. An affidavit of reappearance is not adequate: (1) recording it terminates the subsequent marriage but presupposes a valid subsequent marriage and leaves the fraudulent judgment and its effects standing; (2) Art. 42 applies where the reappearance is not disputed and the absent spouse merely reappears; Celerina contests the very basis of the declaration, since she was never absent. If the judgment is annulled, the subsequent marriage is bigamous and void.

Answer: No. Four years have passed, but W has not proved a well-founded belief arising from diligent and reasonable efforts. Hers was a passive search (Cantor; Nolasco; Orcelino-Villanueva).

Lito, a co-pilot, disappeared in 1998 when his flight vanished over the Pacific. In 2008, without any judicial proceeding, his wife Lita married Jaime. In 2010 Lita saw Lito alive in China. Status of the Lita–Jaime marriage?

Answer: Void (Art. 35(4) with Art. 41). Two years' absence under danger of death would have sufficed, but the FC requires a judicial declaration of presumptive death before the subsequent marriage. Mere absence, however long, does not authorize remarriage. The reason for nullity is the lack of the Art. 41 declaration, not merely that the first marriage subsists.

"Eli and Fely married seven years after the disappearance of Chona, Eli's wife, whose plane crashed in the West Philippine Sea." Status?

Answer: Void as bigamous if, as the facts suggest by their silence, Eli obtained no judicial declaration of presumptive death. If he obtained one, the marriage would be valid under Art. 41 (danger of death; two years suffices), absent bad faith of both (Art. 44). State the assumption.

In 1987 Sonny and Lulu, Filipinos, married here. Sonny went to Canada, obtained a divorce in 1987 and married Auring. In 1990, not hearing from him, Lulu married Tirso. Sonny died in 1991. Status of Lulu–Tirso?

Answer: Void ab initio. A foreign divorce between two Filipinos is not recognized (Art. 15, NCC; Art. 26(2) applies only to mixed marriages), so Lulu was still married to Sonny. Her 1990 remarriage does not fall under Art. 41: only about three years of absence, no well-founded belief of death (she knew of the divorce), no judicial declaration. Sonny's death in 1991 does not validate it.

Art. 42: reappearance and termination of the subsequent marriage

Art. 42, FC. The subsequent marriage referred to in the preceding Article shall be automatically terminated by the recording of the affidavit of reappearance of the absent spouse, unless there is a judgment annulling the previous marriage or declaring it void ab initio.
A sworn statement of the fact and circumstances of reappearance shall be recorded in the civil registry of the residence of the parties to the subsequent marriage at the instance of any interested person, with due notice to the spouses of the subsequent marriage and without prejudice to the fact of reappearance being judicially determined in case such fact is disputed. (n)
  • Status before termination: the subsequent marriage under Art. 41 is valid. It is not among the voidable marriages in Art. 45. Under the Civil Code it was annullable (Tolentino).
  • What terminates it: the recording of the sworn statement (affidavit) of reappearance in the civil registry of the residence of the parties to the subsequent marriage. Not the reappearance itself, not the execution of the affidavit, not its filing.
  • Who may cause the recording: "any interested person" — not necessarily the reappearing spouse. Any of the three parties is an interested person; so is a child of the former marriage (Tolentino).
  • Notice: due notice to the spouses of the subsequent marriage.
  • If disputed: the fact of reappearance may be judicially determined. Tolentino: an action in court to prove the reappearance and obtain a declaration of termination is not precluded and is "the more advisable course" if the spouses may dispute it.
  • Exception ("unless"): if the previous marriage has been annulled or declared void by judgment, reappearance does not terminate the subsequent marriage. There is no prior bond to revive.
  • When no action is taken: mere reappearance, even if known to the spouses of the subsequent marriage, does not terminate it (Tolentino; SSS v. Bailon). Meanwhile the reappeared spouse cannot remarry; he is still married, his marriage being neither dissolved nor terminated by the subsequent marriage.
  • Termination by death: if the subsequent marriage ends by death rather than by recording, the effects of dissolution of a valid marriage arise. The good or bad faith of either spouse can no longer be raised, except that if both were in bad faith (Art. 44) the marriage is void and may be attacked collaterally at any time, even after a spouse's death (Tolentino).
Tolentino (status of the first marriage): The first marriage is not dissolved by the subsequent one; it is wrong to say the second marriage dissolves the first and the first is "revived" on termination. Rather, because the second marriage was contracted on a presumption of death, that presumption continues despite physical reappearance, and by fiction of law the absentee is still regarded as legally absent until the subsequent marriage is terminated. The effects of the first marriage are suspended, like a de facto legal separation, and revive when the second marriage is terminated. The Code leaves serious doubts (e.g., ownership of properties acquired during the absence; adultery if the wife has relations with the reappeared first husband; the status of a child so conceived).
Uribe on Art. 42 (2025 class):

  • "If there is a recording of the affidavit of reappearance, the marriage will be terminated, on the premise that the marriage is valid. Do you agree?" Not necessarily. If the affidavit is executed by a third person, not by the absent spouse himself, the spouses of the subsequent marriage may dispute it ("If nag-sinungaling lang"), and then a judicial determination is needed. If the absentee himself executes it, "alangan naman madispute pa yan."
  • Termination presupposes the validity of the subsequent marriage.

Art. 43: effects of termination of the subsequent marriage

Art. 43, FC. The termination of the subsequent marriage referred to in the preceding Article shall produce the following effects:
(1) The children of the subsequent marriage conceived prior to its termination shall be considered legitimate, and their custody and support in case of dispute shall be decided by the court in a proper proceeding;
(2) The absolute community of property or the conjugal partnership, as the case may be, shall be dissolved and liquidated, but if either spouse contracted said marriage in bad faith, his or her share of the net profits of the community property or conjugal partnership property shall be forfeited in favor of the common children or, if there are none, the children of the guilty spouse by a previous marriage or in default of children, the innocent spouse;
(3) Donations by reason of marriage shall remain valid, except that if the donee contracted the marriage in bad faith, such donations made to said donee are revoked by operation of law;
(4) The innocent spouse may revoke the designation of the other spouse who acted in bad faith as beneficiary in any insurance policy, even if such designation be stipulated as irrevocable; and
(5) The spouse who contracted the subsequent marriage in bad faith shall be disqualified to inherit from the innocent spouse by testate and intestate succession. (n)

Paragraph by paragraph

  1. Children: legitimate if conceived before termination (i.e., before the recording). They need not be born before termination. Custody and support, if disputed, are decided by the court.
  2. Property: the ACP or CPG of the subsequent marriage is dissolved and liquidated. The bad-faith spouse forfeits his or her share of the net profits (not the whole share of the property) in this order:
    1. the common children;
    2. if none, the children of the guilty spouse by a previous marriage;
    3. in default of children, the innocent spouse.
  3. Donations propter nuptias: remain valid, but those made to a donee in bad faith are revoked by operation of law. No act of revocation is needed.
  4. Insurance: the innocent spouse may revoke (an option, not automatic) the designation of the bad-faith spouse as beneficiary, even if stipulated as irrevocable.
  5. Succession: the bad-faith spouse is disqualified to inherit from the innocent spouse, by testate and intestate succession. (The innocent spouse is not disqualified from inheriting from the guilty one.)
  • Bad faith here means knowledge, at the time of the subsequent marriage, that the absent spouse was alive.
  • Through Art. 50, paragraphs (2), (3), (4) and (5) (not (1)) also apply "in the proper cases" to marriages declared void or annulled under Arts. 40 and 45. Children of annulled marriages are covered instead by Art. 54.
  • Through Art. 63(2) (legal separation), the guilty spouse's share in the net profits is forfeited as provided in Art. 43(2).
Quiz traps:
  • "... forfeited in favor of the innocent spouse, or if none, the common children" — FALSE (order reversed). Order: common children → guilty spouse's children by a previous marriage → innocent spouse.
  • "Children of the subsequent marriage conceived after the reappearance of the absentee shall be illegitimate" — FALSE. Termination is by recording; a child conceived after physical reappearance but before recording is legitimate.
  • "Donations by reason of marriage to the bad-faith donee may be revoked by the donor" — FALSE under Art. 43(3): revoked by operation of law.
  • "The innocent spouse may revoke ... unless stipulated as irrevocable" — FALSE: "even if."
  • Art. 43(5) disqualifies the bad-faith spouse from inheriting from the innocent spouse only.

Art. 44: both spouses in bad faith

Art. 44, FC. If both spouses of the subsequent marriage acted in bad faith, said marriage shall be void ab initio and all donations by reason of marriage and testamentary dispositions made by one in favor of the other are revoked by operation of law. (n)
  • Applies even if the present spouse obtained a judicial declaration of presumptive death and all essential and formal requisites are present. This is one of the "all requisites present, yet void" cases.
  • Effects: (1) void ab initio; (2) all donations by reason of marriage made by one in favor of the other are revoked by operation of law; (3) all testamentary dispositions made by one in favor of the other are revoked by operation of law. No act of revocation by either spouse is needed.
  • Being void, the marriage may be attacked collaterally at any time, even after the death of a spouse, and the Art. 44 effects apply even if it was dissolved by death (Tolentino; B29).
  • Property: void marriage with both in bad faith — Art. 148 (the parties were not capacitated to marry each other because of the subsisting prior marriage); the forfeiture rules apply "even if both parties are in bad faith" (Art. 148, last par.).

Which void marriages does Art. 50 cover?

  • Only marriages "declared [void] ab initio ... under Article 40" (and annulled under Art. 45). In Diño, the Court read the Art. 40 case as the subsequent marriage contracted without a prior judicial declaration of nullity of the first.
  • All other void marriages (Arts. 35(1)–(3), (5), 36, 37, 38, 44) never produced an ACP or CPG. Their property relations are governed by Art. 147 or 148 (Valdes; Diño), liquidated under the rules on co-ownership.
  • For those marriages, the decree of nullity issues on finality of the judgment; it does not wait for liquidation, partition and delivery of presumptive legitimes (Diño, interpreting Sec. 19(1) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC).
Valdes v. RTC, Quezon City (1996)

Antonio Valdes and Consuelo Gomez, married in 1971 with five children, had their marriage declared void under Art. 36 for mutual psychological incapacity. The RTC applied Art. 147 to their properties, including the family home. Valdes argued that Arts. 50, 51, 52 and 102/129 (conjugal liquidation) should govern. Doctrine: In a void marriage, regardless of the cause, the property relations during cohabitation are governed by Art. 147 or 148. Art. 147 covers a man and woman capacitated to marry each other living exclusively as husband and wife under a void marriage (e.g., Art. 36); wages and salaries are owned in equal shares and property acquired by work or industry is co-owned, with a presumption of joint acquisition and equal shares; a party who did not participate in acquisition is deemed to have contributed through care of the family and household. Arts. 50–52 (and 102, 129) apply only to voidable marriages and, exceptionally, to void marriages under Art. 40.

Diño v. Diño (2011)

Alain and Ma. Caridad Diño lived together (1984–1994), reconciled (1996), and married in 1998. The marriage was declared void under Art. 36, but the RTC ordered that the decree issue only after liquidation, partition and distribution of their properties under Art. 147. Doctrine: Error. Art. 50 and Sec. 19(1) of A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC (decree after liquidation) apply only to marriages declared void under Art. 40 or annulled under Art. 45. An Art. 36 marriage never created a conjugal or community regime; its property is co-owned under Art. 147 and may be partitioned under the Civil Code rules on co-ownership, independently of the decree. The decree should issue upon finality of the judgment. Art. 147 itself protects the common children through forfeiture of the bad-faith party's share.

Yaptinchay v. Torres (28 SCRA 489, June 9, 1969)

Teresita lived with Isidro Yaptinchay, who was still married to Josefina, and claimed a share in property acquired during the cohabitation. Doctrine: (1) A man and woman living as husband and wife are presumed married; but (2) where there is an impediment (a bigamous relationship), the co-ownership rule of NCC 144 is "circumscribed by conditions." The claimant must show she actually contributed to the acquisition of the property. Until then she has no clear present right warranting injunction. Property built during Isidro's valid marriage, on his lot, belonged to his estate under the special administratrix. (This is the forerunner of FC 148: actual joint contribution required when the parties are not capacitated to marry each other.)

Quiz traps:
  • "Children conceived and born outside a valid marriage are illegitimate" — FALSE (not absolutely true): Art. 36 and Art. 53 children are legitimate though born of void marriages.
  • "If a child was born after the judicial declaration of nullity of his parents' marriage, the child is illegitimate" — FALSE: a child conceived before finality of an Art. 36 judgment is legitimate.
  • "Children conceived or born before the promulgation of judgment of annulment or absolute nullity under Art. 36 are legitimate" — TRUE per key (narrower than "has become final and executory," but not false).
  • "Children born after the dissolution of the marriage of the parents may be legitimate" — TRUE ("may").
  • "Children of the subsequent marriage under Art. 53 are illegitimate because the marriage is void" — FALSE.
  • "If the judgment and partition are not recorded, they are void" — FALSE: they "shall not affect third persons" (Art. 52). It is the remarriage that is void (Art. 53).
  • Presumptive legitimes are computed as of the date of the final judgment of the trial court (Art. 51).

Procedure in actions for declaration of nullity (A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC; Art. 48)

Art. 48, FC. In all cases of annulment or declaration of absolute nullity of marriage, the Court shall order the prosecuting attorney or fiscal assigned to it to appear on behalf of the State to take steps to prevent collusion between the parties and to take care that evidence is not fabricated or suppressed.
In the cases referred to in the preceding paragraph, no judgment shall be based upon a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment. (88a)
Art. 2035, Civil Code (relevant items). No compromise upon the following questions shall be valid: (1) The civil status of persons; (2) The validity of a marriage or a legal separation; (3) Any ground for legal separation; (4) Future support; (5) The jurisdiction of courts; (6) Future legitime.
  • Scope: the Rule governs petitions for declaration of absolute nullity and annulment under the FC; the Rules of Court apply suppletorily. Effective March 15, 2003. It does not govern petitions to recognize foreign judgments (Fujiki) or foreign divorces (Republic v. Cote).
  • Who may file: solely the husband or the wife (Sec. 2(a)), with the exceptions discussed above.
  • Where: the Family Court (Sec. 2(b)) of the province or city where the petitioner or the respondent has been residing for at least six months prior to filing, or, for a non-resident respondent, where he may be found, at the petitioner's election.
  • Petition: verified, personally signed by the petitioner, with a certification against forum shopping; copies served on the OSG and the city or provincial prosecutor. For Art. 36: complete facts and physical manifestations; expert opinion need not be alleged (Sec. 2(d)).
  • No motion to dismiss except on lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter or the parties; other grounds may be raised as affirmative defenses in the answer (Sec. 7). The denial of a motion to dismiss is interlocutory and generally not reviewable by certiorari (Aurelio v. Aurelio, B29).
  • No default. If the respondent does not answer, the court orders the public prosecutor to investigate whether there is collusion and to report; the prosecutor appears for the State throughout to prevent collusion and fabrication or suppression of evidence (Art. 48; Tolentino v. Villanueva; Malcampo-Sin v. Sin: non-participation of the State requires remand).
  • No judgment on stipulation of facts, confession of judgment, judgment on the pleadings or summary judgment (Art. 48; NCC 2035; Jocson v. Robles; Carlos v. Sandoval). Collusion is not presumed; a mere monetary settlement does not prove it.
  • Support and custody pendente lite (Art. 49): see the voidable section.
  • Appeal: a motion for reconsideration or new trial is a precondition to appeal (Sec. 20). The OSG, as defensor vinculi, may appeal judgments granting nullity. A final judgment of nullity may be attacked only by annulment of judgment under Rule 47 (Tortal v. Taniguchi, B29; Santos v. Santos for presumptive death).
  • Decree: for Arts. 40 and 45 cases, issued after the liquidation, partition and distribution and delivery of presumptive legitimes are done (Art. 50; Sec. 19(1)); for other void marriages, on finality (Diño). The decree and partition are recorded (Art. 52).
  • Death of a party before entry of judgment: the case is closed and terminated, without prejudice to the settlement of the estate in proper proceedings (Sec. 24). After entry of judgment, the judgment binds the parties and their successors in interest in the settlement of the estate.
  • Res judicata: psychological incapacity and lack of a license are different grounds for the same cause of action (the status of the marriage). A party whose Art. 36 petition was dismissed on the merits cannot file a new petition on lack of license; all grounds must be raised in the first suit (Mallion v. Alcantara, B29). A second petition is not forum shopping if the first was dismissed without prejudice (Barcelona).
Jocson v. Robles (22 SCRA 521, February 10, 1968)

Gloria Jocson sued to declare her marriage to Ricardo Robles void as bigamous (he had a prior subsisting marriage). Robles admitted the prior marriage and alleged that his consent to the second had been obtained by force, and the parties sought a summary judgment on their pleadings and admissions. Doctrine: Summary judgment was properly denied. A marriage contracted during the subsistence of a previous marriage is void unless the prior spouse had been absent and believed dead, but its nullity cannot be decreed on the parties' admissions. Arts. 88 and 101 of the Civil Code prohibit a decree upon stipulation of facts or confession of judgment; the evidence pointed to collusion. These safeguards apply to actions for declaration of nullity as well as annulment.

Tolentino v. Villanueva (56 SCRA 1, March 15, 1974)

Doctrine: Arts. 88 and 101 of the Civil Code prohibit a decision in annulment and legal separation suits based on stipulation of facts or confession of judgment, and direct that when the defendant does not appear, the prosecutor must inquire whether there is collusion and, if none, intervene for the State to prevent fabrication of evidence. The prosecutor's investigation for collusion is mandatory, and the plaintiff cannot refuse to submit to it.

Republic v. Cote (G.R. No. 212860, March 14, 2018)

Florie Cote sought recognition of a Hawaii divorce obtained by her husband Rhomel, who had become a U.S. citizen, under Art. 26(2). The RTC denied the OSG's notice of appeal for failure to first file a motion for reconsideration, citing A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC. Doctrine: A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC does not govern petitions for recognition of a foreign divorce, which are governed by the Rules of Court (Rule 108 and the rules on foreign judgments). The motion-for-reconsideration precondition of the Rule does not apply; the OSG's ordinary appeal was proper. For Art. 26(2), what matters is the alien citizenship of the spouse at the time the divorce is obtained.

Mallion v. Alcantara (2006) (B29 repository)

Doctrine: A petition for nullity on the ground of lack of a license, filed after a prior petition on the ground of psychological incapacity was dismissed with finality, is barred by res judicata. Both rest on the same cause of action (the validity of the marriage); a party may not split grounds and litigate piecemeal. He is also bound by his admission in the first petition that the marriage was celebrated in accordance with law.

Application drills (Uribe style)

1. A, married to B, married C in 1990, believing his first marriage void because it was solemnized without a license. No judicial declaration was obtained. Status of A–C? Is A liable for bigamy?

Answer: A–C is void (Art. 40; Domingo; Mercado). Bigamy: under Pulido, A may prove in the criminal case that the A–B marriage was void ab initio for lack of a license; if he does, there is no valid first marriage and no bigamy. If he fails, he is liable. Answer conditionally: "A will be acquitted if he proves the first marriage void."

2. Same facts, but at the time of the complaint A has a pending action for declaration of nullity of the A–B marriage and moves to suspend the bigamy case. Rule. What if the pending action is for nullity of the A–C marriage on the ground of Art. 36?

Answer: On the first, the issue (validity of the first marriage) may determine guilt after Pulido, so suspension may be granted, but suspension is not acquittal. On the second: under Tenebro, nullity of the second marriage on Art. 36 is not a defense, so no prejudicial question. Under Uribe's reading of Pulido, only nullity for being bigamous is not a defense, so Art. 36 nullity of the second marriage may be. State both and flag that Tenebro might still be followed on identical facts.

3. A and B, half-siblings, both illegitimate, married in 1985. Status? What if they were first cousins? What if step-siblings?

Answer: Half-siblings: incestuous and void under both codes (NCC 81(2); FC 37(2)). First cousins in 1985: incestuous under NCC 81(3) (under the FC it would be void for public policy). Step-siblings in 1985: void under NCC 80(7) (valid under the FC).

4. H and W, married in 2000, both had personality traits making cohabitation impossible from the start. They jointly ask the court to declare the marriage void and submit a stipulation of facts admitting mutual psychological incapacity. Will the court decide on the stipulation?

Answer: No. No judgment may be based on a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment (Art. 48; NCC 2035; Jocson v. Robles). The prosecutor must appear to prevent collusion. If proven at trial, mutual incapacity is a ground (no in pari delicto), the children conceived or born before finality are legitimate (Art. 54), and the property is governed by Art. 147 (Valdes).

5. B obtained a judicial declaration of the presumptive death of her husband A (absent five years, diligent search). She married C, who knew A was alive. A reappears but no one records an affidavit. B dies. C claims as surviving spouse; A claims too. Who is B's surviving spouse?

Answer: C. The B–C marriage was valid (only C in bad faith; Art. 44 requires both) and was never terminated, since mere reappearance does not terminate it (Art. 42; SSS v. Bailon). It was dissolved by death. Following Tolentino, the effects of dissolution of a valid marriage arise and good or bad faith can no longer be raised. (Had both B and C been in bad faith, the marriage would be void under Art. 44 and attackable collaterally even after death.)

6. X married Y in 2005 by signing a marriage contract in a lawyer's office with no officer present and no ceremony. In 2010 X married Z in church. Status of X–Z? Bigamy?

Answer: The X–Y "marriage" is no marriage at all: it "bears no semblance to a valid marriage" and needs no judicial declaration (Morigo; cf. Uribe on Olaybar: "the premise of Art. 40 is there is a marriage but a void marriage"). X–Z is valid if otherwise complete; no bigamy.

7. In 2015 Pedro, intending to marry his neighbor Susan, poisoned his wife. He was never charged. In 2018 he married Susan, who suspected nothing. In 2024 Pedro's son by his first wife seeks to have the marriage declared void. Status, and may the son file?

Answer: Void under Art. 38(9): intent to marry Susan; conviction unnecessary; Susan's ignorance immaterial (Tolentino). The son cannot file a direct petition while Pedro and Susan are both alive (A.M. Sec. 2(a)); after Pedro's death he may attack the marriage collaterally in the settlement of the estate.

8. A and B married in 1986. A, abandoned since 1990, obtains a judicial declaration of nullity under Art. 36 in 2025. Can B argue the action prescribed in 1998, ten years after the FC took effect?

Answer: No. The E.O. 227 ten-year period for pre-FC marriages under Art. 36 was deleted by RA 8533 (1998). The action does not prescribe (Art. 39).

Last-minute checklist

  • "All requisites present" ≠ valid: Arts. 35(4), (5), (6), 36, 37, 38, 40, 44, 53. Start the answer with "No, not necessarily."
  • Art. 35: (1) below 18 "even with the consent of parents or guardians"; (2) no authority, unless "either or both" believed in good faith (mistake of fact only); (3) no license except Arts. 27–34; (4) bigamous "not falling under Article 41"; (5) mistake as to identity (not name, qualities); (6) Art. 53 remarriage.
  • Art. 26(1) exceptions: 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37, 38. Not 35(2), (3).
  • Civil Code ages: 16/14. FC: 18. Check the date of every marriage (Aug. 3, 1988).
  • Art. 36: at the time of celebration; incapacity to comply; essential obligations Arts. 68–71, 220, 221, 225. Santos: gravity, juridical antecedence, incurability.
  • Tan-Andal (2021): legal not medical; not a mental disorder or personality disorder; expert not required; ordinary witnesses; clear and convincing; incurability in the legal sense (as to the specific partner); gravity excludes mild peculiarities, refusal, neglect, difficulty, ill will.
  • Barcelona / Sec. 2(d): allege complete facts and physical manifestations; expert opinion need not be alleged. Choa/Hamano: personal medical exam not sine qua non. Encelan/Hamano: infidelity and abandonment are legal separation grounds.
  • Uribe's Art. 36 defense sequence: (1) complied; (2) refusal, not incapacity; (3) no gravity/antecedence/incurability. Both incapacitated → prospers (no in pari delicto). Incapacitated spouse's remarriage not necessarily void.
  • Art. 37: direct line any degree; brothers and sisters full or half blood; legitimate or illegitimate. Uncle–niece and first cousins: FC 38(1) public policy; NCC 81(3) incestuous.
  • Art. 38: collaterals to 4th degree; step-parent/step-child; parents-in-law/children-in-law (new in FC); adopter–adopted; surviving spouse of adopter–adopted; surviving spouse of adopted–adopter; adopted–legitimate child of adopter; adopted children of the same adopter; killing with intent to marry.
  • Not impediments under FC: step-siblings (void under NCC 80(7)), in-law siblings, guardian–ward, second cousins (6th degree), persons convicted of adultery or concubinage.
  • 38(9): intent to marry the particular person; no conviction needed (Tolentino); other party's knowledge not needed. NCC 80(6): "found guilty," motive immaterial.
  • Art. 39: no prescription (RA 8533 deleted E.O. 227's 10-year rule for pre-FC Art. 36 marriages). Imprescriptibility ≠ standing.
  • Who may file: husband or wife (FC marriages, filed on/after March 15, 2003); prior spouse in bigamy (Fujiki; Juliano-Llave); Civil Code marriages or pre-2003 cases: real party in interest (Niñal; Carlos; Ablaza). Heirs: collateral attack in estate settlement (Enrico; Carlos; Garcia-Quiazon). Both spouses alive → no third party except the prior spouse.
  • Void marriage: attack collaterally, even after death; cannot be ratified. Voidable: only directly, only during lifetime.
  • Art. 40: final judgment needed only for remarriage; "solely." For other purposes, prove nullity in the case (Domingo; Niñal). Without it, the remarriage is void. Retroactive as procedural (Atienza). Church annulment insufficient. No declaration needed where there was no marriage at all (Morigo; Olaybar).
  • Bigamy elements: legally married; not dissolved / absentee not presumed dead; second marriage; second would be valid but for the first.
  • Pulido (2021): Art. 40 does not amend RPC 349; void marriage never valid; declaration retroacts; accused may prove first marriage void in the bigamy case; second marriage void on a ground other than bigamy defeats element 4. Abandons Mercado/Tenebro line. Second marriage still civilly void.
  • Tenebro: nullity of the second marriage (Art. 36) not per se a defense — Uribe thinks Pulido overrides it except where the ground is bigamy itself.
  • Prejudicial question: annulment of the second marriage for force on the accused (Merced; Donato). Suspension ≠ acquittal.
  • Art. 41: 4 years / 2 years (danger of death per NCC 391); well-founded belief (Cantor: diligent, not passive; Nolasco); summary proceeding for remarriage only (Tadeo-Matias); judgment immediately final (Art. 247), remedy certiorari (Granada; Bermudez-Lorino); fraud → annulment of judgment, Rule 47 (Santos v. Santos 2014).
  • Civil Code: 7 years, no judicial declaration (Jones; Armas); subsequent marriage voidable (NCC 85(2)). FC: valid until terminated.
  • Art. 42: terminated by recording (not reappearance, not filing) of sworn statement; "any interested person"; notice to spouses of the subsequent marriage; judicial determination if disputed; "unless" prior marriage annulled or declared void. Mere reappearance does nothing (SSS v. Bailon).
  • Art. 43: (1) children conceived before termination legitimate; (2) bad-faith spouse's share in net profits forfeited: common children → his children by prior marriage → innocent spouse; (3) donations to bad-faith donee revoked by operation of law; (4) innocent spouse may revoke insurance designation even if irrevocable; (5) bad-faith spouse disqualified to inherit from innocent spouse, testate and intestate.
  • Art. 44: both in bad faith → void ab initio even with declaration; all donations propter nuptias and testamentary dispositions in favor of each other revoked by operation of law.
  • Uribe: present spouse in bad faith with a declaration → valid ("sisihin mo si Judge") unless both in bad faith; "MAY be void," don't promise the client.
  • Art. 50: Art. 43(2)–(5) and 44 apply to marriages under Arts. 40 and 45 only; Art. 36 and other void marriages → Art. 147/148, decree on finality (Valdes; Diño).
  • Art. 147: capacitated, exclusive; wages equal; presumption of equal shares; forfeiture to common children. Art. 148: not capacitated (bigamous, incestuous, Art. 38); actual contribution only (Yaptinchay).
  • Children of void marriages: illegitimate (Art. 165), except Art. 36 (conceived or born before finality) and Art. 53 (Art. 54).
  • Arts. 52–53: recording of judgment, partition, presumptive legitimes; unrecorded → "shall not affect third persons"; remarriage without it → void, children legitimate. Not applicable when the prior marriage ended by death (then separation of property, Arts. 103/130). Legal separation + remarriage → bigamous.
  • Procedure: Family Court; 6 months' residence; no motion to dismiss except jurisdiction; no default; prosecutor investigates collusion; no stipulation, confession, summary judgment; MR before appeal; death before entry of judgment closes the case; res judicata bars splitting grounds (Mallion).
