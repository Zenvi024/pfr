2. Concept of marriage: definition dissected

Two senses of "marriage" (Tolentino)

  • Marriage as an act (the procedure/celebration): the procedure by which a man and a woman become husband and wife, whether by religious ceremony, civil ceremony or without ceremony. It inaugurates the relation: "that act by which a man and a woman unite for life, with the intent to discharge towards society and one another those duties which result from the relation of husband and wife."
  • Marriage as a status (the relation): the act produces a change of status, and the new status is also called marriage. As a status it involves duties and responsibilities that are no longer matters for private regulation but the concern of the State. In this aspect it is a civil or social institution, the foundation of the family and origin of domestic relations: "the civil status of one man and one woman, legally united for life, with rights and duties which, for the establishment of families and the multiplication of the species, are, or from time to time may thereafter be, assigned by law to matrimony."

Elements of the FC 1 definition

  1. Special contract — a contract only in form; in essence an institution of public order (sui generis).
  2. Of permanent union — no term or condition; cannot be ended by agreement (see R-2 example below). Divorce is not available between Filipinos; the only exceptions are recognized by law (FC 26 par. 2; PD 1083 for Muslims).
  3. Between a man and a woman — heterosexual union (FC 2(1): "who must be a male and a female").
  4. Entered into in accordance with law — compliance with essential (FC 2) and formal (FC 3) requisites; absence makes it void (FC 4).
  5. For the establishment of conjugal and family life — the purpose; but the parties' private motive does not affect validity (Republic v. Albios).
  6. Foundation of the family and an inviolable social institution — hence the State's interest (Const. Art. XV Sec. 2).
  7. Nature, consequences and incidents governed by law, not by stipulation — with one exception: property relations, via marriage settlements, within the limits of the Code (FC 74-81).

Purposes of marriage (Tolentino)

  • In general: (1) reproduction, (2) education of the offspring, (3) mutual help.
  • Immediate purpose: constitution of a complete and perfect community between two individuals of different sexes. Remote purpose: preservation of the human race.
Tolentino: Kinds of marriage in older classifications: (1) firm, legal and consummated (canonical celebration not yet followed by carnal union; civil celebration not yet followed by copulation; marriage followed by sexual intercourse); (2) real, putative and presumed (a putative marriage is one celebrated between parties with an existing impediment, whether in good or bad faith; a presumed marriage, in Anglo-American law, is the common-law marriage); (3) public and secret (matrimonio de conciencia); (4) equal and morganatic; (5) valid, voidable and void, which is the only distinction under our present law.

Constitutional overlay

  • Art. XV Sec. 2 constitutionalizes the "inviolable social institution" language: marriage "shall be protected by the State." This is why courts lean toward validity (NCC 220) and why the State, through the prosecutor/OSG, intervenes in nullity and annulment suits (FC 48).
  • Art. II Sec. 12: the family is a "basic autonomous social institution"; Art. XV Sec. 1: the family is the "foundation of the nation."
  • Art. II Sec. 14: fundamental equality of women and men, the policy basis for the FC's equalizing of spouses' rights; compare the equal-protection reasoning in Republic v. Manalo, which refused to treat the Filipino spouse unequally.

3. Marriage as a "special contract": characteristics, distinctions, effects

Tolentino: Marriage is a contract only in form; in essence it is an institution of public order, founded on custom and morality. It is a contract sui generis, a convention of a social character based on the consent of the parties, uniting a man and a woman in a juridical act for procreation and the other material and moral ends necessary for the development of personality. Because it is the foundation of the family, the State is deeply concerned in its maintenance in purity and integrity.

Characteristics of marriage as an institution (Tolentino)

  1. Civil in character: established by the State independently of its religious aspect (cf. Tilar v. Tilar (2017): although a sacrament in the Church, marriage has civil and legal consequences governed by the Family Code, so a civil court may rule on the validity of a church wedding; separation of church and state is not violated).
  2. An institution of public order or policy: governed by rules established by law which cannot be made inoperative by the stipulation of the parties.
  3. An institution of natural character: one of its objects is the satisfaction of the intimate sentiments and needs of human beings for the organic perpetuation of man.

Consequence (Tolentino): once a marriage has been properly solemnized and the obligations of married life undertaken, its validity cannot be affected by an antenuptial agreement not to live together, nor by an agreement that the marriage should not be valid and binding, nor because one or both parties did not intend it to be a permanent relation (citing Gregg v. Gregg, N.Y.: parties may not vary or diminish by private agreement the obligations the law attaches to the relationship).

Principal effects of a valid marriage (Tolentino)

  1. Personal and economic relations between the spouses (rights and duties);
  2. Legitimacy of the sexual union and of the family;
  3. Personal and economic relations between parents and children;
  4. Family relationship (impediments to marriage, right to support, rights to inheritance);
  5. Emancipation of the spouses from parental authority;
  6. Incapacity of the spouses to make donations to each other (FC 87);
  7. Disqualification of the spouses to testify against each other (marital disqualification rule);
  8. Modification of criminal liability: exemption (e.g., one spouse defending the other from unlawful aggression, or being an accessory), mitigation (immediate vindication of a grave offense to the spouse), aggravation (relationship), or as an element (parricide).
Uribe: Legal separation "is a kind of divorce which is relative" (a mensa et thoro: the spouses live apart but the bond is not severed), as opposed to absolute divorce (a vinculo matrimonii), which severs the bond. A divorce is relative when conditions are attached to remarriage or the bond subsists. Our law has no absolute divorce for Filipinos (except FC 26 par. 2 and Muslim divorce under PD 1083).

A couple executes a prenuptial agreement providing that their marriage shall be valid for only 5 years, renewable by mutual consent negotiated at least 6 months before expiration. Is the agreement valid?

Answer: No. FC 1: marriage is a special contract of permanent union whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation. The only permissible stipulation concerns property relations (marriage settlements within the limits of the Code). A term on the marriage is void; the marriage itself, if all requisites are present, is valid and permanent (quiz bank R-2).

4. "Between a man and a woman": same-sex unions

Obergefell v. Hodges, Director, Ohio Department of Health (U.S. Supreme Court, 576 U.S. 644, June 26, 2015)

Same-sex couples challenged Ohio and other states' refusal to license or recognize their marriages. Doctrine: The fundamental liberties protected by the Fourteenth Amendment's Due Process Clause extend to personal choices central to individual dignity and autonomy, including intimate choices defining personal identity and beliefs. The right to marry is a fundamental right inherent in the liberty of the person; under the Due Process and Equal Protection Clauses, same-sex couples may not be deprived of that right. States must license marriages between two people of the same sex and recognize such marriages lawfully performed out of state.

Falcis v. Civil Registrar General (2019)

Petitioner challenged FC 1 and 2 as unconstitutional for limiting marriage to opposite-sex couples (liberty, equal protection, due process, religious freedom). Doctrine: The Court dismissed the petition on procedural grounds (no actual case or controversy, lack of standing, disregard of the hierarchy of courts) and did not rule on the substantive question. The result is that marriage under the Family Code (Arts. 1 and 2) remains a union between a man and a woman; any change in that definition is a matter of policy for Congress, not the courts.

Silverio v. Republic (2007)

Doctrine: Sex reassignment is not among the acts or events recognized by law for correction of the civil register. Recognizing it would undermine the Family Code provisions on marriage, which restrict marriage to a union between a biological man and a woman.

Tolentino: Difference in sex is an indispensable requisite dictated by biological law. Even under the Civil Code, which did not state it, it was understood as a natural element, because procreation cannot be realized by two persons of the same sex; the Family Code "merely states the obvious." If through deceit a marriage is celebrated between persons of the same sex (e.g., a hermaphrodite succeeds in marrying a man), the marriage is void and inexistent, producing no legal effect, without prejudice to liability for damages of the party who acted with fraud or deceit.
Note: Obergefell is a U.S. decision; it is not binding here and was cited in Uribe's syllabus as comparative material. Application to Philippine problems: (1) Two Filipinos of the same sex who marry abroad where it is legal: the marriage is void here; their legal capacity (which includes the FC 2(1) requirement of "a male and a female") is governed by Philippine law even abroad (NCC 15), and FC 1, 2 and 4 make the union void. Answer "void." (2) Two foreigners of the same sex married abroad: there is no Philippine ruling. Their capacity is governed by their national law (NCC 15 applied to aliens; FC 21), and FC 26 par. 1's list of exceptions does not mention lack of sex difference; Tolentino argues the FC 26 exceptions should be limited to Filipinos. Present both views if asked: (a) recognized, because capacity follows national law and the marriage is valid where celebrated; (b) not recognized, because a same-sex union is contrary to a fundamental public policy of the forum (NCC 17 par. 3). (3) A Filipino and a foreigner of the same sex: void as to the Filipino's capacity (NCC 15).

5. "Not subject to stipulation": NCC 221 and the cases

Art. 221, NCC. The following shall be void and of no effect:
(1) Any contract for personal separation between husband and wife;
(2) Every extra-judicial agreement, during marriage, for the dissolution of the conjugal partnership of gains or of the absolute community of property between husband and wife;
(3) Every collusion to obtain a decree of legal separation, or of annulment of marriage;
(4) Any simulated alienation of property with intent to deprive the compulsory heirs of their legitime.
Note: FC 254 expressly repeals Titles III, IV, V, VI, VIII, IX, XI and XV of Book I of the Civil Code. Title VII (The Family, where Arts. 220 and 221 are found) is not in that list, which is why Uribe's syllabus still cites NCC 220 and 221 by number; they survive to the extent not inconsistent with FC 149-151. (Tolentino's introductory list of repealed titles includes Title VII; the codal text of FC 254 does not. Cite the articles as Uribe does.)

Panganiban v. Borromeo (58 Phil. 367, September 9, 1933)

A lawyer prepared and notarized a contract between spouses which in effect permitted the husband to keep a concubine and the wife to commit adultery. Doctrine: Although the Revised Penal Code lets the offended spouse bar prosecution by consent or pardon, the legislature did not thereby legalize adultery and concubinage. A notarized contract permitting concubinage and adultery is not judicially recognizable: the acts remain contrary to good customs and morals and against the sanctity of marriage. The lawyer who drew it is administratively liable.

In re Santiago (70 Phil. 66, June 21, 1940)

A lawyer advised spouses Baniquit and Colares, who had been separated for years, to sign a document he prepared and notarized declaring that each was authorized to marry again and waiving any right of action against the one who remarried. Doctrine: The contract is contrary to law and morals and tends to subvert the vital foundation of the family. Marriage can be dissolved only by death or the causes provided by law; spouses cannot by contract authorize each other to remarry. Advising it, preparing it and notarizing it constitute malpractice justifying discipline of the lawyer.

Selanova v. Mendoza (64 SCRA 69, May 19, 1975)

A judge notarized/ratified a document by which spouses extrajudicially liquidated their conjugal partnership and allowed each other to commit acts of infidelity. Doctrine: Such a document is a void instrument because it contravenes NCC 221 declaring void contracts for the personal separation of husband and wife and for the extrajudicial dissolution of the conjugal partnership. A judge who lends his office to it is administratively liable.

Lichauco-De Leon v. Court of Appeals (186 SCRA 345, June 6, 1990)

The husband's family executed a letter-agreement to pay the estranged wife a sum and transfer property in exchange for her conformity to the separation of property and liquidation of the conjugal partnership. Doctrine: Marriage is not a mere contract but an inviolable social institution whose nature, consequences and incidents are governed by law and not subject to stipulation (NCC 52). An agreement whose object is to facilitate the separation of the spouses, the extrajudicial dissolution of the conjugal partnership, or the termination of the marriage is void for being contrary to law, morals and public policy (NCC 221, 1306).

Quiz trap: "A contract for personal separation between husband and wife is voidable" (FALSE: void and of no effect). "Every extrajudicial agreement, before marriage, for the dissolution of the CPG is void" (FALSE: NCC 221(2) covers agreements during marriage; before marriage, the parties are free to choose any regime in a marriage settlement). "Collusion to obtain a decree of declaration of nullity" is not in NCC 221(3), which says legal separation or annulment; collusion in nullity cases is policed by FC 48 and A.M. 02-11-10-SC.

H and W, separated in fact, sign a notarized "Agreement" that (1) they will live separately, (2) their ACP is dissolved and each keeps what he or she earns, and (3) each may live with another partner without objection. W later lives with X. H files a concubinage/adultery complaint; W invokes the agreement as consent. Effect?

Answer: The agreement is void and of no effect (NCC 221(1) and (2); FC 1; Selanova; Panganiban). It does not dissolve the ACP (only a judicial separation of property can, FC 134-136) and does not license infidelity. However, for criminal purposes, a prior consent may still bar the prosecution under the RPC (the offended spouse cannot institute the case if he consented or pardoned). The civil rule (void agreement) and the penal rule (consent bars prosecution) are distinct; Panganiban stresses that the RPC rule does not legalize adultery. The notarizing lawyer is administratively liable (In re Santiago).

6. Motive is immaterial; simulated and jest marriages

Republic v. Albios (G.R. No. 198780, October 16, 2013)

Liberty Albios (Filipina) married Daniel Lee Fringer (American) before a judge, under an arrangement that she would pay him US$2,000 so she could acquire American citizenship; they never lived together. She sued for nullity on the ground that the marriage was a sham for lack of consent. The RTC and CA declared it void. Doctrine: The SC reversed: the marriage is valid. Consent under FC 2(2) must be (a) freely given and (b) conscious or intelligent, the parties understanding the nature and consequences of their act. Both parties freely and willingly married each other precisely to obtain the benefit (citizenship), which requires a valid marriage; their consent was therefore real, not simulated. Unlike a marriage in jest (no genuine intent to be bound), a "marriage for convenience" or "limited purpose marriage" is valid: the motive for marrying is not among the grounds for nullity, and no law declares a marriage void because it was entered into for purposes other than those in FC 1 (e.g., to acquire foreign citizenship). Motives for marrying are varied and complex; the State does not and cannot dictate the kind of life a couple chooses to lead. So long as all essential and formal requisites are present and no ground for nullity exists, the marriage is valid. Marriage, an inviolable social institution protected by the Constitution, must be safeguarded from the whims and caprices of the contracting parties; courts will not allow it to be used as a tool to abuse the law or aid one who comes with unclean hands.

Ado-an-Morimoto v. Morimoto (G.R. No. 247576, March 15, 2021)

Rosario Ado-an agreed to a "fixed" marriage with Yoshio Morimoto (Japanese) to facilitate her Japanese visa. She signed a blank marriage certificate on the assurance that it would not be registered; no ceremony took place, but the certificate was later registered. She presented certifications that there was no record of the marriage license number stated in the certificate. The lower courts denied her petition. Doctrine: The SC declared the marriage void ab initio. Where no marriage ceremony actually took place and no license existed, the essential and formal requisites are utterly lacking; a simulated marriage used as a front for obtaining benefits is totally inexistent, the parties having no genuine intent to enter into marital relations. Albios does not apply: in Albios the parties actually appeared before a solemnizing officer, went through the ceremony and gave real consent; here there was only a signed and registered certificate. A certification from the local civil registrar that there is no record of the license is adequate proof of its absence. The marriage certificate is only evidence of a marriage; it cannot create one.

F, a Filipina, marries M, a Canadian, before the mayor, with a license, solely so she can immigrate; both agreed to divorce after she gets her residency. A year later she sues for nullity for "lack of consent," alleging the marriage was a sham. Will it prosper?

Answer: No. Under Albios, a marriage for convenience is valid: the parties freely and intelligently consented to marry, which was precisely what was needed to secure the immigration benefit. Their agreement to divorce later is void (FC 1; NCC 221) and does not affect the marriage. Contrast: if no ceremony actually took place and they merely signed a certificate (Morimoto), the "marriage" would be void.

7. Contract to marry and breach of promise to marry (VII.A.3)

Art. 19, NCC. Every person must, in the exercise of his rights and in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith.
Art. 20, NCC. Every person who, contrary to law, wilfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same.
Art. 21, NCC. Any person who wilfully causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy shall compensate the latter for the damage.
Art. 2176, NCC. Whoever by act or omission causes damage to another, there being fault or negligence, is obliged to pay for the damage done. Such fault or negligence, if there is no pre-existing contractual relation between the parties, is called a quasi-delict and is governed by the provisions of this Chapter.
Art. 1403(2)(c), NCC. The following contracts are unenforceable, unless they are ratified: ... (2) Those that do not comply with the Statute of Frauds as set forth in this number. In the following cases an agreement hereafter made shall be unenforceable by action, unless the same, or some note or memorandum, thereof, be in writing, and subscribed by the party charged, or by his agent; evidence, therefore, of the agreement cannot be received without the writing, or a secondary evidence of its contents: ... (c) An agreement made in consideration of marriage, other than a mutual promise to marry;

Rule, exceptions

  • Rule: A mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong. The Code Commission's draft chapter on "Promise of Marriage" (ten articles granting damages) was deliberately eliminated by Congress (Hermosisima, citing De Jesus v. Syquia: the action "has no standing in the civil law, apart from the right to recover money or property advanced by the plaintiff upon the faith of such promise"). The Family Code did not change this. Marriage cannot be compelled by specific performance; consent to the actual marriage must be purely voluntary.
  • Exceptions (damages recoverable, not for the breach itself but for an independent wrong):
    1. Walking out of a formally set, publicized and prepared wedding: contrary to good customs, Art. 21 (Wassmer v. Velez).
    2. Promise used as a deceptive device to obtain the woman's consent to sexual relations (moral seduction): Art. 21 for the fraud and deceit (Baksh v. CA). Mere sexual intercourse between consenting adults is not seduction (Tanjanco).
    3. Recovery of money or property advanced on the faith of the promise; return of gifts made in consideration of the marriage (unjust enrichment, NCC 22; Domalagan v. Bolifer).
    4. Quantifiable injury caused by the defendant's inducement, e.g., urging the woman to resign her job (Garcia v. Del Rosario, per Tolentino and B29).
  • Good faith required of the claimant: no recovery by one who acted in bad faith or is in pari delicto (Guevarra v. Banach (2022): the man who concealed a subsisting marriage could not recover the money he gave; Baksh held the woman's fault was not equal to the deceiver's). Voluntary intimacy between unmarried adults with no impediment and no deceit is not seduction (Abanag v. Mabute (2011), cf. Tanjanco).
Wassmer v. Velez (12 SCRA 648, December 26, 1964)

Two days before the wedding, after the license had been obtained, invitations sent, gown, reception and bridal showers arranged, the groom left a note postponing because his mother opposed; the next day he wired that "nothing changed," then never appeared. Doctrine: Mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong. But to formally set a wedding and go through all the preparation and publicity, only to walk out of it when the matrimony is about to be solemnized, is quite different. It is palpably and unjustifiably contrary to good customs, for which the defendant must be held answerable in damages under Art. 21 (actual, moral and exemplary).

Hermosisima v. Court of Appeals (109 Phil. 629, 1960)

A woman, about ten years older than the man, had intimate relations with him on his promise of marriage, became pregnant, and he married another. Doctrine: The action for breach of promise to marry has no standing in the civil law apart from the right to recover money or property advanced on the faith of the promise; moral damages for the breach itself cannot be recovered. Seduction was not present, given the woman's age relative to the man's; support for the child and actual damages may be awarded.

Tanjanco v. Court of Appeals (18 SCRA 994, December 17, 1966)

An adult woman maintained sexual relations with the man for about a year on his promise of marriage, became pregnant and resigned her job; he did not marry her. Doctrine: Seduction under Art. 21 connotes deceit, enticement, superior power or abuse of confidence that induces the woman to yield against her will. In an action by the woman, the enticement, persuasion or deception is the essence of the injury; mere proof of intercourse is insufficient. Repeated intercourse for a year between adults shows mutual passion, not seduction; no cause of action.

Baksh v. Court of Appeals (G.R. No. 97336, February 19, 1993; the syllabus prints "97326")

A foreign medical student courted a Filipina, promised to marry her, got her parents' consent to their living together preparatory to the wedding, deflowered her, then refused to marry her. Doctrine: Where the man's promise to marry is the proximate cause of the woman's acceptance of his love and of her giving herself to him in sexual congress, proof that he never intended to marry her and that the promise was only a subtle scheme or deceptive device to obtain her consent justifies damages under Art. 21, not because of the promise but because of the fraud and deceit behind it and the willful injury to her honor and reputation. Art. 21 applies where the woman is a victim of moral seduction. In pari delicto does not bar her because her fault was not equal to his.

Cabague v. Auxilio (92 Phil. 294, November 26, 1952)

The man and his father sued the woman and her father: the father had improved the bride's house and spent for the wedding in consideration of the promised marriage, which she refused. Doctrine: Two kinds of agreement were involved: (1) the mutual promise to marry between the lovers, which the Statute of Frauds expressly excepts, so oral evidence of it is admissible and the groom may sue for its breach (for the expenses advanced); and (2) the agreement between the fathers made in consideration of the marriage, which, being "other than a mutual promise to marry," cannot be proved by parol evidence (NCC 1403(2)(c)). The groom's father's action fails.

Domalagan v. Bolifer (33 Phil. 471, February 8, 1916)

Plaintiff paid the defendant a sum of money in consideration of the defendant's promise that his daughter would marry plaintiff's son; she married another. Doctrine: Verbal agreements made in consideration of marriage other than a mutual promise to marry are not invalid for not being in writing; the Statute of Frauds only makes them unprovable in court without a writing, and failure to object to oral evidence is a waiver. The defendant must return the money (Tolentino treats the basis as unjust enrichment).

Estremos v. Ephan (CA, 83 O.G. 4022, No. 35)

Doctrine: A mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong; Congress deliberately eliminated from the draft of the new Civil Code the provisions that would have made it so. The exception is the Wassmer situation, where the defendant's conduct is palpably contrary to good customs under Art. 21.

Tolentino: A contract to marry (engagement) is a purely executory contract which cannot be enforced by court action because consent to the actual marriage must be voluntary. No ceremony is needed; a promise of marriage needs only to be made so that it may reasonably be believed by the injured party. A request to postpone for a reasonable cause is not repudiation (e.g., a party who develops a disease making it unsafe to marry). Tolentino believes that an action purely for breach of the contract to marry will not lie; but damages may be based on tort or quasi-delict, or on Arts. 19, 21 or 22: (a) seduction as a fact separate from the contract (he criticizes as too broad the pre-Code rule barring the woman's recovery because she participated in the carnal act); (b) abuse of right under Art. 19, as when a party capriciously breaks the engagement without reason, especially just before the wedding after a long engagement; (c) unjust enrichment under Art. 22 (Domalagan). Gifts made in contemplation of marriage are conditional and recoverable upon breach (e.g., the engagement ring), but absolute gifts (Christmas presents) are not. On the Statute of Frauds, he submits writing should not be required where the plaintiff already gave the consideration, and the Statute should apply only to actions to enforce the contract, not to actions for damages for breach.
Quiz trap: (1) "A promise to marry has obligatory force; its breach is per se actionable" — FALSE. (2) "An agreement made in consideration of marriage, including a mutual promise to marry, is unenforceable unless in writing" — FALSE: the mutual promise to marry is the exception. (3) "Any person who wilfully or negligently causes loss or injury ... contrary to morals, good customs or public policy" — FALSE for Art. 21 (only "wilfully"; "wilfully or negligently" is Art. 20, which requires an act "contrary to law"). (4) In Baksh, damages are awarded "because of the fraud and deceit," not "because of the breach of the promise."

Perido v. Perido (63 SCRA 97, 1975)

In a partition among descendants of a man's first and second marriages, the first set argued the second set were illegitimate; titles issued to the decedent in 1923 described him as "single/widower." Doctrine: A statement of civil status in certificates of title is not conclusive and is weak and insufficient to rebut the presumption that persons living together as husband and wife are married to each other. The presumption, especially where the legitimacy of the issue is involved, may be overcome only by cogent proof on the part of those who allege illegitimacy. In case of doubt, the law favors the validity of marriage.

Fiel v. Banawa (CA, 76 O.G. 4619)

Doctrine: Philippine law does not recognize common-law marriages. A man and woman not legally married who cohabit for years as husband and wife, represent themselves to the public as such, and are reputed to be spouses in their community may be considered "married" in common-law jurisdictions, but not here. Property of such relationships was governed by NCC 144 (co-ownership), which requires that (1) the couple not be incapacitated to marry, and (2) the property be acquired through the work or industry of either or both. (Now FC 147 and 148.)

Note: Reconcile Perido and Fiel. The presumption of marriage (Rule 131 Sec. 3(aa)) is a rule of evidence that a ceremonial marriage took place; it does not make cohabitation itself a marriage. A common-law marriage (marriage by mere cohabitation and reputation, without ceremony) is not recognized (Fiel). So: cohabitation + reputation raises a disputable presumption that the couple were lawfully married; proof that no marriage was ever celebrated, or that one party had a subsisting prior marriage, rebuts it.

Proof of marriage: the marriage certificate is the best, not the only, evidence

Tolentino: The best proof of marriage is the marriage certificate, but it is not the only proof. Competent proof also includes: the declaration of one of the parties; testimony of persons present at the celebration; public and open cohabitation as husband and wife after the alleged marriage; birth and baptismal certificates of children naming the alleged spouses; statements of the marriage in subsequent documents. A marriage will be presumed valid and regular and will not be set aside because the priest and one of the parties cannot remember what was said during the ceremony, especially if followed by long cohabitation. A statement in a duly probated will that a woman is the testator's wife is admissible as an admission against pecuniary interest. There is a prima facie presumption that a man and woman living maritally under the same roof are married, because such is the common order of society; if they were not, they would be living in constant violation of decency and law. The presumption may be rebutted by evidence negating the fact of marriage or showing that one party is legally married to another; but after long cohabitation, during which the parties were generally known as husband and wife and their children bore the man's surname, the fact that the man abandoned the common home is not enough to overthrow it. It is rebuttable only by cogent proof. The failure of the priest to send a copy of the certificate to the civil registrar does not invalidate the marriage, since forwarding the certificate is not an essential requisite.
Pugeda v. Trias (4 SCRA 849, March 31, 1962)

Doctrine: The parish priest's failure to send a copy of the marriage certificate to the municipal secretary (in a marriage in articulo mortis) does not invalidate the marriage, where it does not appear that any requisite for validity was absent; forwarding the certificate is not a requisite of validity. The marriage may be proved by other evidence.

Other cases on proof/presumption (from the B29 repository) (supplementary)

Mariategui v. CA: the mere absence of a record of the marriage (certificate) does not invalidate it if all requisites were present; cohabitation and reputation raise the presumption. Macua Vda. de Avenido v. Avenido (2014): the fact of marriage may be proved by certifications on the loss/destruction of records and by testimony of witnesses to the ceremony; the presumption of a lawful marriage, so supported, is sufficient. Vda. de Jacob v. CA (1999) (cited in the quiz bank): the marriage certificate is the best but not the only evidence. Calimag v. The formal requisites under FC 3 are authority of the solemnizing officer, a valid marriage license (except FC 27-34), and a marriage ceremony with personal appearance and declaration before at least two witnesses of legal age. The certificate (FC 6, 22) is evidence, and failure to sign or register it is at most an irregularity. "Failure of the solemnizing officer to send copies within fifteen days invalidates the marriage" — FALSE (quiz bank R-68; FC 23; Pugeda).

A and B lived together openly for thirty years, were known as husband and wife, and their children's birth certificates name them as married. No marriage certificate can be found. After B died, B's collateral relatives claim A is not the surviving spouse. Rule.

Answer: A and B are disputably presumed to have been lawfully married (Rule 131 Sec. 3(aa); NCC 220; Perido). The certificate is the best but not the only proof; cohabitation, reputation, and the children's records are competent evidence (Tolentino). The relatives bear the burden of overcoming the presumption with cogent proof (e.g., that no marriage was ever celebrated, or that one had a subsisting prior marriage). NOT excepted (so lex loci governs): what is left out of the list

  • 35(2) lack of authority of the solemnizing officer: authority is a formal requisite, governed by the law of the place (e.g., a marriage solemnized by a ship captain, a notary, a mayor, or even the Prime Minister abroad, if authorized there, is valid here).
  • 35(3) absence of a marriage license: also formal, governed by lex loci (e.g., a country that does not require a license).
  • Personal appearance / proxy marriage: form of the ceremony (FC 3(3), 6), governed by lex loci. Uribe: valid if valid where celebrated, even between Filipinos.
  • Voidable marriages (FC 45): not in the list; a marriage abroad of a Filipino aged 18-21 without parental consent is merely voidable (governed by Philippine law on capacity, FC 45(1)), not void.
  • Art. 41 subsequent marriages: a bigamous marriage falling under Art. 41 (well-founded belief of death and judicial declaration of presumptive death) is excluded from 35(4), so it may be valid.
  • Relationships not prohibited by the FC: e.g., stepbrother and stepsister, brother-in-law and sister-in-law, guardian and ward (the FC dropped some NCC 82 impediments).
Tolentino: As a rule the validity of a marriage is determined by the law of the place where it was entered into; a marriage valid where celebrated is valid everywhere. Thus a common-law marriage valid where contracted, or a proxy marriage contracted in a foreign country where good, is good in states where it is not allowed to be contracted. In private international law there are two general exceptions: (1) marriages deemed contrary to the law of nature and good morals as generally recognized in Christian countries (polygamous marriages; incestuous marriages in the direct line and between brothers and sisters), and (2) marriages which the local law-making power has declared shall not be allowed any validity (statutory prohibitions affecting morals or good order of society). FC 26 has expanded the exceptions by covering marriages void under Arts. 35, 36, 37 and 38. Tolentino believes the unqualified application of the exceptions should be limited to Filipinos. Where both spouses are foreigners and the validity of their marriage is questioned in Philippine courts, our courts must apply our law, but the judgment will have force only in Philippine territory; elsewhere, conflict rules govern. When Filipinos are involved, FC 26 must be read with NCC 15 and 17: their legal capacity to marry must still be tested by the criteria of our law; other requisites not affecting capacity may be governed by foreign law. Marriages between parties under the age of consent entered into in a foreign state have been held void in the state of their domicile, and the validity of a marriage contracted in a foreign state to evade the laws of the domicile may be questioned by the courts of the domicile.
Tolentino (proof of foreign marriage): To uphold a foreign marriage, two facts must be proved: (1) the provisions of the foreign law, and (2) the celebration of the marriage in accordance with those provisions.
Yao Kee v. Sy-Gonzales (1988)

Doctrine: One who asserts a marriage celebrated abroad (there, a marriage in China under Chinese custom) must prove not only the fact of the marriage but also the foreign law or custom under which it was celebrated and its validity under that law. Philippine courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws; absent proof, the foreign law is presumed to be the same as ours (processual presumption), and the marriage is tested by Philippine law.

Board of Commissioners v. De la Rosa (1991); Ambrose v. Suque-Ambrose (2021) (B29 repository)

Doctrine: Valid foreign marriages are recognized in the Philippines under lex loci celebrationis; but the party relying on a foreign marriage must prove the fact of marriage and its validity under the foreign law. Conversely, a marriage celebrated in the Philippines is governed by Philippine law (Ambrose).

11. Marriages of foreigners in the Philippines; certificate of legal capacity (FC 21)

Art. 21, FC. When either or both of the contracting parties are citizens of a foreign country, it shall be necessary for them before a marriage license can be obtained, to submit a certificate of legal capacity to contract marriage, issued by their respective diplomatic or consular officials.

Rules

  • A marriage celebrated in the Philippines, even between foreigners, follows Philippine law as to form (lex loci; FC 3, 6-8; license required unless exempt).
  • The foreigner's legal capacity is governed by his national law (nationality principle; NCC 15 applied to aliens; Garcia v. Recio). The certificate of legal capacity is the means of proving it before the license issues.
  • Stateless persons/refugees: affidavit in lieu of certificate; their capacity is determined by Philippine law (Tolentino: having no personal law, they are governed by the laws of the Philippines under which they enjoy temporary protection).
  • Absence of the certificate does not affect validity. It is a requirement for the issuance of the license, not an essential (FC 2) or formal (FC 3) requisite; at most there is an irregularity (FC 4 par. 3). Whether the alien in fact had capacity is a separate question governed by his national law.
  • A duly authenticated certificate is prima facie evidence of the alien's capacity (Garcia v. Recio).

12. Absolute divorce: the rule for Filipinos and the pre-1987 cases

The baseline

  • There is no absolute divorce in the Philippines for non-Muslims. A marriage contracted in the Philippines cannot be dissolved except by death (Tolentino).
  • A divorce obtained abroad by Filipinos is void and not recognized here: NCC 15 (laws on family rights and duties, status and legal capacity bind Filipinos abroad) and NCC 17 par. 3 (prohibitive laws and public policy cannot be rendered ineffective by foreign judgments). If a Filipino divorcee then contracts a subsequent marriage, that marriage is void (bigamous) because the first is still subsisting (Tolentino).
  • Muslim divorce is governed by PD 1083 Arts. 45-55 (talaq, ila, zihar, li'an, khul', tafwid, faskh); PD 1083 applies where both parties are Muslims, or only the husband is Muslim and the marriage was solemnized under Muslim law (Art. 13, PD 1083). Conversion to Islam does not allow a party to a subsisting civil marriage to contract a second marriage; he remains liable for bigamy (Malaki v. People). A marriage solemnized under civil rites is governed by the Civil Code/FC, not PD 1083, so the RTC has jurisdiction over its nullity (Tamano v. Ortiz).
  • Divorce under special laws in force in the past (e.g., Act 2710; the Japanese occupation) is outside the scope of this section.
Barretto-Gonzales v. Gonzales (58 Phil. 67, March 7, 1933)

Filipino spouses obtained a Reno, Nevada divorce and asked Philippine courts to ratify it. Doctrine: Litigants by mutual agreement cannot compel the courts to approve their own actions or permit the personal relations of citizens of these Islands to be affected by decrees of foreign courts in a manner our Government believes contrary to public order and good morals. The conduct of the parties showed a purpose to circumvent our laws on divorce and secure a change of status for reasons and under conditions not authorized by our law.

Tenchavez v. Escaño (15 SCRA 355, November 29, 1965)

Vicenta Escaño secretly married Pastor Tenchavez before a Catholic chaplain in Cebu in 1948; she went to the U.S. and obtained a Nevada divorce on the ground of "extreme cruelty, entirely mental in character" while still a Filipino citizen; she later married an American and was naturalized. Doctrine: A foreign divorce between Filipino citizens, sought and decreed after the effectivity of the Civil Code, is not entitled to recognition as valid in this jurisdiction. Since Vicenta was still a Filipino when she obtained it, the decree gave her no right to remarry: at that time she was bound by Philippine law on family rights and duties and on status and legal capacity (NCC 15). To recognize it would be a patent violation of the declared public policy of the State (NCC 17 par. 3). Her marriage to Tenchavez subsisted, and her refusal to perform her wifely duties and her denial of consortium gave Tenchavez a right to damages.

Van Dorn v. Romillo, Jr. (139 SCRA 139, October 8, 1985)

Alice Reyes (Filipina) married Richard Upton (American) in Hong Kong in 1972; they were divorced in Nevada in 1982, both parties having appeared (Upton through counsel, stating that there was no community property). Alice remarried; Upton then sued her in Pasay for an accounting of her business as conjugal property. Doctrine: Owing to the nationality principle in NCC 15, only Philippine nationals are covered by the policy against absolute divorces. Aliens may obtain divorces abroad, which may be recognized in the Philippines provided they are valid according to their national law. Under his national law, Upton is no longer the husband; he has no standing to sue as husband or to exercise control over conjugal assets, and he is estopped by his representation before the Nevada court that there was no community property. The Filipina should not be obliged to live together with, observe respect and fidelity, and render support to a man who is no longer her husband under his own law; she should not be discriminated against in her own country if the ends of justice are to be served.

13. FC 26 par. 2: the mixed-marriage exception

History

  • As originally promulgated by E.O. 209 (July 6, 1987), FC 26 had only one paragraph, excepting marriages prohibited under Arts. 35, 37 and 38.
  • E.O. 227 (July 17, 1987), issued before the FC took effect on August 3, 1988, amended FC 26 by (a) narrowing the exceptions to Arts. 35(1), (4), (5), (6) and adding Art. 36 (also inserted by E.O. 227), and (b) adding the second paragraph. Tolentino: the second paragraph "creates an exception to the foregoing rules" for mixed marriages, following Van Dorn.
  • Purpose: to avoid the absurd situation where the Filipino spouse remains married to the alien spouse who, after obtaining a divorce, is no longer married to the Filipino spouse (Orbecido; Manalo).
  • FC 13 confirms the Code contemplates foreign divorce: a previously married applicant for a license must furnish "the death certificate of the deceased spouse or the judicial decree of the absolute divorce, or the judicial decree of annulment or declaration of nullity of his or her previous marriage."

Elements (Orbecido's "twin elements," as later read in Manalo)

  1. There is a valid marriage that has been celebrated between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner; and
  2. A valid divorce is obtained abroad (originally "by the alien spouse"; after Manalo, by either spouse) capacitating the alien spouse to remarry.

Citizenship is reckoned at the time the valid divorce is obtained, not at the time of the celebration of the marriage (Orbecido). Result: "the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law." Before the Filipino can remarry, the divorce must be judicially recognized, and the decree and the foreign law must be proved (Garcia v. Recio; Corpuz).

Republic v. Orbecido III (472 SCRA 114, October 5, 2005)

Cipriano Orbecido and Lady Myros Villanueva, both Filipinos, married in the Philippines in 1981; she later became a naturalized American and obtained a divorce in the U.S., then remarried. Cipriano petitioned for authority to remarry under FC 26 par. 2. Doctrine: FC 26 par. 2 should be interpreted to include cases involving parties who, at the time of the celebration of the marriage, were Filipino citizens, but later on one of them becomes naturalized as a foreign citizen and obtains a divorce decree. The reckoning point is not the citizenship of the parties at the time of the celebration of the marriage, but their citizenship at the time a valid divorce is obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating the latter to remarry. A contrary reading would sanction absurdity and injustice: the naturalized alien would be free while the Filipino stays bound. However, the party pleading the divorce must prove the divorce and the alien's naturalization and national law as facts; mere allegations are not enough (the SC set aside the RTC's grant for lack of such proof).

Republic v. Manalo (En Banc, G.R. No. 221029, April 24, 2018)

Marelyn Manalo, a Filipina, filed a divorce case in Japan against her Japanese husband and obtained a divorce decree; she then petitioned for cancellation of the entry of marriage in the civil registry. The OSG argued that FC 26 par. 2 applies only when the alien spouse obtains the divorce. Doctrine: FC 26 par. 2 applies even if it was the Filipino spouse who initiated and obtained the divorce abroad. The provision only requires that there be a divorce "validly obtained abroad"; the word "obtained" does not demand that the alien spouse be the petitioner. The letter of the law should not be followed where it leads to absurdity and injustice; the purpose is to avoid the absurd situation where the Filipino remains married to an alien who is no longer married. Distinguishing between Filipino-initiated and alien-initiated divorces, where no substantial difference exists, would violate equal protection and unjustly discriminate against Filipinos. The divorce must still be valid under the national law of the alien spouse and capacitate the alien to remarry, and the foreign law and divorce must be proved (the case was remanded to receive evidence on Japanese law).

Racho v. Tanaka (G.R. No. 199515, June 25, 2018)

A Filipina and a Japanese national, married in the Philippines, were divorced by agreement in Japan; she presented the Japanese certificate of acceptance of the report of divorce and sought a declaration of capacity to remarry. Doctrine: The Filipino spouse may be granted capacity to remarry once our courts find that the foreign divorce was validly obtained by the foreign spouse according to his or her national law, and that the foreign spouse's national law considers the dissolution of the marital relationship to be absolute. A divorce by agreement (mutual consent) valid under Japanese law may be recognized; a duly authenticated official certificate attesting to the divorce is competent proof of it.

Dela Cruz Morisono v. Morisono (G.R. No. 226013, July 2, 2018)

A Filipina and her Japanese husband obtained a divorce by agreement in Japan; the RTC refused recognition because the Filipina participated in obtaining it. Doctrine: Pursuant to Manalo, foreign divorce decrees obtained to dissolve marriages between a Filipino and an alien may be recognized in this jurisdiction regardless of who between the spouses initiated the divorce, provided that the party petitioning for recognition (presumably the Filipino) proves the divorce as a fact and demonstrates its conformity to the foreign law allowing it.

Juego-Sakai v. Republic (G.R. No. 224015, July 23, 2018)

A Filipina and her Japanese husband divorced by agreement in Japan; she sought recognition. Doctrine: Manalo applies to a divorce jointly obtained. But the starting point in any recognition of a foreign divorce judgment is that our courts do not take judicial notice of foreign judgments and laws: the foreign judgment and its authenticity must be proved as facts under our rules on evidence, together with the alien's applicable national law to show the effect of the judgment on the alien himself or herself. (Where the national law is not adequately proved, the case is remanded for reception of evidence.)

Later decisions in the sources (B29 repository; doctrines as stated there)

  • Galapon v. Republic (2020): FC 26 par. 2 applies to mixed marriages where the divorce decree is (i) obtained by the foreign spouse, (ii) obtained jointly by the Filipino and foreign spouse, or (iii) obtained solely by the Filipino spouse.
  • Abel v. Rule (2021): FC 26 par. 2 does not require that the foreign spouse solely obtain the divorce; a divorce jointly obtained is recognized.
  • Basa-Egami v. Bersales; Octaviano v. Ruthe: FC 26 par. 2 only requires a divorce validly obtained abroad, without regard to who initiated it.
  • Republic v. Ng: a divorce by mutual agreement recorded in the Japanese family registry may be recognized if valid under the foreign spouse's national law, once the fact of divorce and the foreign law are duly proven (remanded for proper proof of Japanese law).
  • Takahashi v. Republic; Rivera v. Woo Namsun: recognition requires proof of both the fact of divorce and the applicable foreign law.
  • Republic v. Kikuchi: the foreign law must be proved from an official source; a translated photocopy of the Japanese Civil Code is not competent proof (remanded).
  • Anido v. Republic: the petitioner proves the law of the place where the divorce was obtained (there, Kentucky), not necessarily the law of the foreign spouse's nationality.
  • Kondo v. Civil Registrar; Tsutsumi v. Republic: courts should be liberal on procedural matters in recognition cases affecting Filipinos in mixed marriages; procedural rules secure, not override, substantial justice.
  • Johansen v. Office of the Civil Registrar: recognition may be joined with Rule 108 correction/annotation in one proceeding, but Rule 108's venue (where the civil registry is located) is jurisdictional.
  • Tesoro v. Landolt: a foreign public record is proved by an official publication or a copy attested by the officer with legal custody; if kept abroad, accompanied by a certificate of the proper Philippine diplomatic or consular officer and authenticated by his seal.
Quiz trap: The exact words of FC 26 par. 2: "Where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law." Some reviewers misquote it as "shall likewise have capacity"; the codal does not say "likewise." Other alterations: "obtained in the Philippines"; "capacitating the Filipino spouse to remarry" (the divorce must capacitate the alien); "the Filipino spouse shall automatically have capacity" (judicial recognition and proof are required); "the marriage is void" (it is dissolved, not void ab initio; it was valid until the divorce).

14. Proving the foreign divorce and foreign law; judicial recognition

Garcia-Recio v. Recio (366 SCRA 437, G.R. No. 138322, October 2, 2001)

Rederick Recio, a Filipino, married an Australian in 1987; they were divorced in Australia in 1989; he became an Australian citizen in 1992 and in 1994 married Grace Garcia, a Filipina, in Cabanatuan, declaring himself "single" and Filipino. Grace sued for nullity on the ground of bigamy; Recio pleaded the Australian divorce (and, later, that the case was moot because he and Grace had themselves been divorced in Australia in 1998). Doctrine: (1) Philippine law does not provide for absolute divorce; a marriage between two Filipinos cannot be dissolved even by a divorce obtained abroad (NCC 15, 17). In mixed marriages, FC 26 allows the Filipino to remarry if the divorce is validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry. A divorce obtained abroad by a couple who are both aliens may be recognized provided it is consistent with their respective national laws. (2) A divorce obtained abroad by an alien may be recognized provided the decree is valid according to the national law of the foreigner. However, the divorce decree and the governing personal law of the alien spouse must be alleged and proved; our courts do not take judicial notice of foreign laws and judgments. (3) The decree, a public document, must be proved under Rule 132 Secs. 24-25: an official publication, or a copy attested by the officer with legal custody, and, if the record is kept abroad, accompanied by a certificate of the Philippine diplomatic or consular officer stationed there, authenticated by his seal. (4) The burden of proof lies on the party who alleges the existence of the fact (here, the respondent who pleaded the divorce as a defense). (5) Divorces may be absolute or limited; the Australian decree might have been a "decree nisi" or have restrictions on remarriage, so Recio's capacity to marry Grace was not established. A duly authenticated certificate of legal capacity would have been prima facie evidence of capacity. Case remanded to receive evidence on Recio's legal capacity.

Corpuz v. Sto. Tomas (G.R. No. 186571, August 11, 2010)

Gerbert Corpuz, a former Filipino naturalized as Canadian, married Daisylyn Sto. Tomas (Filipina) in the Philippines in 2005, obtained a divorce in Canada later that year, and wanted to remarry here. The NSO told him the divorce could not be recorded without judicial recognition; the RTC denied his petition because only the Filipino spouse can invoke FC 26 par. 2. Doctrine: FC 26 par. 2 grants a substantive right only to the Filipino spouse; the alien spouse cannot invoke it to claim capacity. But the alien spouse is not without remedy: a foreign divorce decree, as a foreign judgment, may be recognized under Rule 39 Sec. 48(b) as presumptive evidence of a right, upon proof of the judgment and the alien's national law (Rule 132 Secs. 24-25). It may be repelled by evidence of want of jurisdiction, want of notice, collusion, fraud, or clear mistake of law or fact. The alien has a legal interest to petition for recognition (e.g., to remarry). A foreign divorce decree has no effect in the Philippines until judicially recognized; the civil registrar may not annotate it on the marriage certificate on the strength of the decree alone. Recognition may be sought in a Rule 108 proceeding for cancellation/correction of entries. Remanded to the RTC.

Fujiki v. Marinay (G.R. No. 196049, June 26, 2013)

Fujiki (Japanese) married Marinay (Filipina) in the Philippines; while that marriage subsisted, Marinay married Maekara (Japanese). Marinay obtained a Japanese family court judgment declaring the Maekara marriage void for bigamy. Fujiki petitioned the RTC for recognition of the Japanese judgment and annotation under Rule 108; the RTC dismissed under A.M. 02-11-10-SC (only the husband or wife of the questioned marriage may file). Doctrine: (1) A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC does not apply to a petition to recognize a foreign judgment relating to the status of a marriage where one party is a foreign citizen. (2) The petitioner need only prove the foreign judgment as a fact under the Rules of Court; Philippine courts do not relitigate the merits. The foreign judgment may be repelled only on the grounds in Rule 39 Sec. 48 (want of jurisdiction, notice, collusion, fraud, clear mistake of law or fact) and courts determine whether it is consistent with domestic public policy and other mandatory laws (e.g., a divorce between two Filipinos cannot be recognized). (3) The prior spouse is a real party in interest: the bigamous marriage threatens his property and financial interests and causes him emotional burden (Juliano-Llave), so he may petition to recognize a foreign judgment nullifying the bigamous marriage and have it annotated via Rule 108. (4) Recognition of a foreign judgment declaring a marriage void does not require a separate FC 26/nullity suit; it is consistent with FC 26 par. 2's policy of avoiding the absurdity of a marriage valid in one country and void in another.

Effects of a recognized foreign divorce

  • The marriage bond is dissolved as to both spouses; the Filipino spouse regains capacity to remarry (FC 26 par. 2).
  • The divorced alien is no longer a "spouse": no standing to manage conjugal/community property (Van Dorn), to file adultery as offended spouse (Pilapil), or to inherit as surviving spouse (Quita).
  • Effects on children (custody, support) are still determined by Philippine courts (Roehr v. Rodriguez); the alien parent may be compelled to support the child (Del Socorro v. Van Wilsem).
  • The decree must be annotated on the marriage record by court order (Corpuz; Rule 108).

Last-minute checklist

  • FC 1: "special contract of permanent union between a man and a woman entered into in accordance with law for the establishment of conjugal and family life"; "foundation of the family"; "inviolable social institution"; "nature, consequences, and incidents ... governed by law and not subject to stipulation"; exception: marriage settlements may fix the property relations during the marriage within the limits provided by this Code.
  • NCC 52: "not a mere contract"; settlements may "to a certain extent" fix property relations. PD 1083 Art. 14: "not only a civil contract but a social institution"; governed by "this Code and the Shari'a."
  • Const. Art. XV Sec. 2: marriage = inviolable social institution, foundation of the family, protected by the State. Art. XV Sec. 1: family = foundation of the nation. Art. II Sec. 12: family = basic autonomous social institution. Art. II Sec. 14: equality of women and men.
  • Marriage vs. ordinary contract (Tolentino): (1) one man + one woman; (2) law fixes rights/duties; (3) not terminable by agreement; (4) breach not actionable for damages (sanctions are penal and legal separation).
  • Three characteristics: civil, public order, natural. Eight principal effects (spouses' relations; legitimacy; parent-child; family relationship; emancipation; no donations; marital disqualification; criminal liability modified).
  • Same-sex: Obergefell (U.S., persuasive only); Falcis (dismissed on procedure; marriage remains man-woman; for Congress); Silverio (biological sex). Filipinos' same-sex marriage abroad: void here.
  • NCC 221: void — (1) contract for personal separation; (2) extrajudicial agreement during marriage dissolving CPG/ACP; (3) collusion to obtain legal separation or annulment; (4) simulated alienation to deprive heirs of legitimes. Cases: Panganiban v. Borromeo, In re Santiago, Selanova v. Mendoza (lawyer/judge liable), Lichauco-De Leon.
  • Albios: marriage for convenience (citizenship) valid; consent real; motive irrelevant. Morimoto: no ceremony + signed blank certificate + no license = void; Albios inapplicable. Marriage in jest by both = no consent, void.
  • Breach of promise: not actionable per se (Hermosisima; Estremos; Congress deleted the chapter). Damages if: Wassmer (walk-out after publicized preparations; Art. 21); Baksh (deceit/moral seduction; Art. 21); recovery of money advanced/gifts (Domalagan; Art. 22). Tanjanco: mutual passion is not seduction. Cabague: mutual promise to marry provable orally; other agreements in consideration of marriage need writing (NCC 1403(2)(c)).
  • NCC 220: in case of doubt, presumptions favor the solidarity of the family: validity of marriage, indissolubility, legitimacy, community of property, parental authority, validity of defense of family members.
  • Rule 131 Sec. 3(aa): man and woman deporting themselves as husband and wife are disputably (not conclusively) presumed married. Rebuttable only by cogent proof (Perido). Common-law marriage not recognized (Fiel v. Banawa).
  • Marriage certificate = best evidence, not the only evidence, and not a formal requisite. Failure to send copies within 15 days does not invalidate (Pugeda).
  • NCC 15 (status/capacity follow Filipinos abroad; "no exceptions" per Uribe); NCC 17 par. 1 (form = lex loci), par. 2 (before PH consul abroad = PH solemnities), par. 3 (prohibitive laws/public policy not defeated by foreign laws or judgments).
  • FC 26 par. 1 exceptions: 35(1), (4), (5), (6), 36, 37, 38. Not excepted: 35(2) authority, 35(3) license, proxy/form, voidable marriages, Art. 41 marriages. NCC 71's old exceptions: bigamous, polygamous, incestuous.
  • London drill: first cousins (38(1)) void; 17 years old, with or without consent (35(1)) void; bigamous not under Art. 41 (35(4)) void; mistake of identity (35(5)) void; adopted children of same adopter (38(8)) void; 18 without consent = voidable only; proxy = valid; step-siblings = valid; Prime Minister solemnizing = valid if authorized there.
  • FC 10: consul-general, consul or vice-consul; only between Filipino citizens abroad; consul issues the license and performs LCR duties; ambassador has no authority; consul cannot solemnize in the PH.
  • FC 21: foreigner ("either or both") submits certificate of legal capacity from "their respective diplomatic or consular officials" "before a marriage license can be obtained"; stateless/refugee: affidavit in lieu. Lack of certificate does not affect validity (irregularity only).
  • FC 26 par. 2 inserted by E.O. 227 (July 17, 1987); FC effective August 3, 1988. Text: "validly celebrated ... divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law" (no "likewise").
