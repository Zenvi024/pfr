Covers Family Code Arts. 55–67, with the related Civil Code provisions (NCC 97–108, 221, 370–372, 921(4), 1002), FC Arts. 26(2), 43(2), 49, 86(3), 99(2), 101, 126(2), 135, 198, 213, RA 9262 Sec. 19, PD 1083 (Muslim divorce) and A.M. Roadmap and the Uribe way of answering

Uribe tests legal separation through the codal wording. Most of his items take one article and change a single word or phrase. Examples are "repeated", "without justifiable cause", "more than six years", "even if pardoned", "occurrence" versus "discovery", "filing" versus "finality", "intestate" versus "intestate and testamentary", "may revoke" versus "revoked by operation of law", "guardian" versus "receiver" and "written notification to the insured" versus "finality". Learn the articles word for word, and know the following for each:

  1. What is the ground (Art. 55, with the elements of each paragraph)?
  2. Who may file (only the aggrieved or innocent spouse; the action is purely personal)?
  3. When (five years from the occurrence of the cause, Art. 57)?
  4. What happens while the case is pending (Arts. 58–62, the six-month cooling-off period and its RA 9262 exception, living separately, administration, support and custody)?
  5. What defenses bar the decree (Art. 56 (1)–(6), plus death of a party and reconciliation)?
  6. What the decree does (Arts. 63–64)?
  7. How reconciliation undoes it (Arts. 65–67)?
Uribe: He asks you to rule on the facts and cite the provision before the case. If there are two or three reasons, give all of them ("ubusin mo ang reasons"). Don't assume facts that are not stated ("Huwag mag-imbento"). What prescribes is the right of action, not the ground.

A. Concept and historical background

1. Divorce and its kinds

Divorce is the dissolution or partial suspension, by law, of the marital relation. There are two kinds:

  • Absolute divorce (a vinculo matrimonii) dissolves the bond of matrimony. It ends the marriage, and the parties may remarry.
  • Relative divorce (a mensa et thoro, "from bed and board") suspends the common life. The spouses may live separately, but the bond stays in full force. Legal separation is a relative divorce.
Tolentino: The Code Commission's draft of the New Civil Code allowed both absolute and relative divorce, "as the petitioner may choose." During the deliberations in Congress, absolute divorce was eliminated and only relative divorce was kept. At the suggestion of women leaders, the term "relative divorce" was changed to "legal separation" to avoid the implication the word divorce carries. The Family Code adopted the same terminology. The FC also increased the grounds. Tolentino calls this "an obvious compromise offered to advocates of absolute divorce," and says it seems to run counter to the Code's avowed intent to strengthen the family.
Uribe (recitation): "What is the relevance of divorce and legal separation? Are they similar?" Answer: Legal separation is a kind of divorce which is relative. To tell whether a foreign divorce decree is absolute or relative, check whether it imposes conditions on remarriage. An absolute divorce gives capacity to remarry from the issuance of the decree. A decree that leaves the parties unable to remarry, or able to remarry only on conditions, is in that sense relative.

Doctrine (Uribe outline): (1) Titles 4 (matrimony) and 12 (civil registry) of Book I of the Spanish Civil Code were never in force here, having been suspended by the Governor-General. Under the Spanish regime he had the power to suspend a general law. (2) The Law of Civil Marriage of 1870 was never extended here except Arts. 44–78, promulgated in 1883. (3) Canon law had no binding force outside the Church except where civil authorities made it part of Spain's civil law. The Council of Trent authorizes separation by the Church but does not state the causes, and the church laws that state them do not have the force of civil law. Under the Partidas, the only divorce was relative, which dissolved the conjugal property but did not sever the bond. Recrimination: Tolentino cites this case for the rule that when both spouses are guilty (the husband of concubinage, the wife of adultery), separation is denied to both.

3. Legal separation distinguished from separation in fact (cf. FC 238–248)

  • Legal separation can be effected only by a court decree. It suspends the common life as to both person and property.
  • Separation in fact (de facto) means the spouses live apart without any judgment. The marriage, the property regime and all marital obligations remain. Under Arts. 100/127, separation in fact does not affect the regime, but the spouse who leaves without just cause loses the right to be supported. Under Arts. 239–248 (summary proceedings), when spouses are separated in fact or one has abandoned the other and the consent of the other is required for a transaction but is withheld or cannot be obtained, a verified petition for judicial authorization may be filed.
  • Separation of property affects only the property relations. The spouses may actually be living together. It may be effected by agreement subject to judicial approval (Art. 134–136), but a decree of legal separation can never be rendered on the parties' agreement (Art. 60) (Tolentino).

4. Agreements to separate (cf. NCC 221(1))

Art. 221, NCC. The following shall be void and of no effect:
(1) Any contract for personal separation between husband and wife;
(2) Every extrajudicial agreement, during the marriage, for the dissolution of the conjugal partnership of gains or of the absolute community of property between husband and wife;
(3) Every collusion to obtain a decree of legal separation, or of annulment of marriage; …
Albano v. Gapusan (71 SCRA 26, 1976)

A judge, while a notary, notarized a document by which spouses agreed to separate and to liquidate their conjugal partnership. Doctrine: Such covenants are contrary to law, morals and good customs and undermine marriage and the family. The law considers void "any contract for personal separation between husband and wife" and "every extrajudicial agreement, during the marriage, for the dissolution of the conjugal partnership." Even before the NCC, the extrajudicial dissolution of the conjugal partnership without judicial sanction was void. The notary was reprimanded.

In re Atty. Rufillo Bucana (72 SCRA 14, 1976)

A lawyer notarized an agreement in which the husband could take a concubine and the wife could live in adulterous relations with another man, without opposition from either. Doctrine: The agreement is contrary to law, morals and good customs, and it even induces each party to commit bigamy. Marriage is an inviolable social institution in whose purity the public is deeply interested. The lawyer was disciplined.

Uribe (recitation, recalling Bucana): Suppose the spouses later file for legal separation. The ground would be sexual infidelity. Would the petition prosper? No. The document, though illegal, amounts to consent to future acts of infidelity (People v. Schneckenberger). It is consent and not condonation, because it was given before the acts.
Note (conflicting view): Tolentino argues that Art. 221 was repealed by the Family Code (Art. 254) and the FC has no similar provision. On that view, the pre-Civil Code rule is restored and an agreement of separation that has actually taken effect would be valid. Uribe's syllabus, Albano and Bucana treat such agreements as void. Marriage is "not subject to stipulation" (FC Art. 1). An extrajudicial dissolution of the regime is ineffective without a judicial order (FC Art. 134). No decree can rest on the parties' agreement (Art. 60), and no compromise is allowed on legal separation (NCC 2035). For Uribe's quiz, answer VOID, and add that the agreement may still operate as consent, a defense under Art. 56(2).

A.3. Legal separation distinguished from absolute divorce

a. Divorce decreed abroad (FC 26(2))

Art. 26(2), FC. Where a marriage between a Filipino citizen and a foreigner is validly celebrated and a divorce is thereafter validly obtained abroad by the alien spouse capacitating him or her to remarry, the Filipino spouse shall have capacity to remarry under Philippine law.
  • General rule: Philippine law has no absolute divorce, and our courts cannot grant one. Under the nationality principle (NCC 15) and NCC 17(3), a divorce obtained abroad between two Filipinos is not recognized. The marriage subsists, so a second marriage by either of them is bigamous and void.
  • Exceptions/recognition: (1) A divorce between two aliens is recognized if valid under their national law. (2) In a mixed marriage, Art. 26(2) applies. It covers a Filipino who has since been naturalized abroad (Republic v. Orbecido, 2005: citizenship is reckoned when the divorce is obtained). It applies whoever initiated the divorce (Republic v. Manalo, 2018). (3) The foreign divorce and the foreign law must be pleaded and proved as facts (Garcia v. Recio).
Tenchavez v. Escaño (15 SCRA 355, 1965)

A Filipina obtained a Nevada divorce from her Filipino husband and married an American. Doctrine: A foreign divorce between Filipino citizens, sought and decreed after the effectivity of the NCC, is not recognized here. Recognizing it would violate our declared public policy. Her remarriage and cohabitation with someone other than her lawful husband entitled the husband to a decree of legal separation under Philippine law, and to damages (Tolentino).

Van Dorn v. Romillo (1985)

Doctrine: Under the nationality principle (NCC 15), only Philippine nationals are covered by the policy against absolute divorce. Aliens may obtain divorces abroad, which may be recognized here if valid under their national law. Here the American ex-husband, divorced in Nevada, was no longer the husband and could not claim conjugal rights over the Filipina's business.

Pilapil v. Ibay-Somera (1989)

Doctrine: A German husband who validly divorced his Filipina wife in Germany is no longer an "offended spouse." He cannot file a complaint for adultery, because the complainant must still be married to the accused when the complaint is filed. Lesson for legal separation: where the alien's divorce is recognized, there is no longer a marriage and nothing left to separate.

Garcia v. Recio (G.R. No. 138322, October 2, 2001)

Doctrine: Philippine law does not provide for absolute divorce, so our courts cannot grant it. A marriage between two Filipinos cannot be dissolved even by a divorce obtained abroad (NCC 15, 17). In mixed marriages, Art. 26 lets the Filipino remarry if the alien validly obtained the divorce abroad. A divorce between two aliens may be recognized if consistent with their national laws. The divorce decree and the alien's national law must be proven, since our courts do not take judicial notice of them. A divorce may be absolute or may impose restrictions on remarriage, so the legal capacity of the alien to remarry must be shown.

Uribe (on Garcia-Recio): Even if the divorce decree is absolute, the defense that the case is "moot and academic" is weak. The validity of the divorce decree carries many effects, such as the legitimacy of children.

b. Muslim divorce (PD 1083, MC Arts. 45–55)

Art. 45, PD 1083. Definition and forms. Divorce is the formal dissolution of the marriage bond in accordance with this Code to be granted only after the exhaustion of all possible means of reconciliation between the spouses. It may be effected by:
(a) Repudiation of the wife by the husband (talaq);
(b) Vow of continence by the husband (ila);
(c) Injurious assimilation of the wife by the husband (zihar);
(d) Acts of imprecation (li'an);
(e) Redemption by the wife (khul');
(f) Exercise by the wife of the delegated right to repudiate (tafwid); or
(g) Judicial decree (faskh).
  • Talaq (Art. 46): a single repudiation during the wife's non-menstrual period (tuhr) in which the husband totally abstained from carnal relations. It becomes irrevocable after the prescribed 'idda. After a first or second repudiation, the husband may take her back (ruju) within the 'idda without a new contract. Otherwise the talaq becomes irrevocable (talaq bain sugra).
  • Ila (Art. 47): a vow to abstain from carnal relations kept for not less than four months. The court may grant divorce after due notice and hearing.
  • Zihar (Art. 48), li'an (Art. 49, the husband accuses the wife of adultery in court and both perform imprecation, resulting in perpetual divorce), khul' (Art. 50, the wife offers to return or renounce her dower), tafwid (Art. 51).
  • Faskh (Art. 52), on the wife's petition: (a) neglect or failure to support the family for at least six consecutive months; (b) conviction of the husband by final judgment sentencing him to imprisonment for at least one year; (c) failure to perform his marital obligation for six months without reasonable cause; (d) impotency; (e) insanity or an incurable disease making continued marriage injurious; (f) unusual cruelty (Art. 53); (g) other causes recognized under Muslim law.
  • Effects of an irrevocable talaq or faskh (Art. 54): (a) the marriage bond is severed and the spouses may remarry; (b) they lose mutual rights of inheritance; (c) custody follows Art. 78; (d) the wife recovers her whole dower if the divorce came after consummation, or one-half if before; (e) the husband is not discharged from support under Art. 67; (f) the conjugal partnership, if stipulated, is dissolved and liquidated. Art. 55 extends these effects to ila, zihar, li'an and khul'.
Quiz trap: Compare the imprisonment grounds. FC 55(4) requires a final judgment sentencing the respondent to imprisonment of more than six years, and it is a ground for legal separation (the bond stays). MC Art. 52(b) requires imprisonment for at least one year, and it is a ground for faskh (the bond is severed).
Uribe (recitation): A and B, Filipinos, married in Cotabato City under civil law. A worked in Saudi Arabia, converted to Islam, and married another Filipina there. B petitions for legal separation. Does it matter whether the second marriage is valid under PH law? No. "The spouse will always be allowed to file for legal separation whether or not the second marriage is valid." Conversion does not let a spouse married under civil law use PD 1083 to marry again. This is the recurring "convert to the Muslim faith" question.
Note (status of absolute divorce): As of the date of this reviewer, the Philippines still has no general absolute-divorce law for non-Muslims. Divorce bills have been filed and debated in Congress, but none has been enacted. Answer on the basis of the FC: there is legal separation, nullity and annulment, recognition of foreign divorce under Art. 26(2), and Muslim divorce under PD 1083.

B. Grounds for legal separation (Art. 55; compare NCC 97)

Art. 55, FC. A petition for legal separation may be filed on any of the following grounds:
(1) Repeated physical violence or grossly abusive conduct directed against the petitioner, a common child, or a child of the petitioner;
(2) Physical violence or moral pressure to compel the petitioner to change religious or political affiliation;
(3) Attempt of respondent to corrupt or induce the petitioner, a common child, or a child of the petitioner, to engage in prostitution, or connivance in such corruption or inducement;
(4) Final judgment sentencing the respondent to imprisonment of more than six years, even if pardoned;
(5) Drug addiction or habitual alcoholism of the respondent;
(6) Lesbianism or homosexuality of the respondent;
(7) Contracting by the respondent of a subsequent bigamous marriage, whether in the Philippines or abroad;
(8) Sexual infidelity or perversion;
(9) Attempt by the respondent against the life of the petitioner; or
(10) Abandonment of petitioner by respondent without justifiable cause for more than one year.
For purposes of this Article, the term "child" shall include a child by nature or by adoption.
Art. 97, NCC (old law). A petition for legal separation may be filed:
(1) For adultery on the part of the wife and for concubinage on the part of the husband as defined in the Penal Code; or
(2) An attempt by one spouse against the life of the other.

General notes on Art. 55:

  • The enumeration is exclusive. A ground not listed (e.g., a wife's life as a prostitute before the marriage, incompatibility, gambling) is not a ground for legal separation, though it may support other remedies (Arts. 72, 135).
  • Grounds that protect a child appear only in pars. (1) and (3): "the petitioner, a common child, or a child of the petitioner." "Child" includes a child by nature or by adoption. A child of the respondent alone is not covered.
  • No criminal conviction is required for any ground except par. (4), which by its terms requires a final judgment of conviction. Pars. (1), (8) and (9) may be proved by preponderance of evidence in the civil case.
  • Each act is a separate ground with its own five-year prescriptive period (Art. 57).

B.1 Sexual infidelity or perversion (FC 55(8); cf. NCC 97(1); RPC 333–334)

Art. 333, RPC (Adultery). Adultery is committed by any married woman who shall have sexual intercourse with a man not her husband and by the man who has carnal knowledge of her, knowing her to be married, even if the marriage be subsequently declared void.
Art. 334, RPC (Concubinage). Any husband who shall keep a mistress in the conjugal dwelling, or, shall have sexual intercourse, under scandalous circumstances, with a woman who is not his wife, or shall cohabit with her in any other place, shall be punished by prision correccional in its minimum and medium periods.
Tolentino: Under the NCC, the infidelity had to be adultery (wife) or concubinage (husband) as defined in the RPC, which made it harder for a wife to obtain legal separation. A husband's single act of intercourse is not concubinage unless it occurs in the conjugal dwelling, under scandalous circumstances, or with cohabitation. Under the FC, sexual intercourse with another person by either spouse is a ground because it is infidelity, even if the husband's act is not concubinage. The FC thus gives "equal remedy for unequal injury." A paramour is one who loves or is loved illicitly and takes the place of a husband or wife without legal right.
Goitia v. Campos-Rueda (35 Phil. 252, 1916)

The husband demanded that the wife perform unchaste and lascivious acts (perversion). When she refused, he maltreated her, so she left and sued for support. Doctrine: A wife forced to leave the conjugal abode by her husband, without fault on her part, may maintain an action for separate maintenance. The husband's option under the support provisions to give support by receiving her in his home does not apply, since it cannot be used where there is a moral or legal obstacle (now FC 204). Relief does not depend on first obtaining divorce or legal separation. Relevance: base sexual demands are the type of "perversion" in par. (8), and a wife who leaves for this reason leaves with justifiable cause, so she has not "abandoned" under par. (10).

People v. Zapata (88 Phil. 688, 1951)

(Spelled "Zapanta" in some outlines; the syllabus spells it "Zapata.") Doctrine: Adultery is an instantaneous crime "of result and not of tendency," consummated at the moment of each carnal union. Each sexual intercourse is a separate crime, and as many complaints may be filed as there are acts without double jeopardy. Adultery is not a continuing offense, because there is no unity of criminal intent. A pardon covers only prior acts, not those committed after it. Application to legal separation: each act of infidelity is a separate ground with its own five-year period under Art. 57, and a condonation of past acts does not cover later ones.

Gandionco v. Peñaranda (155 SCRA 725, 1987)

The wife sued for legal separation on the ground of concubinage. The husband sought suspension of the civil case pending the criminal case. Doctrine: A decree of legal separation on the ground of concubinage may issue upon preponderance of evidence in the legal separation action. No criminal proceeding or conviction is necessary. The civil action may proceed ahead of, or simultaneously with, the criminal action. It is not an action "to enforce the civil liability arising from the offense," but one to obtain the right to live separately with its consequences (dissolution of the regime, custody, support, disqualification from inheriting). Uribe's outline adds that it is not mere sexual infidelity that constituted the ground under the NCC, since the infidelity had to be adultery or concubinage as defined in the RPC. That is the old-law rule. Under FC 55(8), any act of sexual infidelity suffices.

Note: One student reviewer (B29) says sexual infidelity "is a prejudicial question which shall first be decided in a criminal prosecution." That is wrong. Gandionco holds the opposite: no conviction is needed, and the civil case may go ahead.

Condition (3) is the Tolentino view. If an MCQ offers this as the only complete choice, pick it, but remember that Art. 55(5) has no time qualifier.

B.3 Attempt on the life of the other spouse (FC 55(9); cf. NCC 97(2))

Muñoz v. Barrios (CA, 51 O.G. 5247)

Doctrine: An attempt on the life of a person implies that the actor is moved by an intention to kill. The husband's maltreatment of the wife, not shown to be motivated by intent to kill, does not constitute an attempt on her life under NCC 97(2). Under the FC, such maltreatment, if repeated, is par. (1) instead.

Tolentino: The act must constitute attempted or frustrated parricide. If the act against the spouse's life is justified, it is not an illegal act and is no ground. If a husband catches the wife in adultery and tries to kill her but fails, he is not guilty of an act that can be a ground for legal separation (cf. RPC 247, death or physical injuries under exceptional circumstances). The same applies to a spouse who acts in self-defense or in defense of a child against the other spouse's unlawful aggression.

De la Cruz v. De la Cruz (22 SCRA 333, 1968)

The husband left the conjugal abode but kept managing the conjugal properties with the same zeal and religiously supported his wife and children. Doctrine: Mere physical separation, without more, is not abandonment that warrants separation of property. The remedies are designed to protect the partnership from waste and to shield the wife from want. Denying the wife's petition does not condone the husband's act. It only shows that there is no cause of action. Abandonment implies physical, moral and financial desertion.

Partosa-Jo v. CA (1992)

Doctrine: Abandonment implies a departure by one spouse with the avowed intent never to return, followed by prolonged absence without just cause, and without in the meantime providing in the least for one's family although able to do so. There must be absolute cessation of marital relations, duties and rights, with the intention of perpetual separation. A husband who refused to admit the wife into the conjugal home and gave no support for years had abandoned her.

Ong Eng Kiam v. Ong (2006)

The wife sued for legal separation on the ground of repeated physical violence and grossly abusive conduct. The husband argued that she had abandoned the home and only wanted control of the conjugal property. Doctrine: Repeated physical violence and grossly abusive conduct were proven. Her departure to escape the abuse was justified, so it was not abandonment "without justifiable cause." There was no recrimination under Art. 56(4) to bar her petition.

Republic v. Quintero-Hamano (428 SCRA 735, 2004) and Republic v. Encelan (G.R. No. 170022, Jan. 9, 2013)

Doctrine: Abandonment and sexual infidelity, even if true, do not necessarily constitute psychological incapacity. They are simply grounds for legal separation, unless shown to be manifestations of a disordered personality that made the spouse completely unable to discharge essential marital obligations (see also Dedel v. CA: sexual infidelity per se is a ground for legal separation, not Art. 36).

B.6 RA 9262 (Anti-VAWC) and legal separation

Sec. 19, RA 9262. Legal Separation Cases. – In cases of legal separation, where violence as specified in this Act is alleged, Article 58 of the Family Code shall not apply. The court shall proceed on the main case and other incidents of the case as soon as possible. The hearing on any application for a protection order filed by the petitioner must be conducted within the mandatory period specified in this Act.
  • Violence against a woman or her child (RA 9262 Sec. 5) often overlaps with Art. 55(1), (2), (3) and (9). Psychological violence may be committed through marital infidelity (Araza v. People), which overlaps with Art. 55(8).
  • The exception covers only cases where violence as specified in RA 9262 is alleged, i.e., violence against a woman or her child. A husband who alleges that his wife attempted to kill him still faces the six-month cooling-off period of Art. 58.
  • Protection orders (BPO, TPO, PPO) may be sought. Provisional orders and protection orders may also issue under A.M. No. 02-11-12-SC upon receipt of a verified petition for legal separation, "and at any time during the proceeding," motu proprio or on application, with or without a hearing.
  • RA 9262 Sec. 28 gives the woman victim custody and support of her children. Children below seven are given to the mother unless there are compelling reasons, and a victim suffering from battered woman syndrome is not disqualified from custody.

C. Who may file (Art. 55; compare NCC 99–100)

  • Only the aggrieved or innocent spouse. Art. 55 speaks of the "petitioner" against the "respondent." Art. 56 bars a petitioner who condoned, consented, connived, colluded or is also guilty. A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC: the petition "may be filed only by the husband or the wife."
  • Never the guilty spouse. This is the clean-hands doctrine: an offender cannot profit from his wrong.
  • Not heirs, parents, children, creditors or the State. The action is purely personal. If the petitioner dies, the heirs cannot substitute (Lapuz-Sy v. Eufemio).
Art. 100, NCC (old law). The legal separation may be claimed only by the innocent spouse, provided there has been no condonation of or consent to the adultery or concubinage. Where both spouses are offenders, a legal separation cannot be claimed by either of them. Collusion between the parties to obtain legal separation shall cause the dismissal of the petition.
Art. 99, NCC (old law, not retained). No person shall be entitled to a legal separation who has not resided in the Philippines for one year prior to the filing of the petition, unless the cause for the legal separation has taken place within the territory of this Republic.
Quiz trap: The FC has no one-year residence requirement like NCC 99. Venue under A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC is the Family Court of the province or city where the petitioner or the respondent has been residing for at least six months prior to filing, or, for a non-resident respondent, where he may be found, at the petitioner's election. This is a venue rule, not a substantive qualification.
Lapuz-Sy v. Eufemio (43 SCRA 177, January 31, 1972)

The wife sued for legal separation (abandonment and concubinage). The husband counterclaimed for declaration of nullity. Before decision, the wife died in a vehicular accident, and her father sought to substitute. Doctrine: An action for legal separation is purely personal. The Civil Code recognized this by allowing only the innocent spouse, and no one else, to claim it (NCC 100), and by letting the spouses stop the proceedings and even rescind a decree already rendered by reconciliation (NCC 108). Being personal, it dies with the party: actio personalis moritur cum persona. The property rights involved are mere incidents of the bed-and-board separation. They do not keep the action alive, and claims to the conjugal property must be settled in the estate proceedings.

Tolentino: Legal separation is a personal right and does not survive death. This holds even when the decree has been granted but has not yet become final at the time of a party's death (citing Lapuz-Sy; Macadangdang v. CA, 108 SCRA 314).
Note (A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC): The Rule likewise provides that if either party dies at any stage before the entry of judgment, the court orders the case closed and terminated, without prejudice to settlement of the estate in proper proceedings.

D. When the petition may be filed (Art. 57; compare NCC 102, NCC 99)

Art. 57, FC. An action for legal separation shall be filed within five years from the time of the occurrence of the cause. (102a)
Art. 102, NCC (old law). An action for legal separation cannot be filed except within one year from and after the date on which the plaintiff became cognizant of the cause and within five years from and after the date when such cause occurred.
  • Single period: five years, counted from the occurrence of the cause. Knowledge or discovery is irrelevant. The NCC's one-year-from-knowledge period was dropped.
  • Each act is a separate cause. For repeated infidelity, each act has its own five-year period (Zapata logic; 2007 bar). It is not a continuing offense.
  • Prescription is a defense (Art. 56(6)) that the court may notice even if not pleaded, because the State is interested (Brown v. Yambao).
Tolentino: The law does not encourage legal separation. It lets the action prescribe even when the offended party had no knowledge of the cause. Some grounds may be unknown to the plaintiff: an attempt to induce a child into prostitution, a subsequent bigamous marriage abroad, sexual infidelity. If the plaintiff learns of the ground only after five years from its occurrence, he can no longer sue.
Contreras v. Macaraig (33 SCRA 222, May 29, 1970)

Doctrine (under NCC 102): The requirement that the complaint be filed within one year after the plaintiff became cognizant of the cause is not of a prescriptive nature but is of the essence of the cause of action. This accords with the philosophy that marriage is an inviolable social institution, so the law imposes strict requirements before it allows a disruption of its status. Note: The one-year knowledge rule no longer exists under Art. 57, but the case's philosophy (strictness in favor of the marriage) remains.

Matubis v. Praxedes (109 Phil. 709, October 25, 1960)

The spouses agreed in writing to live separately and that neither would prosecute the other for adultery or concubinage. The husband then cohabited with another woman, and the wife sued years later. Doctrine: (1) Prescription. The husband's cohabitation was concubinage, a ground, but the complaint was not filed within one year from cognizance and within five years from occurrence (NCC 102), so it was dismissed. (2) Consent/condonation. Only the innocent spouse may claim legal separation, provided she has not condoned or consented (NCC 100). Having consented in writing, she is undeserving of the court's sympathy (citing People v. Schneckenburger).

Bugayong v. Ginez (100 Phil. 616, December 28, 1956)

Listed by Uribe under both D (timing) and F (condonation). See Condonation below. Condonation is implied from sexual intercourse after knowledge of the other's infidelity.

B (wife) went to Baguio with her boyfriend in 2019, and they had sexual relations. They repeated their "annual retreat" on January 1, 2020 and again on January 1, 2021. Husband A learned of all three in 2023 but did nothing. He filed for legal separation on September 12, 2025. Will the petition prosper?

Under Art. 57 the action must be filed within five years from the occurrence of the cause, and each act of sexual infidelity is a separate ground, not a continuing offense (People v. Zapata). The 2019 act and the January 1, 2020 act prescribed (by January 1, 2025 at the latest). The January 1, 2021 act prescribes only on January 1, 2026, so the September 2025 filing is timely as to it. A's 2023 knowledge is irrelevant to the period, and his mere inaction is not condonation (Ocampo v. Florenciano). (Uribe pressed this point: "Why did you consider the 2021 meet up and not the 2019?")

A petition for legal separation was filed today. The acts alleged were committed by the respondent in 2011, 2012 and 2013. Will the petition prosper?

Answer: No. All the causes occurred more than five years before filing (Art. 57; Art. 56(6)). "Say it's a continuing offense?" Still no: there is no continuing offense, because each act is a ground with its own period (Uribe recitation).

W had an affair in March 2018. H discovered it only in January 2022 and filed for legal separation in December 2023.

Answer: Prescribed. Five years from occurrence (March 2018) ended in March 2023. Discovery does not matter under the FC (Art. 57).

E. Effects of pendency of the petition (Arts. 58, 61, 62; cf. 49, 198)

1. Cooling-off period (Art. 58; compare NCC 103)

Art. 58, FC. An action for legal separation shall in no case be tried before six months shall have elapsed since the filing of the petition. (103)
  • Purpose: to give the spouses a chance to reconcile. During the six months, passions may subside and forgiveness may come (Tolentino).
  • What is barred: the trial on the merits only. The court may hear and act on incidents: support pendente lite, custody, administration of property, injunctions and provisional or protection orders.
  • Applies only to legal separation. It does not apply to annulment, declaration of nullity or judicial separation of property.
  • Exception: RA 9262 Sec. 19. Where violence under the Act is alleged, Art. 58 does not apply, and the court proceeds on the main case as soon as possible.
Araneta v. Concepcion (99 Phil. 709, July 31, 1956)

The husband sued for legal separation on the ground of adultery. The wife filed an omnibus petition for custody of the three minor children, support for herself and the children, and related relief. The judge resolved it on the pleadings, refusing to receive evidence because of NCC 103. Doctrine: The six-month period is a cooling-off period meant to make reconciliation possible. But this practical expedient does not override the other provisions on custody of the children and alimony and support pendente lite, which the court must determine according to the circumstances (NCC 105). All provisions should be reconciled and given effect. Evidence not affecting the cause of the separation, such as the actual custody of the children and the means conducive to their welfare, should be received so the court can decide properly. If courts ignore actual facts, rank injustice may be caused.

Somosa-Ramos v. Vamenta (46 SCRA 110, July 29, 1972)

Doctrine: The ancillary remedy of preliminary mandatory injunction is not barred by the six-month suspension of trial. The court is to remain passive and must leave the parties alone on the merits. But NCC 104 shows that management of the spouses' property need not stay unresolved during the six months, since an administrator may even be appointed. The absolute limitation of NCC 103 is thereby eased, and the parties may be heard on ancillary motions.

De la Viña v. Villareal (41 Phil. 13, July 31, 1920)

Doctrine: The rule that the wife's domicile follows the husband's is not absolute. The wife may acquire a separate domicile when the theoretical unity is dissolved: on the institution of divorce proceedings, where the husband has given cause for divorce, on separation by agreement, on desertion or cruel treatment by the husband, or on forfeiture by the wife. In an action for divorce in which partition of the conjugal property is prayed for, the wife may obtain a preliminary injunction against the husband to stop him from alienating or encumbering conjugal property while the action is pending. A wife unlawfully ejected so the husband can indulge his illicit relations may establish her own residence.

Uribe (recitation, asked twice in 2018 and 2025): "A petition for legal separation was filed; 3–4 weeks after filing, the court scheduled the case for hearing. Proper?" Answer: It depends on the purpose. The court cannot proceed to trial on the merits, but it can hear motions for provisional remedies such as support and custody. The law bars only trial "on the merits."
Quiz traps: (1) "An action for annulment shall in no case be tried before six months…" is FALSE, because Art. 58 covers only legal separation. (2) "This petition cannot be tried before six months: (a) nullity (b) annulment (c) separation of property" gives (e) none of the above. (3) "During the six months the court cannot act on custody or support pendente lite" is FALSE (Araneta). (4) "A motion filed within three months from filing may be validly acted upon" is TRUE.

2. Right to live separately; administration of property (Art. 61; compare NCC 104)

Art. 61, FC. After the filing of the petition for legal separation, the spouses shall be entitled to live separately from each other.
The court, in the absence of a written agreement between the spouses, shall designate either of them or a third person to administer the absolute community or conjugal partnership property. The administrator appointed by the court shall have the same powers and duties as those of a guardian under the Rules of Court. (104a)
  • The right to live separately arises upon filing, not upon the decree or its finality. The plaintiff may have a separate domicile (Tolentino).
  • The marriage bond and the duty of fidelity continue. Infidelity during the case remains adultery or concubinage, and it is a new ground.
  • Administration: the spouses' written agreement controls. Without one, the court designates either spouse or a third person. The administrator has the powers and duties of a guardian, not a receiver.
  • Under old NCC 104 (Tolentino): when one spouse's consent to a transaction of the other is required by law, judicial authorization is needed unless that spouse voluntarily consents. Injunction may issue where there is reason to fear that the offending spouse will dispose of conjugal assets (Sabalones v. CA; De la Viña).
Quiz traps: "The spouses shall be entitled to live separately only from the time the decree becomes final and executory" is FALSE (Art. 61: after the filing). "…upon the start of the trial" is FALSE. "The administrator … shall have the powers of a receiver" is FALSE (guardian). "The obligation of mutual fidelity ceases upon filing" is FALSE.

3. Support and custody pendente lite (Art. 62 in relation to Art. 49; Art. 198)

Art. 62, FC. During the pendency of the action for legal separation, the provisions of Article 49 shall likewise apply to the support of the spouses and the custody and support of the common children. (105a)
Art. 49, FC. During the pendency of the action and in the absence of adequate provisions in a written agreement between the spouses, the Court shall provide for the support of the spouses and the custody and support of their common children. The Court shall give paramount consideration to the moral and material welfare of said children and their choice of the parent with whom they wish to remain as provided to in Title IX. It shall also provide for appropriate visitation rights of the other parent.
Art. 198, FC. During the proceedings for legal separation or for annulment of marriage, and for declaration of nullity of marriage, the spouses and their children shall be supported from the properties of the absolute community or the conjugal partnership. After the final judgment granting the petition, the obligation of mutual support between the spouses ceases. However, in case of legal separation, the court may order that the guilty spouse shall give support to the innocent one, specifying the terms of such order.
Tolentino: Support pendente lite comes from the ACP/CPG (Art. 198). It presupposes a valid subsisting marriage. If the answer denies the marriage, alimony pendente lite should be denied (Yangco v. Rohde). A defendant who defies the order may be cited for contempt or subjected to execution. The amount is provisional and may be modified. The grant is discretionary. Custody pendente lite is fixed (1) by agreement of the spouses, which is not disturbed unless prejudicial to the children, or (2) by court order based on the children's welfare. Arts. 49 and 213 must be observed.
Yangco v. Rohde (1 Phil. 404, October 13, 1902)

Doctrine: A wife's right to support depends on her status as such. Where the existence of that status is put in issue by the pleadings, it cannot be presumed in order to grant alimony. The claimant must prove possession of the civil status of spouse.

Lerma v. CA (61 SCRA 440, December 20, 1974)

The husband had sued the wife for adultery, and the wife filed for legal separation and support pendente lite. Doctrine: Adultery may be a defense against support pendente lite, even when the support is to come from the conjugal property. NCC 292 (now FC 198) is not in itself the source of the right to support. It merely provides that support during the pendency shall come from the conjugal property, and it does not preclude losing the right in proper cases. It also presupposes a prima facie showing that the action will prosper. The court must provisionally determine "the probable outcome of the case" (Rule 61). A petition in bad faith, filed by one who is herself guilty of a ground for legal separation, is not within the law's intendment, and loss of the substantive right to support (NCC 303; NCC 921: "when the spouse has given cause for legal separation") is incompatible with support pendente lite.

Note (A.M. No. 02-11-12-SC): Upon receipt of a verified petition for legal separation, and at any time during the proceeding, the court may motu proprio or upon application issue provisional orders and protection orders, with or without a hearing. Section 2 lists the factors for spousal support. Child support comes from the ACP/CPG, and either or both parents may be ordered to give it in proportion to their resources.

G. Court procedure (Arts. 59–60; A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC)

Art. 59, FC. No legal separation may be decreed unless the Court has taken steps toward the reconciliation of the spouses and is fully satisfied, despite such efforts, that reconciliation is highly improbable. (n)
Art. 60, FC. No decree of legal separation shall be based upon a stipulation of facts or a confession of judgment.
In any case, the Court shall order the prosecuting attorney or fiscal assigned to it to take steps to prevent collusion between the parties and to take care that the evidence is not fabricated or suppressed. (101a)
Art. 101, NCC (old law). No decree of legal separation shall be promulgated upon a stipulation of facts or by confession of judgment.
In case of non-appearance of the defendant, the court shall order the prosecuting attorney to inquire whether or not a collusion between the parties exists. If there is no collusion, the prosecuting attorney shall intervene for the State in order to take care that the evidence for the plaintiff is not fabricated.
  • Art. 59 is a new provision. The court's reconciliation efforts are not limited to the six months. They may continue after trial and before judgment. The court must not pressure the parties. It works by persuasion, with counsel, so that any reconciliation is free and voluntary (Tolentino).
  • Art. 60 broadened NCC 101: the prosecutor's duty now applies "in any case," not only when the defendant does not appear.
  • Necessity of proof (Tolentino): a mere acknowledgment or confession, such as a husband's admission that he lives with another woman, cannot alone support a decree. There must be other proof, specific as to time, person and place. Circumstantial evidence and proof of adulterous disposition are admissible. One spouse's testimony cannot be used to corroborate the other's to prove the cause. The prosecutor may present his own evidence to oppose a petition he finds dubious or fabricated.
  • No default. If the respondent does not answer, the court cannot declare him in default. It orders the prosecutor to investigate whether there is collusion, and the petitioner must still prove the ground (Pacete v. Carriaga; A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC).
  • No compromise (NCC 2035): there can be no compromise on the validity of a marriage or on legal separation. Earnest efforts at compromise under Art. 151 are not a condition precedent to filing (2011 bar). Do not confuse this with Art. 59, which concerns the court's own reconciliation effort before decreeing.
Ocampo v. Florenciano (107 Phil. 35, February 23, 1960)

The husband discovered his wife's dates with other men and later caught her in the act with another man. She left, and he did not look for her. When he sued for legal separation, she said in a deposition that she was willing to have the legal separation as long as no criminal charge was filed against her. The lower courts dismissed the case for confession of judgment and collusion. The SC reversed. Doctrines: (1) Where there is evidence of the adultery independently of the defendant's statement agreeing to the separation, the decree should be granted. It rests on the plaintiff's evidence, not on the confession. What the law prohibits is a judgment based exclusively (or mainly) on the confession. (2) NCC 101 does not exclude admissions or confessions made outside court as evidence. (3) Collusion may not be inferred from the mere fact that the guilty party confesses, desires the separation and makes no defense. (4) The husband's failure to actively search for his wife and take her home is not condonation or consent. It was not his duty to search for her.

Pacete v. Carriaga (1994)

A wife's action combined legal separation with other relief. The defendants failed to answer, and the trial court declared them in default and granted legal separation on the plaintiff's ex parte evidence. Doctrine: In an action for legal separation, a default order is not legally sanctioned. The law requires the court to order the prosecutor to determine whether collusion exists and to see that the evidence is not fabricated. The State's interest in the marriage forbids deciding it by default. Certiorari lies against such an improper default order. The decree was set aside.

Tolentino v. Villanueva (56 SCRA 1, 1974)

Doctrine: NCC 88 and 101 expressly prohibit a decision in suits for annulment and legal separation based on a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment. In case of the defendant's non-appearance, the court shall order the prosecutor to inquire whether collusion exists and, if there is none, to intervene for the State to prevent fabrication of evidence.

Uribe (recitation, 2018 and 2025): "Respondent admitted guilt and the acts alleged. The court granted the petition. Proper?" Answer: It depends. If the admission is the only basis, it is improper (Art. 60). If there is other evidence supporting the ground, the decree is proper (Ocampo v. Florenciano).

A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC (Rule on Legal Separation, 2003): key points

  • The petition may be filed only by the husband or the wife, within five years from the occurrence of any of the grounds.
  • The petition is verified and states the ground with its specific acts. Copies are served on the Office of the Solicitor General and the public prosecutor.
  • Venue: the Family Court where the petitioner or respondent has been residing for at least six months before filing, or, for a non-resident respondent, where he may be found, at the petitioner's election.
  • As in A.M. No. 02-11-10-SC, no motion to dismiss is allowed except for lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter or the parties. Other grounds are raised as affirmative defenses in the answer.
  • No default. If no answer is filed, the court orders the public prosecutor to investigate whether collusion exists. If there is collusion, the case is dismissed.
  • Pre-trial is mandatory. The trial follows Art. 58 (not before six months from filing, except under RA 9262). The court must make reconciliation efforts (Art. 59).
  • The decree of legal separation issues after the decision becomes final and the required liquidation and registration are made. It is registered in the civil registry and the registries of property.
  • Death of a party before the entry of judgment closes and terminates the case, without prejudice to estate proceedings.

1. Consent (Art. 56(2); NCC 100)

People v. Sensano (58 Phil. 73, 1933)

The husband abandoned his wife, who later lived with another man. After she served her sentence for adultery, the husband did not take her back and told her to live however she wanted. He went to Hawaii, returned after seven years, and charged her again for adultery, apparently to use the conviction as a ground for divorce (then available under Act 2710 upon a conviction for adultery). Doctrine: His conduct, including his long absence despite knowledge, warranted the inference that he had consented to his wife's philandering. He was barred.

People v. Schneckenberger (Schneckenburger) (73 Phil. 413, November 10, 1941)

After seven years of marriage, the spouses executed a document agreeing to live separately with "complete freedom of action in any act and in all concepts." The husband obtained a Mexican divorce and married another woman. The wife charged him with bigamy and concubinage. Doctrine: The agreement, though illegal for its purpose, is nevertheless a valid consent to the act of concubinage under RPC 344. "Pardon" refers to the offense after its commission, while "consent" refers to the offense before its commission. No logical difference exists between prior and subsequent consent, because in both the offended party has chosen to compromise with his or her dishonor and becomes unworthy to invoke the court's aid. Prior consent is as effective as subsequent consent. (The narrower view in People v. Guinucod, that only consent given after the crime counts, was rejected.) The husband was acquitted of concubinage but convicted of bigamy. B29's summary of the doctrine: where there is consent (or condonation) in writing, no legal separation on the ground of adultery or concubinage may be granted.

Matubis v. Praxedes (supra)

The written agreement to separate and not to prosecute each other amounted to consent or condonation, which barred legal separation.

Tolentino: Consent may be express or implied and must be freely given. The corrupt consent of a spouse to an offense by the other bars a suit for that offense. A husband who refuses his young wife a home with him, knowing her inability to resist temptation, and leaves her under tempting circumstances, will not be heard to complain if she falls. A wife who leaves her husband knowing he would seek other women, and who was satisfied while he was with her, has consented.
Uribe: "Distinguish consent from condonation." Consent concerns future acts. Condonation concerns acts already committed.

2. Condonation (Art. 56(1))

Bugayong v. Ginez (100 Phil. 616, December 28, 1956)

A US Navy serviceman married Leonila Ginez. While he was on duty, he received letters about her infidelity. He went home, sought her out, and they had sexual intercourse on two nights. He then confronted her, she left, and he sued for legal separation. Doctrine: Condonation is the forgiveness of a marital offense constituting a ground for legal separation. It is implied from sexual intercourse after knowledge of the other's infidelity, since such acts necessarily imply forgiveness. Under American jurisprudence, any cohabitation with the guilty party after the offense, with knowledge or belief of its commission, is conclusive evidence of condonation, though the presumption may be rebutted. A single voluntary act of marital intercourse after discovery of the offense is ordinarily sufficient, especially as against the husband. Where the parties live in the same house, they are presumed to live on terms of matrimonial cohabitation. The complaint was dismissed.

Tolentino: Condonation is always on the condition, implied by law if not expressed, that the wrongdoer will not again commit the offense. A breach of the condition revives the offense as a ground. Condonation may be express (words or writing) or implied (acts). A mere offer of reconciliation by the wronged spouse may constitute condonation. The intent to pardon, based on knowledge of the offense, must be clear. A wife who had intercourse with her husband after finding a hotel bill but without clear knowledge of his infidelity did not condone it. A wife who, for the children's sake and in an effort at reconciliation, cohabited with her husband for one night in a hotel did not condone (Hawkins v. Hawkins).
Uribe (recitation): "If a spouse, having knowledge of the ground (sexual infidelity), had sexual relations with the guilty spouse (like Bugayong), is it possible that this will not amount to condonation?" Answer: Yes, it is possible. The sex may have been an effort to save the marriage. Bugayong says "ordinarily," which leaves room for exceptions. Also: "To constitute condonation, you should know what was done." Knowledge is essential.

After the wedding, Nikki discovered Christian's affair. She decided to give the marriage a try and lived with him for two years, then sued for legal separation on the ground of sexual infidelity. Will it prosper? (2012 bar)

Answer: No, as to the affair she knew of. Living with him for two years with knowledge amounts to implied condonation (Art. 56(1); Bugayong). The action has not prescribed, since five years have not run. However, if the affair continued after the condonation, each later act is a new ground that was not condoned, so the action would prosper as to those acts.

3. Connivance (Art. 56(3))

Tolentino: Consent is unilateral, the act of only one spouse. Connivance implies agreement, express or implied, by both spouses to the ground. Test: Merely suffering a wife whom he suspects to avail herself of an opportunity she arranged, without his knowledge, to indulge her adulterous disposition is not connivance, even if he hopes to obtain proof and refrains from warning her. He may watch her, but he must not make opportunities for her or smooth her path. So a husband who pretended to go away for the night but returned and caught his wife in adultery was not guilty of connivance. But if he actively participates by providing the opportunity, directly or through an agent, even one not authorized to do so, he connives. Examples are a husband whose detective himself brought about the adultery, and a wife whose detectives hired a woman to lure the husband into concubinage.

H suspects W of infidelity and hires a detective, who on his own initiative seduces W. H sues for legal separation. Ruling?

Answer: Deny on the ground of connivance (Art. 56(3)). H is chargeable with his agent's act of bringing about the misconduct, even though he did not authorize it (Tolentino).

4. Recrimination or mutual guilt (Art. 56(4))

Recrimination is a counter-charge in a suit for legal separation that the complainant is also guilty of an offense constituting a ground for legal separation. It rests on the principle that one must come to court with clean hands. It does not matter who committed the first offense or who is more guilty (Tolentino).

Uribe (CivRev 2025): "RECRIMINATION means a counter-charge in a suit for legal separation that the complainant is also guilty of an offense constituting a ground for legal separation. Is it required that they are similar acts? NO." The husband's infidelity may be set up against the wife's attempt on his life.
Brown v. Yambao (102 Phil. 168, October 18, 1957)

William Brown learned of his wife's adultery in 1945, upon his release from internment. In 1955 he sued for legal separation. The wife was declared in default. The prosecutor, on cross-examination, elicited that Brown himself had lived maritally with another woman and had children by her. The court dismissed. Doctrines: (1) Role of the prosecutor: the fiscal is not limited to checking collusion. He may inquire into any relevant matter showing whether the separation is fully justified. (2) Collusion in matrimonial cases is "the act of married persons in procuring a divorce by mutual consent, whether by preconcerted commission by one of a matrimonial offense, or by failure, in pursuance of agreement, to defend divorce proceedings." (3) Recrimination: the husband, being himself guilty, could not obtain legal separation (NCC 100). (4) Prescription: the action was filed about ten years after he learned of the adultery, beyond NCC 102's one-year-from-knowledge and five-year-from-occurrence periods. (5) Because the State is interested in preserving marriage, these defenses may be considered motu proprio even if not pleaded, as when the defendant is in default. No decree should issue if any legal obstacle appears on the record.

Saul, married to Cecile, had an adulterous relation with Tessie. Cecile caught them in flagrante and, in a fit of extreme jealousy, shot Saul, nearly killing him. Four years later Saul sued for legal separation, alleging attempt on his life. (a) Saul's argument? (b) Cecile's defense? (c) Decide. (2006 bar)

Answer: (a) Saul: an attempt by the respondent against the petitioner's life is a ground (Art. 55(9)). No criminal conviction is required, and the action was filed within five years (Art. 57). (b) Cecile: recrimination or mutual guilt. Saul himself committed sexual infidelity (Art. 55(8)), and the petition must be denied "where both parties have given ground" (Art. 56(4)). She may add that her act, impelled by passion upon catching him in flagrante, is not the wrongful, intentional attempt contemplated (Tolentino; cf. RPC 247). (c) Dismiss for mutual guilt. Marriage is an inviolable social institution, and no decree should issue when a legal obstacle appears on record (Brown v. Yambao).

Quiz trap: "Where both parties have given ground for legal separation, the petition shall be denied" is TRUE (verbatim Art. 56(4)). Under NCC 100: "Where both spouses are offenders, a legal separation cannot be claimed by either of them." Any change such as "the court shall grant the decree to the less guilty spouse" is FALSE.

5. Collusion (Art. 56(5); Art. 60; compare NCC 101, 221(3))

Collusion is an agreement between husband and wife for one of them to commit, or to appear to have committed, a matrimonial offense, or to suppress evidence of a valid defense, so that the other can obtain legal separation. It may be implied if not express (Tolentino). Collusion is void (NCC 221(3)). A decree obtained through collusion that the court was unaware of is void, because it is a fraud on the court and contrary to public policy (Tolentino).

  • A mere confession, a desire for the separation and failure to defend do not by themselves prove collusion (Ocampo v. Florenciano).
  • Collusion differs from connivance. Connivance concerns the commission of the offense or act. Collusion concerns obtaining the decree.
  • Whether one spouse gave the other her share of the conjugal property so she would not oppose is of no consequence in determining collusion (Republic v. CA, a nullity case cited in B29).

6. Prescription (Art. 56(6); Art. 57; cf. NCC 102, Brown v. Yambao)

Five years from occurrence (see D above). It may be noticed by the court motu proprio (Brown).

Other bars

  • Death of either party before the decree becomes final abates the action (Lapuz-Sy; Tolentino citing Macadangdang).
  • Reconciliation during pendency terminates the proceedings at whatever stage (Art. 66(1)).

H. Effects of the decree of legal separation (Art. 63; NCC 106, 372; Arts. 198, 213)

Art. 63, FC. The decree of legal separation shall have the following effects:
(1) The spouses shall be entitled to live separately from each other, but the marriage bonds shall not be severed;
(2) The absolute community or the conjugal partnership shall be dissolved and liquidated but the offending spouse shall have no right to any share of the net profits earned by the absolute community or the conjugal partnership, which shall be forfeited in accordance with the provisions of Article 43(2);
(3) The custody of the minor children shall be awarded to the innocent spouse, subject to the provisions of Article 213 of this Code; and
(4) The offending spouse shall be disqualified from inheriting from the innocent spouse by intestate succession. Moreover, provisions in favor of the offending spouse made in the will of the innocent spouse shall be revoked by operation of law. (106a)

1. Personal relations (Arts. 61, 63(1))

  • The spouses may live separately, but they remain married. Neither can remarry. A subsequent marriage is bigamous and void, and it is punishable as bigamy.
  • Fidelity continues. Adultery or concubinage after the decree is still a crime, and its only sanction is penal (Tolentino).
  • The obligation of mutual support ceases. The court may order the guilty spouse to support the innocent one (Art. 198). Joint life and mutual help are discontinued.
Uribe (Quiz 2 discussion, 2025): A legally separated spouse who remarries contracts a bigamous, void marriage. This is not because presumptive legitimes were not delivered (Arts. 52–53 concern nullity and annulment). It is because "NO ONE DIED in legal separation. The marital bond is not severed." Art. 41 is the only case of a valid bigamous marriage.
Uribe (CivRev 2025): "After the decree of legal sep, is the spouse compelled to give support?" It depends. Support presupposes capacity, and there must be a court order directing the guilty spouse to support the innocent spouse (Art. 198). In annulment, the court cannot order the former spouses to support each other because they are no longer married.

2. Custody of children (Art. 63(3); Art. 213; NCC 106(3); PD 603 Art. 17(3))

Art. 213, FC. In case of separation of the parents, parental authority shall be exercised by the parent designated by the Court. The Court shall take into account all relevant considerations, especially the choice of the child over seven years of age, unless the parent chosen is unfit.
No child under seven years of age shall be separated from the mother unless the court finds compelling reasons to order otherwise.
Art. 106(3), NCC (old law). The custody of the minor children shall be awarded to the innocent spouse, unless otherwise directed by the court in the interest of said minors, for whom said court may appoint a guardian;
Art. 17(3), PD 603 (old rule). In case of separation of his parents, no child under five years of age shall be separated from his mother unless the court finds compelling reasons to do so.
Note: PD 603 said five years. FC 213 now says seven. Some reviewers still repeat "below age 5." Answer seven under the FC. The award to the innocent spouse is "subject to" Art. 213. A mother's sexual preference or moral laxity is not by itself a compelling reason to take a child under seven from her. It must be shown that her conduct affected the child's welfare (Pablo-Gualberto v. Gualberto, 2005). A parent adjudged guilty keeps a natural right of access to the child under court regulation, unless forfeited by conduct or injurious to the child (Tolentino).
Matute v. Macadaeg (99 Phil. 340, May 30, 1956)

Custody of the minor children had been awarded to the husband. The wife obtained his permission to bring the children to Manila for their maternal grandfather's funeral and then kept them. Doctrine (Uribe outline): Having obtained physical possession only by the husband's permission, she held it precariously, in his name and as his agent. He may demand their return at any time, and she must comply immediately. She cannot question his authority to demand it, though she is free to seek review of the custody order or ask that the children be placed with her. B29 adds that moral depravity, habitual drunkenness, incapacity or poverty may render a mother unfit or make it unwise to place the children with her.

3. Property relations (Art. 63(2); Art. 43(2); Arts. 99(2), 126(2), 102, 129)

Art. 43(2), FC (incorporated by Art. 63(2)). The absolute community of property or the conjugal partnership, as the case may be, shall be dissolved and liquidated, but if either spouse contracted said marriage in bad faith, his or her share of the net profits of the community property or conjugal partnership property shall be forfeited in favor of the common children or, if there are none, the children of the guilty spouse by a previous marriage or in default of children, the innocent spouse;
  • The ACP terminates "when there is a decree of legal separation" (Art. 99(2)), and so does the CPG (Art. 126(2)). Legal separation dissolves the regime but not the marriage. Thereafter the spouses are under complete separation of property.
  • The offending spouse forfeits his or her share of the net profits. The forfeiture goes, in this order, to (1) the common children; (2) if none, the children of the guilty spouse by a previous marriage; (3) in default of children, the innocent spouse. The offending spouse keeps his or her own share of the capital (the separate properties and his or her share of the community assets other than net profits).
  • Net profits (Art. 102(4)) are the increase in value between the market value of the community property at the time of celebration and its market value at dissolution. For the CPG, see Art. 129(7).
  • Dissolution and liquidation follow ipso facto from the final decree. A decree that does not yet order the division of property is not an incomplete judgment. It becomes final if not appealed, and a later supplemental decision on division is a mere incident (Tolentino).
  • Quiao v. Quiao (2012): the offending spouse's share in the net profits of the CPG was forfeited in favor of the common children. Applying the FC to a marriage celebrated before it took effect did not impair vested rights, because due process was observed.
  • Litigation costs: ACP/CPG are liable for "expenses of litigation between the spouses unless the suit is found to be groundless" (FC 94(10); 121(9)).

4. Support (Art. 198)

Mutual support ceases after final judgment. The court may order the guilty spouse to support the innocent one. In annulment and nullity, no such order is possible.

Quiz trap (two versions): "The obligation of mutual support between spouses ceases after the following, except: death / nullity / annulment / legal separation / none" gives none of the above, because mutual support ceases in all four. "The obligation of supporting a spouse ceases after the following, except" gives legal separation, because the guilty spouse may still be ordered to support the innocent spouse. Read the exact wording.

5. Use of surname (NCC 370–372)

Art. 372, NCC. When legal separation has been granted, the wife shall continue using her name and surname employed before the legal separation.
Laperal v. Republic (116 Phil. 672)

A legally separated wife petitioned to resume her maiden name. Doctrine: The statutory language is mandatory. Even after legal separation, the wife shall continue using the name and surname she used before the legal separation. Her married status is unaffected because the vinculum is not severed, and the policy is that she keep the name indicative of her unchanged status for the benefit of all concerned.

Uribe (CivRev 2025): "With a decree of legal sep, is the wife required to revert to her maiden name, or can she revert?" No, because the marriage is still valid and the ties are not severed. "Same answer if she is the innocent spouse?" It does not matter whether she is innocent or guilty. Contrast NCC 371 (annulment): a guilty wife shall resume her maiden name, and an innocent wife may resume it or keep the husband's surname unless the court decrees otherwise or either remarries.

6. Right to inherit (Art. 63(4); NCC 921(4), 1002)

  • Intestate succession: the offending spouse is disqualified from inheriting from the innocent spouse, including the legitime (Tolentino: "She would not even be entitled to the legitime").
  • Testamentary provisions in the offender's favor in the innocent spouse's will are revoked by operation of law. This applies to wills executed before the decree. A will executed by the innocent spouse after the decree that favors the offender is valid, because it is the testator's later intent (Tolentino).
  • The disqualification is one-directional. The innocent spouse is not disqualified from inheriting from the offending spouse.
  • NCC 1002: in case of legal separation, if the surviving spouse gave cause for the separation, he or she shall not have any of the successional rights of a surviving spouse.
  • Disinheritance (NCC 921(4)): a spouse may be disinherited by will "when the spouse has given cause for legal separation." No decree is required. In contrast, Art. 63(4) disqualification requires a decree. A wife convicted of adultery is not disqualified from intestate succession if there is no decree of legal separation.
Quiz traps: "…disqualified from inheriting from the innocent spouse by intestate and testamentary succession" is FALSE (the "effects, except" answer). "…disqualified … by intestate succession if he gave cause for legal separation" is FALSE, because the decree is required. "A decree is required for disinheritance of a spouse who gave cause" is FALSE (NCC 921(4)). "…the offending spouse shall have a share of the net profits" is FALSE.

During the pendency of the petition, the innocent spouse A died. A's will gives a condominium unit to the guilty spouse B. Does B get it? What if there had been a final decree? What could A's counsel have advised?

Answer: (1) If A died pending the case, the action abates (Lapuz-Sy). There is no decree, so Art. 63(4) never operates and B takes under the will (and as compulsory heir). (2) If a final decree existed and the will was made before it, the provision is revoked by operation of law (Art. 63(4)). If A made the will after the decree, the provision is valid. (3) Counsel should have advised A to revoke the provision, or to disinherit B in a will under NCC 921(4) ("has given cause for legal separation"). For disinheritance, no decree of legal separation is required (Uribe recitation: "Is it a requirement for disinheritance to be valid that there be a decree? No."). (4) Uribe's follow-up: is there condonation? A will in the offender's favor made after the decree has become final is the clear case. A spouse may still pursue the petition, for example to end the CPG, even if she is not otherwise hostile. Condonation needs knowledge and intent to forgive.

7. Revocation of donations and insurance designation (Art. 64)

Art. 64, FC. After the finality of the decree of legal separation, the innocent spouse may revoke the donations made by him or by her in favor of the offending spouse, as well as the designation of the latter as beneficiary in any insurance policy, even if such designation be stipulated as irrevocable. The revocation of the donations shall be recorded in the registries of property in the places where the properties are located. Alienations, liens and encumbrances registered in good faith before the recording of the complaint for revocation in the registries of property shall be respected. The revocation of or change in the designation of the insurance beneficiary shall take effect upon written notification thereof to the insured.
The action to revoke the donation under this Article must be brought within five years from the time the decree of legal separation has become final. (107a)
  • When: after the finality of the decree.
  • Who: the innocent spouse, and only as to donations made by him or her.
  • Optional ("may"): revocation is not ipso jure. If the innocent spouse dies before exercising the right, the donations subsist (Tolentino).
  • Insurance: the designation may be revoked "even if such designation be stipulated as irrevocable." It takes effect upon written notification to the insured, not upon finality.
  • Donations: the revocation is recorded. Good-faith alienations, liens and encumbrances registered before the recording of the complaint for revocation are respected.
  • Period: 5 years from the time the decree became final, for the action to revoke the donation.
  • Cf. FC 86(3): a donation by reason of marriage may be revoked by the donor "upon legal separation, the donee being the guilty spouse."

I. Reconciliation (Arts. 65–67; compare NCC 108, 195)

Art. 65, FC. If the spouses should reconcile, a corresponding joint manifestation under oath duly signed by them shall be filed with the court in the same proceeding for legal separation. (n)
Art. 66, FC. The reconciliation referred to in the preceding Article shall have the following consequences:
(1) The legal separation proceedings, if still pending, shall thereby be terminated at whatever stage; and
(2) The final decree of legal separation shall be set aside, but the separation of property and any forfeiture of the share of the guilty spouse already effected shall subsist, unless the spouses agree to revive their former property regime.
The court's order containing the foregoing shall be recorded in the proper civil registries. (108a)
Art. 67, FC. The agreement to revive the former property regime referred to in the preceding Article shall be executed under oath and shall specify:
(1) The properties to be contributed anew to the restored regime;
(2) Those to be retained as separated properties of each spouse; and
(3) The names of all their known creditors, their addresses and the amounts owing to each.
The agreement of revival and the motion for its approval shall be filed with the court in the same proceeding for legal separation, with copies of both furnished to the creditors named therein. After due hearing, the court shall, in its order, take measures to protect the interest of creditors and such order shall be recorded in the proper registries of properties.
The recording of the order in the registries of property shall not prejudice any creditor not listed or not notified, unless the debtor-spouse has sufficient separate properties to satisfy the creditor's claim. (195a, 108a)

How reconciliation is done (Art. 65)

  • It requires a joint manifestation (not unilateral), made under oath, duly signed by both, and filed in the same proceeding (not a new petition).
  • Reconciliation is a mutual, voluntary agreement to live together again as husband and wife. The plaintiff cannot compel the defendant to resume conjugal life. Under the NCC it could be in any form, express or tacit. Under the FC, a joint sworn manifestation is required. Court approval of the reconciliation itself is not required (Tolentino).
Note (two views on what produces the effects): Tolentino submits that resuming common life is the essence of reconciliation and terminates the legal separation even if no joint manifestation is filed. On this view, filing is needed only to formally end the case and for future property relations. The other view (2013 reviewer in the transcripts) is that "it is not the reconciliation which produces the effects in Art. 66 but the filing of the joint manifestation." For a codal T/F, follow the text: the effects attach to "the reconciliation referred to in the preceding Article," i.e., the one manifested under Art. 65.

They may proceed against the properties of the restored regime, unless the debtor-spouse has enough separate property to pay them. More worked examples (Uribe style)

Spouses A and B. A petitions for legal separation because B was found guilty of a crime. B's defense: he was not imprisoned because he availed of probation. Will the petition prosper?

Answer: No. Art. 55(4) requires a final judgment sentencing the respondent to imprisonment of more than six years. Probation is unavailable to those sentenced to more than six years, so B's grant of probation shows his sentence did not exceed six years. There is no ground. (If the sentence had exceeded six years, a later pardon would not remove the ground: "even if pardoned.")

Rosa and Ariel married in church in 1988. In 1990 Ariel went to Saudi Arabia, converted to Islam and married Mystica. Rosa learned of it on January 1, 1992 and sued for legal separation on February 5, 1994. (a) Ground? (b) Prescribed? (1994 bar)

Answer: (a) Yes. Ariel contracted a subsequent bigamous marriage abroad (Art. 55(7), "whether in the Philippines or abroad"), and he also abandoned Rosa without justifiable cause for more than one year (Art. 55(10)). His conversion does not authorize a second marriage while the civil/church marriage subsists. (b) No. Five years run from the occurrence (Art. 57). The second marriage could not have occurred before 1990, so Rosa had until at least 1995. (The bar answer notes that if Rosa agreed on his return to cohabit with him, there could be condonation.)

The wife filed for legal separation on the ground of sexual infidelity without first exerting earnest efforts toward a compromise. The judge dismissed the case for failure to comply with a condition precedent. Proper? (2011 bar)

Answer: No. Legal separation, like the validity of a marriage, cannot be the subject of a compromise (NCC 2035), so Art. 151's earnest-efforts requirement does not apply.

"No decree of legal separation can be issued: (A) unless the children's welfare is attended to first; (B) without prior efforts at reconciliation shown to be futile; (C) unless the court first directs mediation; (D) without prior investigation by a public prosecutor." (2011 bar)

Answer: (B), Art. 59. (D) misreads Art. 60: the prosecutor's role is to prevent collusion and fabrication or suppression of evidence.

H's wife W, in self-defense against H's unlawful attack, stabbed him seriously. H sues for legal separation under Art. 55(9).

Answer: Deny. The attempt against life must be wrongful and with intent to kill. A justified act such as self-defense is not a ground (Tolentino). If H's attacks were repeated, W herself may sue under Art. 55(1), and her leaving the home is justified (Ong Eng Kiam v. Ong).

H and W both agreed to, and both took part in, "swinging" sexual practices. Later W sued H for legal separation for sexual perversion and infidelity.

Answer: Deny. Perversion requires coercion by the respondent. By free mutual agreement, both are equally guilty (Tolentino). There is also consent or connivance (Art. 56(2)–(3)) and mutual guilt (Art. 56(4)).

H filed for legal separation against W (sexual infidelity). W did not answer. The court declared her in default, received H's evidence ex parte, and decreed legal separation four months after filing.

Answer: The decree is void or reversible on two counts. (1) Default is not allowed in legal separation. The court must order the prosecutor to investigate collusion and ensure the evidence is not fabricated (Art. 60; Pacete v. Carriaga; A.M. No. 02-11-11-SC). (2) Trial within six months of filing violates Art. 58 (unless RA 9262 violence is alleged, which is not the case where the husband is the petitioner).

A decree of legal separation became final in 2020. In 2023, the spouses reconciled and filed a joint sworn manifestation. Their regime had been ACP. What is their property regime now?

Answer: The decree is set aside (Art. 66(2)), but the separation of property and the forfeiture of the guilty spouse's net profits subsist. They remain under separation of property unless they execute a sworn agreement to revive (specifying the properties contributed anew, those retained, and all known creditors with addresses and amounts). It must be filed with a motion for approval in the same proceeding, with copies to the creditors, and approved by the court after hearing (Art. 67). Tolentino: they may even agree on a different regime.

During the pendency of a legal separation case, W (petitioner) asks the court to let her administer the CPG, alleging that H is selling conjugal lots. There is no written agreement. Two months have passed since filing.

Answer: The court may act now. Art. 58 bars only trial on the merits. Under Art. 61, absent a written agreement, the court shall designate either spouse or a third person as administrator, with the powers of a guardian, and may enjoin dispositions (De la Viña; Somosa-Ramos; Sabalones).

Last-minute checklist

  • Legal separation is a relative divorce (a mensa et thoro): separation from bed and board, with the bond intact. Absolute divorce is a vinculo matrimonii.
  • History: Partidas (relative only), then Act 2710 (Mar. 11, 1917) (absolute: wife's adultery or husband's concubinage, with conviction), then E.O. 141 (Japanese period, more grounds), then the Oct. 23, 1944 MacArthur proclamation (Act 2710 revived), then the NCC (legal separation only, 2 grounds), then the FC (10 grounds). Muslims: RA 394, then PD 1083 (Feb. 4, 1977).
  • Art. 55 has 10 grounds. "Child" includes a child by nature or adoption, and only pars. (1) and (3) cover children: "petitioner, a common child, or a child of the petitioner."
  • (1) repeated violence or grossly abusive conduct; (2) violence or moral pressure to change religious or political affiliation (single act is enough); (3) attempt of respondent to corrupt or induce to prostitution, or connivance; (4) final judgment, more than six years, even if pardoned; (5) drug addiction or habitual alcoholism; (6) lesbianism or homosexuality; (7) subsequent bigamous marriage, PH or abroad (valid or void, doesn't matter); (8) sexual infidelity or perversion; (9) attempt against the life of the petitioner (intent to kill, no conviction needed); (10) abandonment without justifiable cause for more than one year.
  • No criminal conviction is needed (Gandionco: preponderance). The civil case may go ahead of the criminal one.
  • Probation means the sentence was 6 years or less, so there is no 55(4) ground.
  • Art. 101: 3 months gives a prima facie presumption of abandonment (for separation of property). Art. 55(10): more than 1 year. Art. 135(6): separated in fact at least 1 year.
  • Only the innocent spouse may file. The action is purely personal and dies with either party, even after the decree but before finality (Lapuz-Sy; Macadangdang). The FC has no NCC 99 one-year residence rule.
  • Art. 57: 5 years from occurrence. NCC 102 (1 year from knowledge and 5 years from occurrence) is gone. Each act has its own period.
  • Art. 58: not tried before 6 months from filing. Incidents (support, custody, administration, injunction) may be heard (Araneta; Somosa-Ramos). RA 9262 Sec. 19 is the exception. It applies only to legal separation.
  • Art. 59: no decree unless the court has tried reconciliation and is satisfied it is highly improbable.
  • Art. 60: no decree on a stipulation of facts or confession of judgment. The prosecutor prevents collusion and fabrication or suppression "in any case." There is no default (Pacete). A confession plus independent evidence is OK (Ocampo).
  • Art. 61: live separately after filing. Absent a written agreement, the court designates either spouse or a third person as administrator, with the powers of a guardian.
  • Art. 62 applies Art. 49 (support of the spouses; custody and support of the common children; visitation). Art. 198: support comes from the ACP/CPG during the case.
  • Art. 56 defenses: condonation (after, forgiveness, express or implied; one voluntary intercourse ordinarily suffices, per Bugayong; conditional, so a new offense revives the old); consent (before, unilateral; prior consent is valid, per Schneckenberger; implied, per Sensano); connivance (both, active participation, including through an agent); recrimination (both gave grounds; similar acts not required; clean hands); collusion (agreement to obtain the decree; the decree is void); prescription (5 years). Also death and reconciliation.
  • Brown v. Yambao: the prosecutor may probe anything relevant; recrimination and prescription may be noticed motu proprio; definition of collusion.
  • Art. 63: (1) live separately, bond not severed, fidelity continues; (2) ACP/CPG dissolved, and the offender has no share of net profits, forfeited per 43(2) to the common children, then the guilty spouse's children by a prior marriage, then the innocent spouse; (3) custody to the innocent spouse, subject to Art. 213 (under 7 stays with the mother absent compelling reasons); (4) offender disqualified by intestate succession, and the will provisions are revoked by operation of law (a will made after the decree is valid).
  • NCC 921(4): disinheritance for "given cause" needs no decree. Art. 63(4) needs a decree.
  • Art. 64: after finality, the innocent spouse may revoke donations (action within 5 years from finality; recorded; prior good-faith registered encumbrances respected) and the insurance designation (even if irrevocable; effective upon written notification to the insured).
  • NCC 372: the wife continues using her married name, innocent or guilty (Laperal). NCC 371 (annulment): a guilty wife shall resume her maiden name, and an innocent wife may.
  • Art. 198: mutual support ceases. The court may order the guilty spouse to support the innocent spouse. Know the "mutual support" item (answer: none of the above) versus the "supporting a spouse" item (answer: legal separation).
  • Art. 65: a joint manifestation under oath, duly signed by both, filed in the same proceeding. No court approval of the reconciliation is needed.
  • Art. 66: if pending, the case is terminated at whatever stage. If there is a final decree, it is set aside, but the separation of property and forfeiture subsist unless the spouses agree to revive. The order is recorded in the civil registries.
  • Art. 67: revival agreement under oath, listing (1) the properties contributed anew, (2) the properties retained, (3) all known creditors with addresses and amounts. It is filed with a motion for approval in the same proceeding, with copies to the creditors. The court approves after hearing and protects the creditors. Unlisted or unnotified creditors are not prejudiced unless the debtor-spouse has sufficient separate property. Under the NCC, revival was automatic. Under the FC, it is not.
  • A remarriage by a legally separated spouse is bigamous and void, because the bond subsists.
  • PD 1083 faskh: support neglect for 6 consecutive months; imprisonment for at least 1 year. An irrevocable talaq or faskh severs the bond.
  • Art. 26(2): a mixed marriage plus a valid foreign divorce capacitating the alien lets the Filipino remarry (Orbecido: citizenship at the time of divorce; Manalo: whoever initiated it). Divorce between Filipinos is not recognized (Tenchavez; Garcia v. Recio).
