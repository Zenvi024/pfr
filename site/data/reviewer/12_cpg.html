Conjugal Partnership of Gains

Title IV, Chapter 4, Family Code, Arts. 105–133 (syllabus X.E). Covers when the CPG applies (Arts. 105–108), exclusive property and its administration (Arts. 109–115), the presumption and what is conjugal (Arts. 116–120), charges (Arts. 121–123), administration (Arts. 124–125), dissolution (Art. 126), separation in fact and abandonment (Arts. 127–128), liquidation (Arts. 129–133), and the syllabus cases decided mostly under the Civil Code (NCC 142–189).

The CPG in one paragraph, and how it differs from the ACP

In the CPG, each spouse keeps what he or she owned before the marriage and what he or she later receives gratuitously. What goes into the common fund is the gains: the fruits and income of everything (including the exclusive properties), the products of each spouse's work, and acquisitions by onerous title at the common fund's expense. Upon dissolution, the net gains are divided equally (Art. 106). The ACP is the opposite starting point: everything is common, with a short list of exclusions (Arts. 91–92).

Arts. 105–108: when the CPG applies; nature; suppletory rules

Art. 105, FC. In case the future spouses agree in the marriage settlements that the regime of conjugal partnership gains shall govern their property relations during marriage, the provisions in this Chapter shall be of supplementary application.
The provisions of this Chapter shall also apply to conjugal partnerships of gains already established between spouses before the effectivity of this Code, without prejudice to vested rights already acquired in accordance with the Civil Code or other laws, as provided in Article 256. (n)
Art. 106, FC. Under the regime of conjugal partnership of gains, the husband and wife place in a common fund the proceeds, products, fruits and income from their separate properties and those acquired by either or both spouses through their efforts or by chance, and, upon dissolution of the marriage or of the partnership, the net gains or benefits obtained by either or both spouses shall be divided equally between them, unless otherwise agreed in the marriage settlements. (142a)
Art. 107, FC. The rules provided in Articles 88 and 89 shall also apply to conjugal partnership of gains. (n)
Art. 108, FC. The conjugal partnership shall be governed by the rules on the contract of partnership in all that is not in conflict with what is expressly determined in this Chapter or by the spouses in their marriage settlements. (147a)
  • Art. 105: two situations, (1) chosen in a settlement under the Family Code, and (2) pre-Family Code CPGs, to which the Family Code chapter applies without prejudice to vested rights.
  • Art. 107: the CPG, like the ACP, commences at the precise moment of the celebration (any contrary stipulation is void), and no waiver is allowed during the marriage except upon judicial separation of property. UP BOC 2011 MCQ: "When does the CPG begin to exist?" A: at the moment the parties take and declare each other as husband and wife.
  • Art. 108: suppletory rules are those on the contract of partnership, not co-ownership.
Quiz traps: (1) "The conjugal partnership shall be governed by the rules of co-ownership in all that is not in conflict with what is expressly determined in this Chapter or by the spouses in their marriage settlements" is False (Quiz comp TF8). (2) "The conjugal partnership shall be governed by the rules on the contract of partnership" is graded False in the Quiz #3 pair-5 variant (key B), because it drops the qualifier "in all that is not in conflict with what is expressly determined in this Chapter or by the spouses in their marriage settlements".
PNB v. Quintos (46 Phil. 370)

"The conjugal partnership is liable for obligations contracted during marriage, and in default of conjugal property, the spouses are jointly liable therefor with their private properties, unless by express stipulation, they shall have bound themselves solidarily." This is the old rule, applying partnership principles (Art. 108). The Family Code now makes the spouses solidarily liable for the unpaid balance (Art. 121, last par.; Art. 129(4)).

Quiz trap (Quiz comp MCQ 17; Civ Rev samplex): "The conjugal partnership shall be liable for all debts and obligations contracted during the marriage by the designated administrator-spouse for the benefit of the conjugal partnership of gains. In case of insufficiency of said assets, the spouses shall be liable: (a) solidarily; (b) jointly; (c) alternatively; (d) cumulatively." A (Art. 121, last paragraph). "Jointly" is the PNB v. Quintos rule under the old law.

Art. 109: exclusive property of each spouse

Art. 109, FC. The following shall be the exclusive property of each spouse:
(1) That which is brought to the marriage as his or her own;
(2) That which each acquires during the marriage by gratuitous title;
(3) That which is acquired by right of redemption, by barter or by exchange with property belonging to only one of the spouses; and
(4) That which is purchased with exclusive money of the wife or of the husband. (148a)

(1) Brought to the marriage

Everything owned at the moment of the celebration, however acquired. Under the CPG, this is decisive in the "inherited at 10 a.m., married at 5 p.m." problems (situation 9).

Laperal v. Katigbak (10 SCRA 493)

"There is no denying that all properties acquired during the marriage are, by law, presumed conjugal. The presumption, however, is not conclusive but merely rebuttable, for the same law is unequivocal that it exists only 'unless it be proved that it (the property) belongs exclusively to the husband or the wife.'" The Court held that the presumption had been "sufficiently and convincingly disproven".

Berciles v. GSIS (128 SCRA 53)

Retirement premiums totaling ₱9,700 were presumed conjugal, "there being no proof that the premiums were paid from the exclusive funds of the deceased Judge" (NCC 160). One-half belongs to the wife as her share in the partnership, and the other half to the judge's estate for his heirs.

(2) Gratuitous title during the marriage

Art. 113, FC. Property donated or left by will to the spouses, jointly and with designation of determinate shares, shall pertain to the donee-spouses as his or her own exclusive property, and in the absence of designation, share and share alike, without prejudice to the right of accretion when proper. (150a)
Art. 114, FC. If the donations are onerous, the amount of the charges shall be borne by the exclusive property of the donee spouse, whenever they have been advanced by the conjugal partnership of gains. (151a)
Art. 115, FC. Retirement benefits, pensions, annuities, gratuities, usufructs and similar benefits shall be governed by the rules on gratuitous or onerous acquisitions as may be proper in each case. (n)
Quiz trap (CivRev midterms #30): "In a CPG, without a marriage settlement, this property shall NOT form part of the conjugal partnership properties: (a) the rentals from the exclusive property of a spouse; (b) a property acquired by gratuitous title during the marriage; (c) a property acquired by onerous title during the marriage; (d) a property acquired by onerous title before the marriage; (e) none of the above." A student answered B. Sir: "Not necessarily. It may form part of the conjugal properties … because the testator may have expressly provided" that it go to the partnership. "Shall NOT form part" is absolute, so B fails. D is the answer: property acquired before the marriage is brought to it as one's own (Art. 109(1)), whatever the title.
Veloso v. Martinez (28 Phil. 255)

The wife's jewels, which she inherited from her mother, were her paraphernal property. She had kept their exclusive control and management and had not delivered them to her husband to administer. Her husband disposed of them, and Veloso claimed to have bought them without her consent. Held: she may recover them. As paraphernal property not delivered to the husband for administration, they could not be disposed of by any act of his without her consent.

(3) Redemption, barter or exchange

Plata v. Yatco (12 SCRA 718)

"The subsequent conveyance of paraphernal property does not transform it from paraphernal to conjugal property, there being no proof that the money paid came from common or conjugal funds."

Lim v. Garcia (7 Phil. 320)

"One claiming that certain property is the separate estate of the husband, brought by him to his marriage, must establish that fact by evidence sufficient to overcome the presumption … that all the property of the spouses is conjugal partnership property (bienes gananciales)."

(4) Purchased with exclusive money

Property bought during the marriage with one spouse's own money is that spouse's exclusive property: real subrogation. UP BOC 2011 MCQ: Karen inherited ₱5M before marrying Karl and later used it to buy a Mercedes-Benz in her name, which the couple used as the family car. The car is D: Karen's exclusive property since she bought it with her own money, despite family use and the purchase during the marriage. The source of funds must be proved, because the Art. 116 presumption otherwise applies.

Arts. 110–112: administration and disposition of exclusive property

Art. 110, FC. The spouses retain the ownership, possession, administration and enjoyment of their exclusive properties.
Either spouse may, during the marriage, transfer the administration of his or her exclusive property to the other by means of a public instrument, which shall be recorded in the registry of property of the place the property is located. (137a, 168a, 169a)
Art. 111, FC. A spouse of age may mortgage, encumber, alienate or otherwise dispose of his or her exclusive property, without the consent of the other spouse, and appear alone in court to litigate with regard to the same. (n)
Art. 112, FC. The alienation of any exclusive property of a spouse administered by the other automatically terminates the administration over such property and the proceeds of the alienation shall be turned over to the owner-spouse. (n)
  • Transfer of administration to the other spouse: public instrument, recorded in the registry of property where the property is located. Art. 142 also allows the court to transfer administration in specific cases (guardianship, judicial declaration of absence, civil interdiction, or the other spouse being a fugitive from justice or in hiding as an accused).
  • A spouse of age may dispose of exclusive property without the other's consent (Art. 111). ("Of age" means 18 and above since RA 6809; cf. Art. 236 as amended.)
  • Alienation of exclusive property administered by the other spouse automatically terminates the administration. The proceeds go to the owner-spouse.
Manotok Realty v. CA (149 SCRA 372)

The Court held that the sale by Don Vicente Legarda was void ab initio because he was "neither an owner nor administrator of the subject property," and therefore "the sale cannot be the subject of the ratification by the Philippine Trust Company or the probate court." The syllabus places it under administration of exclusive property by the other spouse: a person with no power to administer or dispose cannot convey, and his sale is void.

Palanca v. Smith-Bell (9 Phil. 131)

"When a loan is negotiated by a husband upon property belonging to his wife, with the consent of the latter, the money becomes conjugal property, and if the funds are invested in the construction of a house the building is likewise conjugal property and is liable for the debts of the husband."

Lim Queco v. Cartagena (71 Phil. 163)

The wife alone borrowed money and secured it with a mortgage on her paraphernal property, executed with her husband's consent. Since the wife did not manage or represent the conjugal partnership (the husband being qualified), the loan "constituted a transaction that did not involve the community, and the creditor could seek repayment exclusively from her properties." The money loaned to her, and the property acquired with it, were deemed her exclusive property.

Art. 116: the presumption of conjugality

Art. 116, FC. All property acquired during the marriage, whether the acquisition appears to have been made, contracted or registered in the name of one or both spouses, is presumed to be conjugal unless the contrary is proved. (160a)

Rules from the cases

  1. Proof of acquisition during the marriage is a condition sine qua non. The party who invokes the presumption must first prove it (Torela; Dela Peña; Andrade; Go v. Yamane).
  2. The source of funds need not be shown. "This article does not require proof that the property was acquired with funds of the partnership. The presumption applies even when the manner in which the property was acquired does not appear" (Castro v. Miat).
  3. Registration in one spouse's name does not defeat it (Jocson; BA Finance; Art. 116 itself).
  4. "Married to …" is merely descriptive of civil status and does not prove the property conjugal (Magallon; Go-Bangayan).
  5. It yields to innocent third persons who relied on a title in the name of one spouse (PNB v. CA; Cuenca).
  6. It is rebuttable, but only by "strong, clear, categorical and convincing evidence"; the burden is on the party asserting exclusive ownership (Andrade; Laperal; Mendoza v. Reyes).
Quiz trap (CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #15): "A parcel of land is NOT presumed to belong to the conjugal partnership if: (a) acquired by gratuitous title; (b) acquired by onerous title; (c) acquired during the marriage; (d) registered during the marriage; (e) none of the above." E. Sir: for A and B, "it is immaterial" how it was acquired; the presumption still arises if it was acquired during the marriage (gratuitous acquisitions are a ground to rebut it, not a reason it never arises). C is the "worst answer", because acquisition during the marriage is precisely what triggers the presumption. D: "Just because it was registered during the marriage does not necessarily mean that it was acquired during the marriage."
Jocson v. CA (170 SCRA 333, February 16, 1989)

"All property of the marriage is presumed to belong to the conjugal partnership, unless it be proved that it pertains exclusively to the husband or to the wife (Art. 160, Civil Code). Registration of the property in the name of only one spouse does not negate the possibility of it being conjugal."

Castro v. Miat (397 SCRA 271, February 11, 2003)

Moises and Concordia were married before the Family Code, so the Civil Code applies. The Paco property, acquired by onerous title during the marriage out of the common fund, "is clearly conjugal property" under NCC 153(1). And NCC 160 "does not require proof that the property was acquired with funds of the partnership. The presumption applies even when the manner in which the property was acquired does not appear."

Ansaldo v. Sheriff (64 Phil. 115)

(1) The fruits of the paraphernal property form part of the assets of the conjugal partnership. (2) Those fruits answer for the debts and expenses of the spouses, "but not to the payment of the personal obligations of the husband, unless it be proved that such obligations were productive of some benefit to the family." (3) "The right of the husband or wife to one-half of the property of the conjugal partnership does not vest until the dissolution of the marriage, when the conjugal partnership is also dissolved." (Before dissolution, a spouse's share is a mere inchoate right.)

Torela v. Torela (93 SCRA 391)

"While it is true that all property of the marriage is presumed to be conjugal … nonetheless the party who invokes the presumption must first prove that the property was acquired during the marriage. This proof is a condition sine qua non for the application of the presumption."

PNB v. CA (158 SCRA 435)

The properties were registered in the name of "Donata Montemayor, widow" when she mortgaged them to PNB. PNB relied on the titles. Held: PNB was a mortgagee and purchaser in good faith. "When the property is registered in the name of a spouse only and there is no showing as to when the property was acquired by said spouse, this is an indication that the property belongs exclusively to said spouse. And this presumption under Article 160 … cannot prevail when the title is in the name of only one spouse and the rights of innocent third parties are involved."

Magallon v. Montejo (146 SCRA 282, December 16, 1986)

The petitioner relied on the entry in the title naming her as the "wife" of the registered owner. Held: the phrase "married to Epifania Magallon" after the owner's name "is merely descriptive of the civil status" of the registered owner and "does not necessarily prove that the land is 'conjugal' property." The NCC 160 presumption also did not apply, because the homestead claim was perfected during the owner's marriage to another woman, and there was no showing of acquisition during the alleged marriage (citing Maramba v. Lozano: the presumption does not operate where there is no showing of when the property was acquired).

Cuenca v. Cuenca (168 SCRA 335)

The presumption "refers only to the property acquired during marriage and does not operate when there is no showing as to when property alleged to be conjugal was acquired." For homestead land, the time of acquisition is the perfection of the homestead claim; that the parcels were surveyed and approved during the marriage is not determinative. The presumption also "cannot prevail when the title is in the name of only one spouse and the rights of innocent third parties are involved."

Dela Peña v. Avila (G.R. No. 187490, February 8, 2012)

"Although it is not necessary to prove that the property was acquired with funds of the partnership, proof of acquisition during the marriage is an essential condition for the operation of the presumption in favor of the conjugal partnership."

Andrade v. Tan (G.R. Nos. 171904 & 172017, August 7, 2013)

For the presumption to apply, the party invoking it "must, however, preliminarily prove that the property was indeed acquired during the marriage … the presumption in favor of conjugality does not operate if there is no showing of when the property alleged to be conjugal was acquired. Moreover, the presumption may be rebutted only with strong, clear, categorical and convincing evidence. There must be strict proof of the exclusive ownership of one of the spouses, and the burden of proof rests upon the party asserting it."

Ballados v. CA (November 19, 1990)

Listed in the syllabus at the head of the CPG cases. It is not digested in your materials, so check the original before relying on any detail.

Uribe (situation 1, CivRev 2025; 2018 situationer): X died. His heirs found a TCT registered in his name (or "X, married to Y"). You are counsel for the widow. Your best argument is that the land is not X's at all but hers (e.g., bought with her exclusive funds, with X merely holding title as trustee in an implied trust, or registered in his name for a loan). The next best argument is that it is community or conjugal property, so she takes 50% as her share plus her share as heir. Sir warned that the presumption is not automatic: it requires showing that the land was acquired during the marriage, which "is not clear in the facts". First check whether there is a marriage settlement, then the date of the marriage. Who has the burden of proof? The party claiming exclusive ownership, once acquisition during the marriage is shown.

2022 Bar: Nonoy and Daday executed a CPG settlement before marrying in 2000. During the marriage they bought land with conjugal funds, titled "Nonoy, married to Daday". Nonoy sold it to Barby without Daday's consent. Nonoy argued that her consent was unnecessary because the property was registered in his name. Valid?

No. The land is presumed conjugal regardless of whose name is on the title (Art. 116; Cordova v. Ty, G.R. No. 246255, February 3, 2021: registration is not conclusive evidence of exclusive ownership). Disposition of conjugal property without the other spouse's consent is void under Art. 124.

Art. 117: what is conjugal

Art. 117, FC. The following are conjugal partnership properties:
(1) Those acquired by onerous title during the marriage at the expense of the common fund, whether the acquisition be for the partnership, or for only one of the spouses;
(2) Those obtained from the labor, industry, work or profession of either or both of the spouses;
(3) The fruits, natural, industrial, or civil, due or received during the marriage from the common property, as well as the net fruits from the exclusive property of each spouse;
(4) The share of either spouse in the hidden treasure which the law awards to the finder or owner of the property where the treasure is found;
(5) Those acquired through occupation such as fishing or hunting;
(6) Livestock existing upon the dissolution of the partnership in excess of the number of each kind brought to the marriage by either spouse; and
(7) Those which are acquired by chance, such as winnings from gambling or betting. However, losses therefrom shall be borne exclusively by the loser-spouse. (153a, 154a, 155, 159)

Fruits of exclusive property: his favorite CPG item

Under Art. 117(3), the net fruits of each spouse's exclusive property are conjugal, whether the exclusive property was acquired gratuitously or onerously, before or during the marriage. Rentals are civil fruits (NCC 442).

Quiz trap (asked at least five times: CivRev 2025 Quiz 4 #11 and #14; CivRev midterms #31, #46, #49; Quiz #3 MCQ 7): "In a CPG, without a marriage settlement, the rentals from the exclusive property of a spouse shall form part of the: (a) exclusive properties of a spouse; (b) conjugal partnership properties only if the property was acquired by gratuitous title; (c) conjugal partnership properties only if the property was acquired by onerous title; (d)/(e) community properties if …; (f) none of the above." None of the above. A is wrong because Art. 117(3) makes the fruits conjugal. B and C are wrong because of "only": "the manner of acquisition is not relevant." D and E are the "worst answers" because "community" property belongs to the ACP, not the CPG.
Uribe (Quiz 4 #11, the general version): "The fruits from the exclusive property of a spouse shall form part of the: (a) exclusive properties of a spouse in a CPG; (b) CPG properties only if acquired by gratuitous title; (c) community properties if acquired by gratuitous title; (d) community properties if acquired by onerous title; (e) none of the above." E. On C, a student said the fruits "will not form part" of the ACP. Sir: "Hindi pwedeng 'will not form part'. Ano siya? Will it be owned by the neighbor?" The fruits form part of the separate property. "Pag sinabing 'community property', it should be clear that the regime is ACP. You don't have to be told that the regime of the spouses is ACP." In the ACP, the fruits of excluded property are separate, regardless of the manner of acquisition, so C and D are both wrong.
Zulueta v. Pan American World Airways (listed in the syllabus as "Zulueta v. Pan Am, SCRA 1")

Damages for breach of a contract of carriage, whose fare was presumably paid with conjugal funds, are conjugal property under NCC 153(1), "the right thereto having been acquired by onerous title during the marriage." They do not fall under the provisions on paraphernal property. "[I]t follows necessarily that that which is acquired with money of the conjugal partnership belongs thereto or forms part thereof."

Mendoza v. Reyes (124 SCRA 154)

The NCC 160 presumption "must also be overcome by one who contends that the disputed property is paraphernal."

Hidden treasure, occupation, livestock, winnings

  • Hidden treasure (NCC 438): if found on another's property by chance, half goes to the finder and half to the owner. Under Art. 117(4), either spouse's share, as finder or as owner, is conjugal. UP BOC 2011 MCQ: a husband finds treasure on his wife's paraphernal property → D: both halves belong to the conjugal partnership. 2008 Bar: if the finder or owner is married, the treasure forms part of the ACP or CPG (Arts. 91, 93, 106, 117).
  • Livestock: only the excess over the number of each kind brought to the marriage is conjugal.
  • Gambling: winnings → conjugal; losses → the loser-spouse (Arts. 117(7), 123).

Arts. 118–119: installments and credits

Art. 118, FC. Property bought on installments paid partly from exclusive funds of either or both spouses and partly from conjugal funds belongs to the buyer or buyers if full ownership was vested before the marriage and to the conjugal partnership if such ownership was vested during the marriage. In either case, any amount advanced by the partnership or by either or both spouses shall be reimbursed by the owner or owners upon liquidation of the partnership. (n)
  • The test is when full ownership vested, not when the price was fully paid or who paid more.
  • Absolute sale on installments (ownership passes on delivery, even if the price is unpaid) → if delivered before the marriage, the property belongs to the buyer-spouse.
  • Conditional sale / contract to sell (ownership passes only on full payment) → if full payment occurs during the marriage, the property is conjugal.
  • Either way, the other side is reimbursed upon liquidation.
Quiz trap (CivRev midterms pair #17, Statement 1): "Property bought on installments paid partly from exclusive funds of either or both spouses and partly from conjugal funds belongs to the conjugal partnership." False (Sir's answer: D): it belongs to the buyer if full ownership was vested before the marriage.
Uribe (situation 11, CivRev 2025; 2018 situationer): five years before the marriage, A bought a house and lot payable in equal monthly installments over 10 years. After the wedding, both salaries paid the amortizations. A died six years after the wedding. Part of A's estate? Sir: check for a settlement, then the date. If ACP, the property is community regardless of when ownership vested. If CPG, apply Art. 118. A student presumed that ownership passed only upon full payment (during the marriage). Sir: "May presumption ka kasi. Di naman necessarily true … even if the price is paid by installments, it may still be an absolute sale. The ownership may be passed prior to the marriage." "Sale on installment does not change the nature of the contract." If ownership passed before the marriage, the property is A's (part of his estate), and the partnership is reimbursed for its payments. If it passed during the marriage (a conditional sale), the property is conjugal, and A's estate is reimbursed for his premarital payments.

UP BOC 2011 MCQ: Audrey, single, bought land for ₱1M under a contract that already vested full ownership in her, payable in monthly installments over four years. A year later she married Arnel under a CPG settlement, and later installments were paid from conjugal funds. Conjugal or paraphernal?

B: paraphernal, because ownership was acquired before the marriage (Art. 118). The partnership is reimbursed for its payments upon liquidation.

2018 Bar: Sancho bought land on installment before marrying Sandy, paid four annual installments from his salary, and completed the rest after the wedding (CPG settlement). The marriage was later declared void under Art. 36. Who gets the land?

Since the marriage is void, there was never a CPG. The parties' property relations fall under Art. 147, and Arts. 118–129 do not apply. The UPLC suggested answer awards the land to Sancho and the lottery-bought jewelry to Sandy (lottery winnings are not "work or industry", and Sandy's care of the household was not established). See the Unions section.

Castillo v. Pasco (11 SCRA 102, decided under the Spanish Civil Code)

A fishpond bought in 1932 during the marriage. Under the Spanish Civil Code, property acquired for onerous consideration during the marriage was conjugal or separate "depending on the source of the funds employed for its acquisition" (Arts. 1396(4), 1401(1)). That the sale was made in favor of both spouses "is indifferent for the determination of whether the property should be deemed paraphernal or conjugal." (The source of funds decides, not the names on the deed.)

Art. 119, FC. Whenever an amount or credit payable within a period of time belongs to one of the spouses, the sums which may be collected during the marriage in partial payments or by installments on the principal shall be the exclusive property of the spouse. However, interests falling due during the marriage on the principal shall belong to the conjugal partnership. (156a, 157a)
  • Principal collected during the marriage → the creditor-spouse.
  • Interest falling due during the marriage → the partnership (it is a civil fruit; compare Art. 117(3)).

UP BOC 2011 MCQ: Solomon sold his plantation for ₱100M payable in monthly installments with 6% interest. Five months later he married Lorna under a CPG. To whom do the payments after the marriage go?

D: the principal to Solomon; the interest to the conjugal partnership (Art. 119).

Uribe (2018 situationer): A, a moneylender, lent X ₱1M at 24% a year, payable in two years. A year later, A married B. X paid only three years after the loan. Who is entitled? Under a CPG, the principal goes to A as his exclusive property. Interest falling due during the marriage goes to the partnership. After default, with a demand, legal interest (6%) runs as damages. "What matters most? The principal amount. It goes to the spouse." Under an ACP, everything is community ("madali lang, wag na").

Art. 120: improvements on separate property (reverse accession)

Art. 120, FC. The ownership of improvements, whether for utility or adornment, made on the separate property of the spouses at the expense of the partnership or through the acts or efforts of either or both spouses shall pertain to the conjugal partnership, or to the original owner-spouse, subject to the following rules:
When the cost of the improvement made by the conjugal partnership and any resulting increase in value are more than the value of the property at the time of the improvement, the entire property of one of the spouses shall belong to the conjugal partnership, subject to reimbursement of the value of the property of the owner-spouse at the time of the improvement; otherwise, said property shall be retained in ownership by the owner-spouse, likewise subject to reimbursement of the cost of the improvement.
In either case, the ownership of the entire property shall be vested upon the reimbursement, which shall be made at the time of the liquidation of the conjugal partnership. (158a)

The test

  1. Compare (cost of the improvement + resulting increase in value) with (value of the separate property at the time of the improvement).
  2. If the first is greater → the entire property becomes conjugal (reverse accession), and the owner-spouse is reimbursed the value of the property.
  3. Otherwise → the owner-spouse keeps the entire property, and the partnership is reimbursed the cost of the improvement.
  4. Ownership vests only upon reimbursement, which is made at liquidation.

2012 Bar: Maria withdrew ₱5M from conjugal funds and built a building on a lot she inherited from her father. Is the building conjugal or paraphernal?

It depends. If the value of the building is more than the value of the land, the building is conjugal and the land becomes conjugal too (reverse accession under Art. 120). Otherwise, the ordinary rule of accession applies: the land is principal, both remain paraphernal, and the partnership is reimbursed. Note: reverse accession applies only to the CPG, under both the FC and the NCC.

UP BOC 2011 MCQ: Josie owned a lot worth ₱5M before marrying Rey. Their CPG spent ₱3M to build a house on it, and the house and lot became worth ₱9M. Who owns them?

B: Josie owns both, since the value of the house and the increase in value is less than her lot's value; she reimburses the partnership's expenses (UP BOC suggested answer).

Calimlim-Canullas v. Fortun (129 SCRA 675, 1984)

Under NCC 158, buildings constructed at the expense of the partnership during the marriage on land belonging to one spouse "also pertain to the partnership, but the value of the land shall be reimbursed to the spouse who owns the same." Held: both the land and the building belong to the conjugal partnership, but the partnership is indebted to the husband for the value of the land, which is reimbursed at liquidation. "The conversion from paraphernal to conjugal assets should be deemed to retroact to the time the conjugal buildings were first constructed thereon," the acquisition being subject to the suspensive condition of reimbursement. (In the case, the husband sold the lot and the conjugal house to his concubine. The sale was also declared void as contrary to morals and public policy, applying by analogy the prohibition on sales and donations between spouses to persons living in concubinage.)

Maramba v. Lozano (20 SCRA 474)

"The construction of a house at conjugal expense on the exclusive property of one of the spouses does not automatically make it conjugal." In the meantime, the partnership uses the land and building "not as owner but in the exercise of the right of usufruct." "The ownership of the land remains the same until the value thereof is paid, and this payment can only be demanded in the liquidation of the partnership." (Read with Art. 120's last paragraph: ownership vests upon reimbursement at liquidation.)

Arts. 121–123: charges upon and obligations of the CPG

Art. 121, FC. The conjugal partnership shall be liable for:
(1) The support of the spouse, their common children, and the legitimate children of either spouse; however, the support of illegitimate children shall be governed by the provisions of this Code on Support;
(2) All debts and obligations contracted during the marriage by the designated administrator-spouse for the benefit of the conjugal partnership of gains, or by both spouses or by one of them with the consent of the other;
(3) Debts and obligations contracted by either spouse without the consent of the other to the extent that the family may have benefited;
(4) All taxes, liens, charges, and expenses, including major or minor repairs upon the conjugal partnership property;
(5) All taxes and expenses for mere preservation made during the marriage upon the separate property of either spouse;
(6) Expenses to enable either spouse to commence or complete a professional, vocational, or other activity for self-improvement;
(7) Ante-nuptial debts of either spouse insofar as they have redounded to the benefit of the family;
(8) The value of what is donated or promised by both spouses in favor of their common legitimate children for the exclusive purpose of commencing or completing a professional or vocational course or other activity for self-improvement; and
(9) Expenses of litigation between the spouses unless the suit is found to groundless.
If the conjugal partnership is insufficient to cover the foregoing liabilities, the spouses shall be solidarily liable for the unpaid balance with their separate properties. (161a)
Art. 122, FC. The payment of personal debts contracted by the husband or the wife before or during the marriage shall not be charged to the conjugal properties partnership except insofar as they redounded to the benefit of the family.
Neither shall the fines and pecuniary indemnities imposed upon them be charged to the partnership.
However, the payment of personal debts contracted by either spouse before the marriage, that of fines and indemnities imposed upon them, as well as the support of illegitimate children of either spouse, may be enforced against the partnership assets after the responsibilities enumerated in the preceding Article have been covered, if the spouse who is bound should have no exclusive property or if it should be insufficient; but at the time of the liquidation of the partnership, such spouse shall be charged for what has been paid for the purpose above-mentioned. (163a)
Art. 123, FC. Whatever may be lost during the marriage in any game of chance or in betting, sweepstakes, or any other kind of gambling whether permitted or prohibited by law, shall be borne by the loser and shall not be charged to the conjugal partnership but any winnings therefrom shall form part of the conjugal partnership property. (164a)

The "benefit of the family" cases

Luzon Surety Co. v. de Garcia (30 SCRA 111, 1969)

The husband signed an indemnity agreement as a guarantor or surety for a third person's loan. Held: the conjugal partnership is not liable. "The benefit accruing to the family must be clear and not fanciful and remote before the conjugal partnership can be held liable." That signing might enhance his reputation is "too remote and fanciful." (Pre-FC rule; Arts. 121(3) and 122 carry it forward: "to the extent that the family may have benefited.")

BA Finance Corp. v. CA (161 SCRA 608)

Registration in one spouse's name does not destroy the property's conjugal nature. But for conjugal property to answer for the husband's obligation, the obligation "must have redounded to the benefit of the conjugal partnership." Here the husband contracted it "for his own benefit", after he had abandoned his family, and he falsely made it appear that his wife had authorized it. Holding the wife's business (A & L Industries) liable "would be unjust".

Carlos v. Abelardo (380 SCRA 361, April 9, 2002)

The husband refused to sign the acknowledgment of a loan signed by his wife. "[U]ndoubtedly, the loan redounded to the benefit of the family because it was used to purchase the house and lot which became the conjugal home." Under Art. 121, he is solidarily liable with his wife for the loan, notwithstanding his lack of consent.

Javier v. Osmeña (34 Phil. 336)

Since the fruits of paraphernal property are conjugal and liable for marriage expenses, the husband's creditor may proceed "not against the paraphernal property, but against the fruits and revenues of this private property of the wife," notwithstanding the rule that the husband's personal obligations cannot be paid from those fruits unless they benefited the family.

Vda. de Sta. Romana v. PCIB (118 SCRA 330)

The unpaid price of a lot the husband bought on behalf of the partnership is chargeable against the conjugal assets, the obligation having been contracted for the partnership's benefit (NCC 161(1)). The wife need not be joined as a defendant in a suit against the husband to bind the conjugal property, the husband then being the legal administrator.

DBP v. Adil (161 SCRA 307, 1988)

A debtor who executes a new promissory note for a prescribed debt waives prescription; "a new contract recognizing and assuming the prescribed debt would be valid and enforceable." As the administrator of the partnership (NCC 165), the husband who signed the new note for the partnership's benefit binds the conjugal partnership.

People v. Lagrimas (29 SCRA 153)

Fines and indemnities imposed on a spouse may be enforced against the partnership assets even before liquidation, after the NCC 161 responsibilities are covered, if the spouse has no or insufficient exclusive property. At liquidation, that spouse is charged with what was paid (NCC 163, now Art. 122). Otherwise, the accused would effectively escape civil liability, and the victim's heirs "would be made to suffer still further."

Quiz traps on Arts. 121–122: (1) Both Art. 94(1) and Art. 121(1) cover only the legitimate children of either spouse; support of illegitimate children follows the rules on Support (Art. 197). (2) Art. 121(5) has no "used by the family" qualifier. (3) The last paragraph of Art. 121 has no exception for advances (contrast Art. 94: "except those falling under paragraph (9)"). (4) Art. 122: personal debts before or during the marriage are chargeable only "insofar as they redounded to the benefit of the family". Fines are not chargeable, except subsidiarily under the third paragraph.

Arts. 124–125: administration and disposition of conjugal property

Art. 124, FC. The administration and enjoyment of the conjugal partnership shall belong to both spouses jointly. In case of disagreement, the husband's decision shall prevail, subject to recourse to the court by the wife for proper remedy, which must be availed of within five years from the date of the contract implementing such decision.
In the event that one spouse is incapacitated or otherwise unable to participate in the administration of the conjugal properties, the other spouse may assume sole powers of administration. These powers do not include disposition or encumbrance without authority of the court or the written consent of the other spouse. In the absence of such authority or consent, the disposition or encumbrance shall be void. However, the transaction shall be construed as a continuing offer on the part of the consenting spouse and the third person, and may be perfected as a binding contract upon the acceptance by the other spouse or authorization by the court before the offer is withdrawn by either or both offerors. (165a)
Art. 125, FC. Neither spouse may donate any conjugal partnership property without the consent of the other. However, either spouse may, without the consent of the other, make moderate donations from the conjugal partnership property for charity or on occasions of family rejoicing or family distress. (174a)

Ysasi v. Fernandez (23 SCRA 1079, NCC)

"The husband is the administrator of the conjugal partnership … More, the husband is the sole administrator. The wife is not entitled, as of right, to joint administration." She may be held in contempt for refusing to deliver the conjugal assets and may be required to account. Her remedy for abuse is to ask the court for receivership, administration by her, or separation of property (NCC 167). Superseded by the joint administration rule of Art. 124.

Felipe v. Heirs of Aldon (120 SCRA 628, NCC)

The wife (Gimena) sold conjugal lands without her husband's consent. Under the Civil Code, such a contract is voidable, "annullable at [the husband's] instance during the marriage and within ten years from the transaction questioned" (NCC 173). Only the victim (the husband) could seek annulment, and only during the marriage. Gimena, the party responsible for the defect, could not, and the children had a merely inchoate right while the marriage subsisted. "What she could not do during the marriage, she could not do thereafter."

Heirs of Aguilar-Reyes v. Mijares (410 SCRA 97, August 28, 2003)

The husband sold a conjugal lot in 1983 without the wife's knowledge and consent. Under the Civil Code, the sale is voidable. Ignacia's action to annul, filed in 1986 before her death, was "perfectly within the 10-year prescriptive period under Article 173." (The sale was annulled as a whole, subject to reimbursement of what was paid.)

Which rule applies? The date of the sale decides. A sale after August 3, 1988 of conjugal property without consent is void under Art. 124, even if the marriage was celebrated under the Civil Code (Art. 105, 2nd par.; Art. 256), because the other spouse has no vested right to a voidable-contract regime. Sales before that date are voidable under NCC 166 and 173.

2006 Bar: Spouses Biong and Linda agreed to sell their house to Ray for ₱2M. Biong signed the deed and received a check, but Linda, back from abroad, refused to sign. She sued to nullify the sale. Will it prosper?

Yes. Sale of community or conjugal property requires the written consent of both spouses (Arts. 96, 124; Abalos v. Macatangay, G.R. No. 155043, September 30, 2004). Biong's consent alone is insufficient, and Linda's refusal shows she never consented. Ray, who was not in bad faith, is not liable for damages, but he may recover the ₱2M he paid.

Arts. 126–128: dissolution, separation in fact, abandonment

Art. 126, FC. The conjugal partnership terminates:
(1) Upon the death of either spouse;
(2) When there is a decree of legal separation;
(3) When the marriage is annulled or declared void; or
(4) In case of judicial separation of property during the marriage under Articles 134 to 138. (175a)
Art. 127, FC. The separation in fact between husband and wife shall not affect the regime of conjugal partnership, except that:
(1) The spouse who leaves the conjugal home or refuses to live therein, without just cause, shall not have the right to be supported;
(2) When the consent of one spouse to any transaction of the other is required by law, judicial authorization shall be obtained in a summary proceeding;
(3) In the absence of sufficient conjugal partnership property, the separate property of both spouses shall be solidarily liable for the support of the family. The spouse present shall, upon petition in a summary proceeding, be given judicial authority to administer or encumber any specific separate property of the other spouse and use the fruits or proceeds thereof to satisfy the latter's share. (178a)
Art. 128, FC. If a spouse without just cause abandons the other or fails to comply with his or her obligation to the family, the aggrieved spouse may petition the court for receivership, for judicial separation of property, or for authority to be the sole administrator of the conjugal partnership property, subject to such precautionary conditions as the court may impose.
The obligations to the family mentioned in the preceding paragraph refer to marital, parental or property relations.
A spouse is deemed to have abandoned the other when he or she has left the conjugal dwelling without intention of returning. The spouse who has left the conjugal dwelling for a period of three months or has failed within the same period to give any information as to his or her whereabouts shall be prima facie presumed to have no intention of returning to the conjugal dwelling. (167a, 191a)
De la Cruz v. De la Cruz (22 SCRA 333, 1968)

The husband left the conjugal home but "continues to manage the conjugal properties with the same zeal, industry, and efficiency as he did prior to the separation, and religiously gives support to his wife and children." Held: the wife's petition for separation of property is denied. Mere physical separation is not abandonment. The remedies against abandonment "are designed to protect the conjugal partnership from waste and shield the wife from want." Denying the petition is not a condonation of the husband's leaving; it reflects the absence of a cause of action. (Abandonment, for purposes of these remedies, means leaving with intent not to return and failing to comply with obligations to the family.)

Arts. 129–133: liquidation of the CPG

Art. 129, FC. Upon the dissolution of the conjugal partnership regime, the following procedure shall apply:
(1) An inventory shall be prepared, listing separately all the properties of the conjugal partnership and the exclusive properties of each spouse.
(2) Amounts advanced by the conjugal partnership in payment of personal debts and obligations of either spouse shall be credited to the conjugal partnership as an asset thereof.
(3) Each spouse shall be reimbursed for the use of his or her exclusive funds in the acquisition of property or for the value of his or her exclusive property, the ownership of which has been vested by law in the conjugal partnership.
(4) The debts and obligations of the conjugal partnership shall be paid out of the conjugal assets. In case of insufficiency of said assets, the spouses shall be solidarily liable for the unpaid balance with their separate properties, in accordance with the provisions of paragraph (2) of Article 121.
(5) Whatever remains of the exclusive properties of the spouses shall thereafter be delivered to each of them.
(6) Unless the owner had been indemnified from whatever source, the loss or deterioration of movables used for the benefit of the family, belonging to either spouse, even due to fortuitous event, shall be paid to said spouse from the conjugal funds, if any.
(7) The net remainder of the conjugal partnership properties shall constitute the profits, which shall be divided equally between husband and wife, unless a different proportion or division was agreed upon in the marriage settlements or unless there has been a voluntary waiver or forfeiture of such share as provided in this Code.
(8) The presumptive legitimes of the common children shall be delivered upon the partition in accordance with Article 51.
(9) In the partition of the properties, the conjugal dwelling and the lot on which it is situated shall, unless otherwise agreed upon by the parties, be adjudicated to the spouse with whom the majority of the common children choose to remain. Children below the age of seven years are deemed to have chosen the mother, unless the court has decided otherwise. In case there is no such majority, the court shall decide, taking into consideration the best interests of said children. (181a, 182a, 183a, 184a, 185a)

Beumer v. Amores (G.R. No. 195670, December 3, 2012)

A Dutch national, after the nullity of his marriage to a Filipina, sought reimbursement for half of the lots bought during the marriage, claiming he paid for them with his own money. Held: no reimbursement. He knowingly violated the constitutional prohibition on aliens owning land, so "he acquired no right whatsoever over the subject properties by virtue of its unconstitutional purchase." "[E]quity as a rule will follow the law and will not permit that to be done indirectly which, because of public policy, cannot be done directly" (NCC 1412: the law leaves the parties where it finds them). His inconsistent statements about the source of funds also meant he came to court with unclean hands.

Santero v. CFI of Cavite (153 SCRA 728, 1987)

Under NCC 188 (now FC 133), support during liquidation goes to the surviving spouse and the children "without distinction". That the children "are of age, gainfully employed, or married is of no moment." The Rules of Court limit allowances to the widow and the minor or incapacitated children, but this procedural rule cannot impair the substantive right in the Civil Code. The allowances are advances from their shares. "Spouse" means the legitimate spouse, not a common-law spouse.

Checklist: CPG in one page

  1. Applies by settlement (Family Code) or by default (pre-August 3, 1988 marriages). The Family Code chapter applies to old CPGs without prejudice to vested rights.
  2. Commences at the celebration; no waiver during the marriage except upon judicial separation (Art. 107). Suppletory rules: partnership (Art. 108).
  3. Exclusive (Art. 109): brought to the marriage; gratuitous during the marriage; redemption, barter or exchange with exclusive property; bought with exclusive money.
  4. Conjugal (Art. 117): onerous acquisitions at the common fund's expense; labor and profession; fruits of everything, including net fruits of exclusive property; hidden treasure shares; occupation; excess livestock; winnings.
  5. Presumption (Art. 116): acquired during the marriage → conjugal, whatever the name in the title. Proof of acquisition during the marriage comes first; the source of funds need not be shown; "married to" is descriptive; innocent third parties are protected.
  6. Installments (Art. 118): when full ownership vested. Credits (Art. 119): principal → spouse; interest → partnership.
  7. Improvements (Art. 120): cost + increase vs. value of the property; ownership vests upon reimbursement at liquidation.
  8. Charges (Art. 121): note "legitimate children of either spouse" and preservation of separate property without the "used by the family" condition; solidary liability for any balance. Art. 122: personal debts only if they benefited the family; fines and support of illegitimate children are subsidiary, charged at liquidation.
  9. Joint administration; disposition without written consent or court authority → void (NCC: voidable within 10 years); continuing offer (Art. 124). Donations need consent except moderate ones (Art. 125).
  10. Termination (Art. 126) and separation in fact or abandonment (Arts. 127–128) mirror the ACP.
  11. Liquidation (Art. 129): inventory → credit advances → reimburse spouses → pay debts → return exclusive property → indemnify movables → divide profits equally → presumptive legitimes → conjugal dwelling.
  12. Death: estate proceeding or six months, otherwise dispositions are void; remarriage without liquidation → mandatory separation (Art. 130). Support from the common mass during liquidation (Art. 133; Santero).
