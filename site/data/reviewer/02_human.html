Human Relations (NCC 19–35), Prejudicial Question (NCC 36) & Breach of Promise to Marry

Covers Civil Code Arts. 19–36 (syllabus II.K–L), Rule 111 of the Rules of Criminal Procedure (independent civil actions, Secs. 1–7), and Breach of Promise to Marry (syllabus VII.A.3: NCC 19–21, 2176, 1403(2)(c)). Syllabus cases: People v. Ritter; Ardiente v. Spouses Pastorfide; Donato v. Luna; Landicho v. Relova; Pulido v. People; Cabague v. Auxilio; Domalagan v. Bolifer; Hermosisima v. CA; Wassmer v. Velez; Estremos v. Ephan; Tanjanco v. CA; Baksh v. CA. Supporting cases: Aberca v. Ver, Merced v. Diez, Zapanta v. Montesa, Tenebro v. CA, and others.

Overview: the chapter on Human Relations

Chapter 2 of the Preliminary Title ("Human Relations," Arts. 19–36) is entirely new in the 1950 Civil Code. According to the Code Commission, the old Civil Code only stated the effects of the law and did not spell out its spirit. These articles state norms "that spring from the fountain of good conscience," guides for human conduct that should "run as golden threads through society, to the end that law may approach its supreme ideal, which is the sway and dominance of justice."

Tolentino: Human beings come into relations with one another, and their interests (wants and desires) constantly conflict. The primary function of law is to protect these interests. The ruling social philosophy is that, in the ultimate ideal social order, the welfare of every man depends on the welfare of all. Because these are new provisions, they may be given retroactive effect if they do not prejudice or impair vested or acquired rights under the old legislation (cf. Art. 2252).

The chapter has three blocks you must keep apart:

  1. Arts. 19–28: substantive norms and causes of action: abuse of rights (19), violation of law (20), acts contra bonos mores (21), unjust enrichment (22–23), protection of the disadvantaged (24), thoughtless extravagance (25), dignity and privacy (26), refusal or neglect of a public servant (27), and unfair competition (28).
  2. Arts. 29–35: civil liability vis-à-vis criminal proceedings: acquittal on reasonable doubt (29), separate civil action ex delicto with no criminal case (30), civil action on other sources of obligation (31), and the independent civil actions (32, 33, 34) plus the residual civil action when the prosecutor will not prosecute (35).
  3. Art. 36: pre-judicial questions, which the Code leaves to the Rules of Court (now Rule 111, Secs. 6–7).

Art. 19: Abuse of rights

Art. 19, NCC. Every person must, in the exercise of his rights and in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith.
Quiz trap (Uribe Quiz 1, #10; recurring samplex item): "Under the Abuse of Rights Principle, every person must, in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith." FALSE. The omitted phrase is "in the exercise of his rights and." Uribe's explanation: "Abuse of rights is not at the performance of duties but the exercise of rights. A person may have a right but may still be liable if in the exercise of that right he became abusive." Memorize the three standards: (1) act with justice, (2) give everyone his due, (3) observe honesty and good faith. Expect him to swap "honesty and good faith" for "diligence" or to drop one of them.

Source and nature

  • Taken from modern codes (Swiss Art. 2: "every person is bound to exercise his rights and fulfill his obligations according to the principles of good faith. The law does not protect the manifest abuse of a right"; also the German, Mexican, Chinese, Peruvian and Soviet codes). Our Code follows the broader Swiss concept, not the narrower German rule that limits abuse to acts whose only purpose is to injure.
  • Art. 19 states a norm of conduct. When a right is exercised contrary to it and damage results, the wrong is actionable. The sanction comes from Art. 20 or Art. 21 (Globe Mackay v. CA). Tolentino: every abuse of right is now "contrary to law," the law being Art. 19 itself, so Art. 20 supplies the sanction.
  • Uribe (recitation situationer on performance of obligations): Art. 19 is the cardinal rule in carrying out obligations. "Full compliance is not enough." You comply with justice, honesty and good faith, not merely with the letter of the contract or law. If the obligation comes from law, read the IRR too; if from contract, read the contract, which is the law between the parties.
Tolentino (abandonment of the old rule): The classical rule was qui jure suo utitur neminem laedit, "he who uses a right injures no one," and Spanish jurisprudence applied it absolutely. The modern tendency, adopted by our Code, departs from it: indemnity is granted even where the act is not illicit, if there is abuse of right. "If mere fault or negligence in one's acts can make him liable for damages, with more reason should abuse or bad faith make him liable." A person is protected only when he acts in the legitimate exercise of his right, that is, with prudence and in good faith. "Abuse of rights" is technically a contradiction in terms, because when a person acts with abuse his right ceases and his act becomes illicit. The exercise of a right ends when the right disappears, and it disappears when it is abused, especially to the prejudice of others. The three supreme norms of justice are honeste vivere, alterum non laedere, jus suum quique tribuere (to live honestly, not to injure another, to give each one his due), and he who violates them violates the law.

Test of abuse (Tolentino)

  • There is undoubtedly an abuse when a right is exercised for the only purpose of prejudicing or injuring another. The illicit act cannot be concealed under the guise of exercising a right.
  • More broadly: every abnormal exercise of a right, contrary to its socio-economic purpose, is an abuse that gives rise to liability. The exercise must accord with the purpose for which the right was established, must not be excessive or unduly harsh, and must show no intention to injure. Ultimately the courts decide on the facts.
  • Good faith: the absence of good faith is essential to abuse of right. Good faith is an honest intention to abstain from taking any unconscientious advantage of another, even through the forms or technicalities of the law, together with an absence of all information or belief of facts that would render the transaction unconscientious. In business it means good faith as understood by men of affairs.
  • Relation to Art. 21: the principles of abuse of rights and of acts contra bonos mores are complementary. Some cases fall within both.

Elements (jurisprudence)

  1. There is a legal right or duty;
  2. which is exercised in bad faith;
  3. for the sole intent of prejudicing or injuring another.

(Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. CA, 1989; Albenson Enterprises v. CA, 1993; restated in Ardiente v. Spouses Pastorfide, 2013.)

Ardiente v. Spouses Pastorfide (G.R. No. 161921, July 17, 2013)

Ardiente sold her rights over a housing unit to the Pastorfide spouses. The water service account remained in Ardiente's name. Ardiente later asked the water district (COWD) to disconnect the water supply, and COWD cut it off without notice to the spouses, who went without water for months. Doctrine: The principle of abuse of rights in Art. 19 provides that every person must, in the exercise of his rights and in the performance of his duties, act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith. Its elements are (1) a legal right or duty, (2) exercised in bad faith, (3) for the sole intent of prejudicing or injuring another. Ardiente's act of having the water disconnected without notice, knowing the spouses were the ones using it, was done in bad faith, and COWD's disconnection without prior notice was likewise an abuse of right. Both were held liable for damages. A right, though recognized by law, must be exercised in good faith. One who exercises it with the intent of injuring another is liable even if the act would otherwise be lawful.

Globe Mackay Cable and Radio Corp. v. CA (1989)

An employee was accused of anomalies, called a "crook" and "swindler," made to undergo questionable investigations, and dismissed. The employer then wrote a damaging letter to a prospective employer, preventing him from getting new employment. Doctrine: Art. 19 sets the standards for the exercise of rights and the performance of duties. A right, though by itself legal because recognized by law, may be exercised in a manner that does not conform to these norms and thus results in damage to another, a legal wrong for which the wrongdoer must be held responsible. Art. 19 does not itself provide a remedy. The remedy is under Art. 20 (violation of law) or Art. 21 (acts contra bonos mores). The employer's right to dismiss was exercised abusively, so it was liable for damages.

Carpio v. Valmonte (2004)

A wedding coordinator was publicly accused of stealing jewelry by the bride's aunt, who searched her and humiliated her in front of others. Doctrine: One is not allowed to exercise his rights in a manner that would cause unnecessary prejudice to another or offend morals or good customs. The aunt had the right to look for her lost jewelry, but the public verbal reproach was uncalled for. She was liable for moral damages (Arts. 19 and 26).

Nikko Hotel Manila Garden v. Reyes (2005)

A man who was not on the guest list of a private party was asked to leave. Doctrine: A common theme runs through Arts. 19 and 21: the act complained of must be intentional. Absent proof of ill will or intent to injure, and given that the hotel had a basis (he was not on the list), there is no abuse of right. One who gate-crashes cannot complain of being asked to leave, especially when it was done discreetly.

Albenson Enterprises v. CA (1993)

Doctrine: Restated the three elements of abuse of right. Filing a criminal complaint against the wrong person, in good faith and on probable cause, is not an abuse of right. The law gives no penalty for the right to litigate, and malicious prosecution requires malice and want of probable cause.

Metroheights Subdivision Homeowners Ass'n v. CMS Construction (2018)

A water line installed at the homeowners' expense was cut and rerouted without their knowledge or consent. Doctrine: Acts done arbitrarily, without consent, injuriously and prejudicially, show an abuse of right. One must act with justice, give everyone his due, and observe honesty and good faith.

Spouses Cabasal v. BPI Family Savings Bank (2020)

The bank refused to recognize a sale with assumption of mortgage because of its policy, then foreclosed. Doctrine: Following a legitimate bank policy in good faith is not an abuse of right. Bad faith must be shown before damages can be awarded under Arts. 19–21.

Illustrations of abuse (Tolentino)

  • Spite structures: a tall dummy chimney built only to deprive a neighbor of light (French Court of Colmar). The same applies to a high wall built only to cut off a neighbor's light, air or view.
  • Scaffolds bristling with spikes built to pressure an airship company into buying the lot at a high price. Pumping a mineral spring only to diminish a neighbor's yield.
  • A father who, for purely personal reasons, refuses to let his son enter the tomb where the mother was buried.
  • A mortgagee who, instead of foreclosing, levies on machinery not subject to the mortgage and removes it from the debtor's business.
  • A suit filed purely to harass. A majority of stockholders adopting a resolution that prejudices the minority with no benefit to the corporation.
  • Pre-contractual abuse: a virtual monopoly (railroad, shipping) refusing to carry goods without legitimate reason. A hotel refusing guests without just cause when there is no other place for them. An offeror withdrawing an offer, in abuse of right, after the offeree in good faith has incurred expenses.
  • Contractual abuse: dismissing an employee because he joined a union or party. A lessor capriciously refusing consent to a sublease. Inducing an artist to breach a contract with a rival theater. "Cut-throat" competition. Interposing the Statute of Frauds after advising the other party that no formality was needed. Pleading prescription after delaying the plaintiff through bad-faith settlement talks. Revoking an agency capriciously or to prejudice the agent. In Manila Oriental Sawmill v. National Labor Union (1952), mass resignation from one union to join another, done to circumvent a subsisting agreement, was held a derogation of the good faith that must exist in contractual relations.
  • Breach of promise to marry: a promise to marry has no obligatory force. But a prospective bride or groom who capriciously breaks an engagement without reason, causing moral and material injury, may be liable, especially if the break occurs just before the wedding after a long engagement. Freedom of consent entitles either party to withdraw, but the right to withdraw must not be exercised unjustly or abusively. (See the Breach of Promise subtopic.)

No abuse: use of a right; absolute rights; justice by own hand

  • Use of right (damnum absque injuria): a person who merely uses a right, without bad faith or intent to injure, is not liable even if another suffers damage. No abuse was found where (1) a theft victim denounces suspects to the police; (2) a person who believes his right was violated sues, even if the defendant is absolved; (3) a merchant opens a store near another's and attracts his patrons; (4) a credit agency gives unfavorable reports based on gathered data; (5) an employer dismisses an employee who has grown old, slow and inefficient.
  • Absolute rights, whose exercise can never be the basis of liability: (1) the right of parents to refuse or deny consent to the marriage of a minor child; (2) the right to deprive one's legal heirs of an inheritance by giving away property, so long as the legitime is not impaired; (3) the right to set up the nullity of contracts, legal presumptions, and prescription of obligations. Ordinarily the refusal to enter into a contract is also absolute, and the motive cannot be inquired into.
  • Justice by own hand is generally not allowed. One must go to court. Exceptions include the parents' right to discipline children with moderation, an owner's right to cut off roots of a neighbor's trees that penetrate his land (Art. 680), and abating a nuisance extrajudicially (Arts. 704, 706). A lessor who fences off a gasoline station after the lease expires, instead of filing unlawful detainer, commits an abuse of right (cf. Art. 536: one who believes he has a right to deprive another of the holding of a thing must invoke the aid of the competent court).
Tolentino (legal sanction): Critics said abuse-of-rights provisions are mere "advice," improper in a code. Even without them, courts can apply the principle, which is implied in any system of law. In any case, the sanction is in Art. 20, because every abuse of right is now contrary to law, the law being Art. 19.

L leased an apartment to R. Because of the Rent Control Law, L could not raise the rent or evict R. To force her out, L stopped repairs and had the water and electricity cut. R suffered a nervous breakdown. Can R recover actual and moral damages?

Answer: Yes (1996 Bar). L's acts were done in bad faith and only to prejudice R, in circumvention of the law. That is an abuse of right (Art. 19) and an act contra bonos mores (Art. 21). Art. 20 also applies because L violated his legal duty to make necessary repairs (Art. 1654). Moral damages are recoverable under Art. 2219(10) for Art. 21 acts, and actual damages upon proof.

Art. 20: Damage caused contrary to law

Art. 20, NCC. Every person who, contrary to law, wilfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same.
  • Source: German Code Art. 823 and California Civil Code Art. 1714.
  • Scope: it furnishes the general sanction for all other provisions of law that do not especially provide their own sanction. It is broad enough to cover all legal wrongs that do not constitute violations of contract (Tolentino).
  • Elements: (1) an act contrary to law; (2) done wilfully or negligently; (3) damage to another.
  • "Law" includes Art. 19 itself, so an abuse of right is actionable through Art. 20.
Tolentino (moral negligence): A person must act with prudence toward others, not with charity. The law imposes diligence, not altruism. Failure to make sacrifices, or egoism, is not a source of liability, and there is no civil responsibility for purely moral fault or negligence. So a person who fails to help a drowning person, or the victim of an accident, cannot be held liable for damages under Art. 20.
Quiz trap: Art. 20 = "wilfully OR negligently" + "contrary to law." Art. 21 = "wilfully" only + "contrary to morals, good customs or public policy." Uribe likes to transplant "or negligently" into Art. 21. If he does, the statement is FALSE (bank item P-43). Art. 20 says "shall indemnify the latter for the same." Art. 21 says "shall compensate the latter for the damage." Art. 20 says "causes damage." Art. 21 says "causes loss or injury."

Art. 21: Acts contra bonos mores

Art. 21, NCC. Any person who wilfully causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy shall compensate the latter for the damage.

Elements

  1. An act which is legal (or at least not prohibited by a specific law);
  2. which is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy;
  3. done wilfully (with intent to injure, or with knowledge of its injurious effect);
  4. causing loss or injury to another.
  • Source: German Code Art. 826 ("He who in a manner contrary to good customs wilfully causes injury to another, shall be obliged to indemnify him for the damage caused").
  • Scope (Tolentino): liability may arise from acts that are in themselves legal or not prohibited if they are contrary to morals, good customs, public order or public policy. Art. 21 rests on the principle of Art. 19, with which it is very intimately related. Together with Arts. 19 and 20, it has greatly broadened our law on civil wrongs, which is now more supple than Anglo-American tort law. It is difficult to conceive of any malevolent exercise of a right that these articles could not check.
  • Code Commission's justification: it fills countless gaps in the statutes, which leave many victims of moral wrongs helpless. "Every good law draws its breath of life from morals." Codes and statutes change from age to age, but the conscience of man stays fixed, so it is safe and salutary to transmute moral norms into legal rules. "There is no belief of more baneful consequence upon the social order than that a person may with impunity cause damages to his fellow-men so long as he does not break any law of the State."
  • Loss or injury may relate to any determinate right or property, material or otherwise. It may be caused by an act or by an omission (e.g., the only baker in a locality refusing without cause to sell bread).
  • "Wilfully caused": the act is wilful if done with knowledge of its injurious effect. The act need not be done purposely to produce the injury, nor be directed at the person injured. Example: D burns X's house to prejudice an insurance company, and the insurer can recover from D.
  • Exception (Tolentino): the actor is not liable if the damage results from the inexcusable fault or negligence of the victim, because the actor must have caused the injury. The doctrine of proximate cause applies.
Tolentino (illustrations): German courts held these to be within Art. 21: opening a saloon on one's own property to depress the value of neighboring properties; deliberately manipulating another's finances to acquire his property cheaply; abusing a public office for illicit gain; systematically delaying a case with defenses known to be untenable; failing to correct false information originally given in good faith; manipulating balance sheets to induce share purchases; acquiring property only to prevent another who needs it from getting it; alienating all property to frustrate another's right to support; and fraudulently procuring unjust judgments. On conflicts of interest, a strike, lockout or boycott may be contrary to good customs when based on false assertions, aimed at the total ruin of the other party, done purely for vengeance, or carried out by reprehensible means. Our Supreme Court has awarded moral damages where irrigation water was cut off to force farmers to vacate their landholdings.

Illicit sexual relations: the Code Commission example

The Code Commission's example: "A" seduces the nineteen-year-old daughter of "X." No promise of marriage was made, or it cannot be proved. The girl becomes pregnant. There is no crime because she is above the age covered by the seduction law, and no civil action for breach of promise. Under Art. 21, however, she and her parents would have a cause of action.

Tolentino: Does Art. 21 overturn Batarra v. Marcos (7 Phil. 156), which held that if the carnal intercourse is a crime, the crime is common to both and recovery is barred, and if it is not a crime, it is immoral and both parties are at fault? Tolentino submits that Batarra still subsists. The Commission's example is correct only if there was seduction, not necessarily in the legal sense but in the vulgar sense of deception. When the sexual act is done without any deceit or qualifying circumstance of abuse of authority or influence, and the woman, already of age, has knowingly given herself to a man, there is no injury that can be the basis for indemnity. But so long as there is fraud (wilfulness), the action lies. The court weighs the degree of fraud: an act that would deceive a girl of sixteen may not deceive an experienced woman of thirty. Where there is a wrongful act and a resulting injury, there should be civil liability even if the act is not punishable or the criminal case was dismissed. (This is the line Tanjanco and Baksh later drew.)
People v. Ritter (194 SCRA 690, March 5, 1991)

Ritter, a foreigner, was charged with rape with homicide of Rosario Baluyot, a street child in Olongapo whose death was linked to a foreign object (a vibrator) left inside her. The prosecution failed to prove guilt beyond reasonable doubt. Doctrine: A person not criminally liable may still be civilly liable. The judgment of acquittal extinguishes civil liability only when it includes a declaration that the facts from which civil liability might arise did not exist (Padilla v. CA). Guilt requires proof beyond reasonable doubt, but civil liability requires only a preponderance of evidence (Art. 29). "It is only the constitutional presumption of innocence and the failure of the prosecution to build an airtight case for conviction which saved him, not that the facts of unlawful conduct do not exist." Moral and exemplary damages were awarded to the victim's heirs, who suffered mental anguish and moral shock. Uribe's doctrines outline adds: a person can be held liable even if the act is not punishable under the RPC. The Court granted damages with Art. 21 as basis (acts contrary to morals, good customs, public order or public policy), because pedophilia as such was not yet a crime at the time.

Uribe: Ritter is listed under Human Relations, not under Art. 29 alone. Learn it for two propositions: (1) lack of a penal law does not mean no liability, since Art. 21 covers acts contra bonos mores even when no crime is committed; and (2) acquittal on reasonable doubt does not extinguish civil liability (Arts. 29, 2177; Rule 111, Sec. 2).
Velayo v. Shell Co. of the Philippines (100 Phil. 186, 1956)

A creditor, knowing its debtor was insolvent and that other creditors had agreed to a pro-rata arrangement, secretly assigned its credit to its foreign sister company, which then attached the debtor's airplane abroad, ahead of the other creditors. Doctrine: Arts. 19 and 21 were applied. Taking advantage of the other creditors' trust to grab the debtor's asset is contrary to good faith and good customs and gives rise to damages. (Cited by Tolentino as an application of the human relations articles.)

Spouses Dorao v. Spouses BBB and CCC (2023)

The parents of a boy repeatedly insulted his minor girlfriend for alleged promiscuity, to her face and in front of others, causing her serious emotional harm. Doctrine: A person who debases, degrades or demeans a child's intrinsic worth and dignity as a human being is liable for damages under Arts. 21 and 26. Such conduct is contrary to public policy as expressed in the Constitution, international law and child-protection statutes.

Arts. 22–23: Unjust enrichment (accion in rem verso) and liability without fault

Art. 22, NCC. Every person who through an act of performance by another, or any other means, acquires or comes into possession of something at the expense of the latter without just or legal ground, shall return the same to him.
Quiz trap (Uribe Quiz 1, #6): "Every person who, through an act of performance by another, or any other means, acquires or comes into possession of something at the expense of the latter, shall return the same to him." FALSE. It omits "without just or legal ground." Uribe: "There should be a qualification which is without 'just or legal reason.'" A person who receives payment of a debt actually due, or a donation, has a just ground and need not return anything.
  • Source: German Code Art. 812 (similar provisions in the Swiss, Mexican and Soviet codes). The principle was applied here even before the Code: Bonzon v. Standard Oil Co. (27 Phil. 141), where an execution purchaser who was later evicted because the judgment debtor had no title could recover the price from the judgment creditor.
  • Concept: the action to recover what has been paid or given without just cause is the accion in rem verso. Our Code keeps a separate chapter on quasi-contracts (Arts. 2142–2175) but recognizes that quasi-contract itself rests on unjust enrichment (Art. 2142: "to the end that no one shall be unjustly enriched or benefited at the expense of another"). In practice the two concepts have merged.
  • Code Commission: "It is most needful that this ancient principle be clearly and specifically consecrated in the Civil Code to the end that in cases not foreseen by the lawmaker, no one may unjustly benefit himself to the prejudice of another."

Requisites of accion in rem verso (Tolentino)

  1. The defendant has been enriched;
  2. the plaintiff has suffered a loss;
  3. the enrichment is without just or legal ground; and
  4. the plaintiff has no other action based on contract, quasi-contract, crime or quasi-delict.

Measure of restitution: it must cover the plaintiff's loss but can never exceed the defendant's enrichment, if that is less than the loss. The aim is to restore the patrimonial benefit obtained without cause, giving due regard to the good or bad faith of the defendant.

Tolentino's commentary on each element

  • Enrichment is every patrimonial, physical or moral advantage appreciable in money. It includes enjoying another's thing; benefiting from services; acquiring a right; an increase in the value of property; improving or recognizing a right; improving one's living conditions; and avoiding expenses or preventing a loss (e.g., extinguishing a debt, releasing a burden, or extinguishing a fire threatening the defendant's property). Although Art. 22 speaks of acquiring "something," Tolentino submits it also covers benefits from services, through Arts. 2142–2143.
  • Incapacity of the defendant does not bar the action so long as he was enriched. Example: a teacher may recover from a minor pupil who was "morally and intellectually enriched." A minor who receives a loan acquires ownership of the money, and under Art. 22 must return what he received or the amount of the enrichment. The enrichment must exist when the action is filed, unless the defendant fraudulently alienated the property.
  • Expense of plaintiff: there must be a correlative prejudice, which may be a loss of property, non-payment for a prestation, or failure to acquire something he would have obtained. It is enough that the enrichment would not have occurred without the fact from which the injury derives. The relation must be immediate, though it may be indirect through a mere representative. It is not immediate if an independent third party intervened through his own act.
  • Want of just cause: just cause is presumed, and the plaintiff must prove its absence. Groups: (1) enrichment through the injured party's own act (payment of a non-existent debt; payments above controlled prices; payment under a void contract; payment for a purpose not attained); (2) enrichment without the injured party's will, by the defendant's act (theft) or a third person's act (a debtor paying in good faith the old creditor after the credit was assigned).
  • Natural obligations (Art. 1423) are a sufficient cause. A voluntary payment of one cannot be recovered under Art. 22.
  • Subsidiary character: most writers, supported by jurisprudence (Aubry & Rau, Josserand, Demogue and others), treat the action as subsidiary. If another action is available, use it. A plaintiff who let his contract, quasi-delict or other action prescribe cannot switch to Art. 22. (Planiol & Ripert and Colin & Capitant do not consider the fourth requisite essential.)
  • Permissible enrichment: the law does not forbid all enrichment at another's expense. The enrichment must cause injury. Examples of no unjust enrichment: Juan improves his tenement and Pedro's adjoining land rises in value; Juan builds a riverbank wall that also protects Pedro's land downstream; a usufructuary's improvements accrue to the owner under Art. 579.
  • Illicit contracts: payment under an illicit cause is without just ground. Arts. 1411–1412 (in pari delicto), however, still bar recovery when both parties are at fault. Tolentino criticizes these as contrary in principle to Art. 22, but as special provisions they control.
  • Effects: the Code has no specific rules on accessions, improvements and deteriorations, so Tolentino applies the quasi-contract rules by analogy (Arts. 2159–2161).

Art. 23, NCC. Even when an act or event causing damage to another's property was not due to the fault or negligence of the defendant, the latter shall be liable for indemnity if through the act or event he was benefited.
  • Source: Argentine Code Art. 907, which limits liability "only to the extent of such enrichment."
  • Liability without fault: the traditional rule was "no liability without fault." Modern law has departed from it (cf. Art. 1711 on employers, Art. 1174 on assumption of risk). Art. 23 is a corollary of unjust enrichment.
  • Basis is equity: an involuntary act cannot by its nature create an obligation, but if its author was benefited, it is just that he indemnify. Liability is limited to the extent of his enrichment. It does not include unrealized profits of the injured party, and the plaintiff must prove the extent of the benefit.
  • Code Commission's example: without A's knowledge, a flood drives his cattle onto the cultivated highland of B. A's cattle are saved, but B's crop is destroyed. A was not at fault but was benefited, so it is right that he indemnify B.
Quiz trap: "When an act or event causing damage to another's property was not due to the fault or negligence of the defendant, the latter shall not be liable for indemnity even if through the act or event he was benefited." FALSE (Art. 23, "Even when ... shall be liable"). Also note the coverage: Art. 23 speaks of damage to another's property, not person.
Guevarra v. Banach (2022)

Before the planned marriage, the man gave the woman ₱500,000. She broke off the relationship when she discovered he was still married and using a false name. He sued for damages for breach of promise and to recover the money. Doctrine: The party seeking damages must have acted in good faith, and a party in bad faith cannot recover. The money, given as a gift, was acquired with a legal ground (donation), so it is not subject to the unjust enrichment principle.

Arts. 24–25: Protection of the disadvantaged; thoughtless extravagance

Art. 24, NCC. In all contractual, property or other relations, when one of the parties is at a disadvantage on account of his moral dependence, ignorance, indigence, mental weakness, tender age or other handicap, the courts must be vigilant for his protection.
  • Memorize the six handicaps (plus "other handicap"): moral dependence, ignorance, indigence, mental weakness, tender age, other handicap. It covers "contractual, property or other relations."
  • The Code Commission gives no source, but it resembles German Code Art. 138(2), Swiss Code of Obligations Art. 21 (lesion through exploitation of distress, light-mindedness or inexperience), and Mexican Code Art. 17.
  • Effect (Tolentino): such provisions protect the weak and ignorant against the rigid classical principle of "equality before the law," and qualify the rule that the will of the contracting parties is the supreme law between them. Example: an indemnity agreement signed by an employee in error and under the undue influence of the employer is void as to that employee.
  • Undue influence (Art. 1337) is a concrete application. Among the circumstances considered are the confidential, family, spiritual and other relations between the parties, and whether the person was suffering from mental weakness, was ignorant, or was in financial distress.
  • Ignorance of law: Tolentino suggests Art. 24 is broad enough to let courts follow the Mexican rule, which allows judges, considering the notorious mental deficiency or misery of certain individuals, to exempt them from sanctions of laws they did not know, unless the law directly affects public interest. This is a qualification of the conclusive presumption in Art. 3.
  • Change of circumstances: Tolentino compares the Swiss approach to clausula rebus sic stantibus. Compare our Art. 1267 (release when service becomes so difficult as to be manifestly beyond the contemplation of the parties).
  • Art. 24 is the parens patriae provision. It is a rule of vigilance for courts, not an independent ground to annul contracts.
Art. 25, NCC. Thoughtless extravagance in expenses for pleasure or display during a period of acute public want or emergency may be stopped by order of the courts at the instance of any government or private charitable institution.
  • Formulated by the Code Commission itself.
  • Reason (Tolentino): a person may generally use and consume his property provided he does not injure others. Art. 25 limits that right. When the rich indulge in thoughtless extravagance during acute public want, they may "kindle the flame of unrest" among the poor and demoralize the suffering masses. It reaffirms the police power of the State and promotes social stability.
  • Elements: (1) thoughtless extravagance (2) in expenses for pleasure or display (3) during a period of acute public want or emergency. Remedy: court order stopping it. Who may sue: any government or private charitable institution.
Quiz trap: "may be stopped by order of the courts at the instance of any person" → FALSE (bank P-50). Also false: "at the instance of the State," "of the Solicitor General," "of any government institution" only (it is government OR private charitable). "Acute public want or emergency," not "and." The remedy is to stop the extravagance, not damages.

Art. 26: Dignity, personality, privacy and peace of mind

Art. 26, NCC. Every person shall respect the dignity, personality, privacy and peace of mind of his neighbors and other persons. The following and similar acts, though they may not constitute a criminal offense, shall produce a cause of action for damages, prevention and other relief:
(1) Prying into the privacy of another's residence;
(2) Meddling with or disturbing the private life or family relations of another;
(3) Intriguing to cause another to be alienated from his friends;
(4) Vexing or humiliating another on account of his religious beliefs, lowly station in life, place of birth, physical defect, or other personal condition.
  • Four interests named in the first sentence: dignity, personality, privacy, peace of mind, of "his neighbors and other persons."
  • Remedies: "damages, prevention and other relief" (so injunction is available). The act need not be a crime ("though they may not constitute a criminal offense").
  • The list is illustrative ("the following and similar acts"). The principal rule is the general first sentence.
  • Source: American jurisprudence and Swiss Code Art. 28. Tolentino calls it "perhaps one of the most fruitful sources of litigation," because it incorporated much of American tort law and went farther.
Tolentino (rights protected): (1) personal dignity; (2) personal security; (3) family relations; (4) social intercourse; (5) privacy; and (6) peace of mind.
Dignity: under American law, mere rudeness or lack of consideration was generally not actionable. Our Code goes farther, and abusive, insulting or humiliating language is actionable. Para. (4) supplements the inadequate penal laws on defamation and unjust vexation. In the Code Commission's words, religious freedom does not authorize anyone to heap obloquy on another because of his religion, and "place of birth, physical defect and other personal conditions are too often the pretext of humiliation cast upon persons."
Personal security: the Code in effect incorporates the common-law principles of assault (an intentional, unlawful offer of physical injury creating a well-founded fear of imminent peril, with apparent present ability) and battery (actual infliction of unlawful or unauthorized violence, however slight, including touching clothing or things held).
Family relations: para. (2) creates a statutory action against anyone who meddles with or disturbs another's family relations, including alienation of affection. Its elements are (1) wrongful conduct of the defendant, (2) loss of affection or consortium, and (3) a causal connection. The defendant must be the "pursuer, not merely the pursued." Parents acting in good faith to advise a married child are privileged unless malice is shown. Distant relatives are not presumed in good faith but escape liability if they prove good faith. A stranger may not volunteer advice to a spouse to separate. The plaintiff's consent or connivance bars recovery.
Social intercourse: para. (3), "intriguing," requires malice.
Privacy: the right to be let alone. It covers more than the residence ("similar acts"), including offices from which the public is excluded and correspondence. Publishing a person's photograph for commercial purposes without consent, "shadowing," and disclosing debts or bank accounts are examples of invasion. It is limited for public characters and newsworthy events (a murderer cannot object to publication of his picture) and by a privilege similar to fair comment.
Peace of mind: unlike the common-law rule (damnum absque injuria for purely mental disturbance), disturbance of mental and emotional tranquility is a legal injury in itself under our Code. It is judged by the sensibilities of ordinary persons, not peculiarly susceptible ones, unless the defendant knew of the susceptibility. Example: a false rumor that a woman's son had committed suicide, which made her ill, was actionable.
Concepcion v. CA (2000)

The defendant publicly accused a married man of having an illicit affair with the defendant's own sister-in-law, causing a confrontation and humiliation. Doctrine: Art. 26 protects personal dignity, personality, privacy and peace of mind. The violation of these rights is actionable even if it is not a crime, and moral damages are recoverable (Art. 2219[10]).

St. Louis Realty Corp. v. CA (1984)

A realty firm published an advertisement showing a doctor's house as if it were part of its subdivision, without his consent. It continued even after he protested and only belatedly published a rectification. Doctrine: This violated the owner's privacy and peace of mind under Art. 26, and moral damages were awarded.

Spouses Hing v. Choachuy (2013)

The respondents installed surveillance cameras aimed at the petitioners' property and business premises. Doctrine: Art. 26(1) ("prying into the privacy of another's residence") extends to places where a person has a reasonable expectation of privacy, including business offices where only certain people are allowed to enter. The test is whether there is a reasonable expectation of privacy and whether it was violated.

"Under Art. 26, the following acts, though they may not constitute a criminal offense, shall produce a cause of action, except: (a) prying into the privacy of another's residence; (b) meddling with the private life or family relations of another; (c) intriguing to cause another to be alienated from his friends; (d) vexing or humiliating another on account of his lowly station in life or physical defect; (e) none of the above."

Answer: (e). All four are expressly enumerated, and the list is not even exclusive.

Quiz trap: The four personal conditions in para. (4) are religious beliefs, lowly station in life, place of birth, physical defect, "or other personal condition." Uribe may slip in "political beliefs," which is not in the text (though it is arguably covered by "other personal condition," the statement is not the codal). Para. (3) is "from his friends," not "from his family." Para. (1) is "privacy of another's residence."

Arts. 27–28: Refusal or neglect of a public servant; unfair competition

Art. 27, NCC. Any person suffering material or moral loss because a public servant or employee refuses or neglects, without just cause, to perform his official duty may file an action for damages and other relief against the latter, without prejudice to any disciplinary administrative action that may be taken.
  • Source: German Code Art. 839, per the Code Commission.
  • Reason: "a public office is a public trust." The article is meant to discourage official inaction that pressures ordinary citizens into giving gifts or bribes, and to stop discrimination between those who can bribe and those who cannot.
  • Scope: it covers nonfeasance only, that is, refusal or neglect (inaction). It does not cover malfeasance or misfeasance, which are actionable under Arts. 20, 21 and 32 instead. Per German authority, the action is supplementary and lies only where Arts. 20 and 21 do not apply.
  • Loss may be "material or moral." The civil action is "without prejudice to" administrative discipline, so both may proceed.

Requisites (Tolentino)

  1. The defendant is a public official charged with the performance of official duties;
  2. there is a violation of an official duty in favor of an individual (not a duty owed solely to the State or public);
  3. the violation was wilful or negligent; and
  4. there is an injury to the individual.
Tolentino: The act refused must be one the law absolutely requires. He rejects a literal ministerial/discretionary split. A judge's decision is discretionary in content, but his duty to decide is mandatory, so a judge who leaves a submitted case unacted upon for an unreasonable time without cause may be liable. But where the discretion is whether to act at all (e.g., the Constabulary chief granting a firearms permit), failure to act, even without reason, is not actionable under Art. 27, though Art. 19 may still apply. Duties owed only to the public (e.g., a police officer's failure to arrest a criminal) do not ground individual suits. Excuses: impossibility of performance not due to the officer's fault, and contributory negligence of the plaintiff. Not excuses: a superior's order (except a purely ministerial officer bound to obey), good faith, or absence of malice. These may only mitigate damages.
Art. 28, NCC. Unfair competition in agricultural, commercial or industrial enterprises or in labor through the use of force, intimidation, deceit, machination or any other unjust, oppressive or highhanded method shall give rise to a right of action by the person who thereby suffers damage.
  • A new article formulated by the Code Commission. Its purpose is to preserve competition in a wholesome atmosphere, so that there is no monopoly through unjust means in capital or labor. "Democracy becomes a veritable mockery if any person or group of persons by any unjust or high-handed method may deprive others of a fair chance to engage in business or earn a living."
  • Fields covered: agricultural, commercial, industrial enterprises, labor.
  • Means: force, intimidation, deceit, machination, or any other unjust, oppressive or highhanded method.
  • Characteristics of unfair competition (Tolentino): (1) an injury to a competitor or trade rival, and (2) acts "contrary to good conscience" or otherwise unlawful. The privilege of competition extends only to actual competitors. A wholesaler not engaged in retail who drives a retailer out of business is not a bona fide competitor.
  • Permissible competition: rivals may compete on quality, price, packaging, advertising and hiring agents. Lowering prices is generally fair, but predatory pricing (selling at a loss solely to drive a competitor out, then taking advantage) is "cut-throat competition."
  • Art. 28's concept is broader than "passing off" under trademark law (formerly R.A. 166, now the IP Code). It covers stealing trade secrets, bribing employees, misrepresentation, and malicious interference with contracts or business.
Willaware Products Corp. v. Jesichris Manufacturing Corp. (2014)

A competitor hired away the respondent's employees and used their knowledge to manufacture the same plastic products, selling to the same customers at lower prices to drive the respondent out of the market. Doctrine: Unfair competition under Art. 28 does not require that the act be a crime or a trademark violation. It is enough that there is (1) an injury to a competitor or trade rival, and (2) acts characterized as contrary to good conscience, shocking to judicial sensibilities, or otherwise unlawful. Here the employment of unjust means made the petitioner liable.

Quiz trap: Art. 28 does NOT require that the unfair competition be a crime under the RPC, or punishable under the IP Code (bank P-52). Art. 27 is "without just cause" and "refuses or neglects." It covers "material or moral loss." The action is "without prejudice to" administrative action.

Arts. 29–31: Civil liability and the criminal action

Background: two sources of civil liability for the same act

A single act may give rise to (a) civil liability ex delicto (Art. 100, RPC: "Every person criminally liable for a felony is also civilly liable"; NCC 1161), and (b) civil liability from other sources: law, contract, quasi-contract, quasi-delict (NCC 1157), including Arts. 19–21, 32–34 and 2176. The rules differ depending on which is sued upon.

Art. 29, NCC. When the accused in a criminal prosecution is acquitted on the ground that his guilt has not been proved beyond reasonable doubt, a civil action for damages for the same act or omission may be instituted. Such action requires only a preponderance of evidence. Upon motion of the defendant, the court may require the plaintiff to file a bond to answer for damages in case the complaint should be found to be malicious.
If in a criminal case the judgment of acquittal is based upon reasonable doubt, the court shall so declare. In the absence of any declaration to that effect, it may be inferred from the text of the decision whether or not the acquittal is due to that ground.
  • Elements: (1) the accused is acquitted on reasonable doubt; (2) the civil action is for damages for the same act or omission; (3) it requires only preponderance of evidence.
  • Bond: "Upon motion of the defendant," the court "may" (discretionary) require the plaintiff to file a bond against a malicious complaint.
  • Duty of the criminal court: it "shall so declare" if the acquittal is on reasonable doubt. Absent a declaration, the ground may be inferred from the text of the decision.
  • Reason (Code Commission): the old rule that acquittal also releases the accused from civil liability was "one of the most serious flaws in the Philippine legal system." Criminal and civil liabilities are distinct. One affects the social order and the other private rights; one is for punishment, the other for reparation. Why should the victim's private right have to be proved beyond reasonable doubt just because the wrong is also a crime?
  • Effect on prior jurisprudence (Tolentino): Art. 29 reverses Almeida v. Abaroa (8 Phil. 178; 40 Phil. 1056) insofar as it held that exemption from criminal responsibility carries exemption from civil responsibility.

Tolentino: An acquittal may still bar a civil action on the theory of res judicata. An acquittal because the accused was not the author of the act is "a far cry" from one based on reasonable doubt. The first closes the door to civil liability, and so does a finding that the act did not exist. Even if the parties and objects differ, the point is the same and res judicata applies. On the other hand, in estafa the accused may be acquitted for lack of criminal intent (he believed in good faith the money was a loan), or because the crime prescribed. In those cases a civil action may lie under quasi-delict, Art. 22, or the Rules, even if the acquittal is not on reasonable doubt.
Padilla v. CA (1984)

Doctrine: The judgment of acquittal extinguishes civil liability only when it includes a declaration that the facts from which civil liability might arise did not exist. An accused acquitted on reasonable doubt may be ordered in the same criminal case to pay civil liability proved by preponderance of evidence, without the need for a separate civil action. This avoids multiplicity of suits. (Quoted in Ritter.)

Manantan v. CA (2001)

Doctrine: There are two kinds of acquittal: (1) on the ground that the accused is not the author of the act or omission complained of, which closes the door to civil liability ex delicto; and (2) on reasonable doubt, where a civil action for damages for the same act may still be filed. The civil action based on quasi-delict is distinct and survives either way.

Art. 30, NCC. When a separate civil action is brought to demand civil liability arising from a criminal offense, and no criminal proceedings are instituted during the pendency of the civil case, a preponderance of evidence shall likewise be sufficient to prove the act complained of.
  • A new article, but it merely states an existing rule of evidence in civil cases.
  • Elements: (1) a separate civil action to enforce civil liability arising from a criminal offense (ex delicto); (2) no criminal proceedings instituted during its pendency. Result: preponderance suffices.
  • Tolentino: it is settled that prosecution and conviction are not conditions precedent to a civil action for damages, especially in estafa cases. Art. 100 RPC does not mean one must first be convicted. Once a criminal action is instituted, however, the civil action ex delicto is suspended until the criminal action is finally determined (now Rule 111, Sec. 2).
Art. 31, NCC. When the civil action is based on an obligation not arising from the act or omission complained of as a felony, such civil action may proceed independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter.
  • A new article. It covers civil actions based on an act or omission distinct from the felony charged, or on another source of obligation: contract, quasi-contract, quasi-delict, law.
  • Tolentino's example: X deposits property with Y, who misappropriates it, and Y is prosecuted for estafa. X may sue on the contract of deposit for failure to return. Even if Y is acquitted, the civil action prospers if X proves Y cannot return the things deposited without lawful excuse.
  • It proceeds "independently of the criminal proceedings and regardless of the result of the latter."
  • Quasi-delict: Barredo v. Garcia (1942) and Elcano v. Hill (1977): the same negligent act may be a crime (reckless imprudence) and a quasi-delict. An acquittal, even one declaring that the accused was not negligent for criminal purposes, does not bar an action under Art. 2176, though the plaintiff cannot recover twice (Art. 2177).

A was charged with estafa for failing to return B's car, which he had borrowed. The trial court acquitted A because the evidence did not show deceit or abuse of confidence beyond reasonable doubt, but found he still had not returned the car. Can B still recover?

Answer: Yes. (1) Under Art. 29, the acquittal on reasonable doubt does not bar a civil action for the same act, provable by preponderance of evidence, and the court may even award it in the criminal case (Padilla). (2) Independently, under Art. 31, B may sue on the contract of commodatum, which does not arise from the felony and proceeds regardless of the result of the criminal case.

Art. 32: Violation of civil liberties

Art. 32, NCC. Any public officer or employee, or any private individual, who directly or indirectly obstructs, defeats, violates or in any manner impedes or impairs any of the following rights and liberties of another person shall be liable to the latter for damages:
(1) Freedom of religion;
(2) Freedom of speech;
(3) Freedom to write for the press or to maintain a periodical publication;
(4) Freedom from arbitrary or illegal detention;
(5) Freedom of suffrage;
(6) The right against deprivation of property without due process of law;
(7) The right to a just compensation when private property is taken for public use;
(8) The right to the equal protection of the laws;
(9) The right to be secure in one's person, house, papers, and effects against unreasonable searches and seizures;
(10) The liberty of abode and of changing the same;
(11) The privacy of communication and correspondence;
(12) The right to become a member of associations or societies for purposes not contrary to law;
(13) The right to take part in a peaceable assembly to petition the Government for redress of grievances;
(14) The right to be free from involuntary servitude in any form;
(15) The right of the accused against excessive bail;
(16) The right of the accused to be heard by himself and counsel, to be informed of the nature and cause of the accusation against him, to have a speedy and public trial, to meet the witnesses face to face, and to have compulsory process to secure the attendance of witness in his behalf;
(17) Freedom from being compelled to be a witness against one's self, or from being forced to confess guilt, or from being induced by a promise of immunity or reward to make such confession, except when the person confessing becomes a State witness;
(18) Freedom from excessive fines, or cruel and unusual punishment, unless the same is imposed or inflicted in accordance with a statute which has not been judicially declared unconstitutional; and
(19) Freedom of access to the courts.
In any of the cases referred to in this article, whether or not the defendant's act or omission constitutes a criminal offense, the aggrieved party has a right to commence an entirely separate and distinct civil action for damages, and for other relief. Such civil action shall proceed independently of any criminal prosecution (if the latter be instituted), and may be proved by a preponderance of evidence.
The indemnity shall include moral damages. Exemplary damages may also be adjudicated.
The responsibility herein set forth is not demandable from a judge unless his act or omission constitutes a violation of the Penal Code or other penal statute.

Key features

  • Who is liable: "any public officer or employee, or any private individual." A private person can be liable under Art. 32.
  • How: "directly or indirectly obstructs, defeats, violates or in any manner impedes or impairs." Superiors who ordered or tolerated the violation are covered (Aberca).
  • Nineteen rights (mirroring the 1935 Bill of Rights).
  • Nature of action: "whether or not" the act is a crime, an "entirely separate and distinct civil action for damages, and for other relief," which "shall proceed independently of any criminal prosecution (if the latter be instituted)" and "may be proved by a preponderance of evidence."
  • Damages: the indemnity "shall include moral damages" (mandatory component). Exemplary damages "may also be adjudicated" (discretionary).
  • Judges: responsibility is not demandable from a judge unless his act or omission constitutes a violation of the Penal Code or other penal statute. Other public officers and private persons are liable even if their act is not a crime.
  • Source: American jurisprudence. Code Commission's reasons: (1) threats to freedom usually come from abuse of power by officials and police, and citizens depended on fiscals who were overburdened or reluctant to prosecute fellow officials; (2) proof beyond reasonable doubt often prevented punishment; (3) subtle, indirect violations not reached by the penal law are the greatest danger to democracy.
Tolentino (malice not necessary): It is not necessary that the defendant acted with malice or bad faith. Making it a requisite would defeat the article's main purpose, the effective protection of individual rights. Officials in the past abused their powers on the pretext of justifiable motives or good faith, and the object of the article is to put an end to the plea of good faith. Limited liability of judges: this follows Art. 839 of the German Code. Tolentino also notes that para. (15) seems only to give an action when the bail fixed is excessive, but with more reason the denial of bail itself should be actionable, reading Art. 32 with Arts. 19 and 20.
Aberca v. Ver (G.R. No. 69866, April 15, 1988)

Members of Task Force Makabansa, under orders of General Ver and other officers, raided houses without warrants, seized belongings, and detained and allegedly tortured the occupants. The victims sued the officers and the soldiers under Art. 32. The officers invoked immunity, the suspension of the privilege of the writ of habeas corpus, and lack of personal participation. Doctrine: (1) The suspension of the privilege of the writ does not destroy the right to sue for damages for violation of constitutional rights. It suspends only the remedy of habeas corpus, not other rights. (2) Officers are not immune from suit for acts done in excess of authority that violate constitutional rights. (3) Art. 32 makes liable not only the person who directly violates the right but also anyone who indirectly obstructs, defeats or impairs it. A superior officer who orders, abets or tolerates the violation, by acts or omissions, is liable. The doctrine of respondeat superior does not apply as such, but liability attaches under the words "directly or indirectly." (4) Good faith is not a defense, since Art. 32 does not require malice. Those who acted in bad faith may additionally be liable for exemplary damages.

Lim v. Ponce de Leon (1975)

A provincial fiscal ordered the seizure of a motor launch without a search warrant. Doctrine: The seizure violated the right against unreasonable searches and seizures, and the officers are liable under Art. 32. Good faith is not a defense, because the article does not require malice or bad faith. Bad faith is relevant only to the award of exemplary damages.

Vinzons-Chato v. Fortune Tobacco Corp. (2007)

The Commissioner of Internal Revenue reclassified the respondent's cigarette brands, subjecting them to a higher tax, without notice and hearing. Doctrine: Art. 32 is a special provision on liability for violation of constitutional rights, so it prevails over the general rule exempting public officers who act in good faith in the performance of their duties (Admin. Code). Under Art. 32 the plaintiff need not allege or prove malice or bad faith. The complaint stated a cause of action for violation of the right against deprivation of property without due process (Art. 32[6]).

Pursuant to General V's orders, military agents raided several houses without warrants, seized belongings, and detained the occupants. Sued under Art. 32, General V says he did not personally take part and acted in good faith in the performance of official duty. Should the suit against him be dismissed?

Answer: No (Aberca v. Ver; Lim v. Ponce de Leon). Art. 32 holds liable any public officer who "directly or indirectly" violates or impairs the enumerated rights, and the superior who ordered or tolerated the acts is indirectly responsible. Good faith is not a defense. The civil action is independent of any criminal prosecution and needs only a preponderance of evidence.

Judge J, through gross ignorance but without any criminal intent, issued an order that led to X's illegal detention. Can X sue J for damages under Art. 32?

Answer: No. Under Art. 32, last paragraph, the responsibility "is not demandable from a judge unless his act or omission constitutes a violation of the Penal Code or other penal statute" (e.g., knowingly rendering an unjust judgment or order). Without a penal violation, the judge is not liable under Art. 32, though administrative sanctions may apply. A sheriff or police officer who carried out the detention is not so protected.

Quiz trap: Common alterations: "judge ... liable even if his act does not constitute a violation of the Penal Code" (FALSE); "the indemnity may include moral damages" (FALSE, it is "shall"); "exemplary damages shall be adjudicated" (FALSE, "may"); "must be proved beyond reasonable doubt" (FALSE); "only public officers are liable" (FALSE, private individuals too); "only those who directly violate" (FALSE, "directly or indirectly"); "requires bad faith" (FALSE).

Arts. 33–35: Defamation, fraud and physical injuries; police; no prosecution

Art. 33, NCC. In cases of defamation, fraud, and physical injuries a civil action for damages, entirely separate and distinct from the criminal action, may be brought by the injured party. Such civil action shall proceed independently of the criminal prosecution, and shall require only a preponderance of evidence.
  • Three cases: defamation, fraud, physical injuries.
  • Who sues: "the injured party." The action is "entirely separate and distinct" and "shall proceed independently," requiring "only a preponderance of evidence."
  • Source: American principles. The Code Commission wanted to foster individual self-reliance in enforcing one's rights, instead of depending on the fiscal. In England and the US, a person may sue in tort for assault, battery, libel, slander, deceit and malicious prosecution, independent of the criminal statutes.
  • The terms are used in their generic sense. "Fraud" includes estafa and swindling. "Defamation" includes libel, slander and slander by deed. "Physical injuries" means bodily injury, not the crime of physical injuries as defined in the RPC, so it includes attempted or frustrated homicide and even death (Tolentino; Madeja v. Caro, 1983).
  • Injuries from negligence (reckless imprudence) are better pursued as a quasi-delict (Art. 2176) independent action under Rule 111, Sec. 3.
Tolentino (scope): The general rule is that when the criminal action is instituted, the civil action ex delicto is impliedly instituted with it, and after the criminal action is commenced no separate civil action can be prosecuted. Art. 33 creates an exception: the civil action may be filed independently even without reservation, since the law itself makes the reservation. The claimant does not, however, get to decide whether the civil action is suspended. Suppose the civil action fails for insufficient evidence but the criminal case, tried later, results in conviction. Tolentino submits that no civil liability can then be imposed in the criminal case. The law separated the civil action, which ceased to be involved in the criminal case from the moment it was filed. Even without separation, the civil judgment is res judicata on civil liability. The same applies to all independent civil actions (Arts. 32, 34).
Madeja v. Caro (1983)

A doctor was charged with homicide through reckless imprudence for the death of a patient, and the widow filed a separate civil action. Doctrine: "Physical injuries" in Art. 33 is used in its generic sense and includes death.   • Formulated by the Code Commission.
  • Situation: (1) a person claims injury from a crime and charges another; (2) no independent civil action is granted for that offense; (3) the investigating judge (formerly the justice of the peace) finds no reasonable ground, or the prosecutor refuses or fails to prosecute. Result: the complainant may bring a civil action for damages, provable by preponderance. A bond is available "upon the defendant's motion."
  • Second paragraph: if an information is later filed during the pendency of the civil action, it is the CIVIL action that is suspended until the termination of the criminal proceedings.
  • Tolentino: the first paragraph is not limited to offenses without an independent civil action. With more reason the right exists for offenses that have one. It mentions only offenses without one because the other provisions already cover the rest. The second paragraph, however, applies exclusively to offenses without an independent civil action. If the offense has one (Arts. 32, 33, 34), filing the criminal case does not suspend the civil action.

Art. 36: Prejudicial (pre-judicial) questions

Art. 36, NCC. Pre-judicial questions, which must be decided before any criminal prosecution may be instituted or may proceed, shall be governed by rules of court which the Supreme Court shall promulgate and which shall not be in conflict with the provisions of this Code.
Rule 111, Sec. 6, Rules of Court. Suspension by reason of prejudicial question. — A petition for suspension of the criminal action based upon the pendency of a prejudicial question in a civil action may be filed in the office of the prosecutor or the court conducting the preliminary investigation. When the criminal action has been filed in court for trial, the petition to suspend shall be filed in the same criminal action at any time before the prosecution rests.
Rule 111, Sec. 7, Rules of Court. Elements of prejudicial question. — The elements of a prejudicial question are: (a) the previously instituted civil action involves an issue similar or intimately related to the issue raised in the subsequent criminal action, and (b) the resolution of such issue determines whether or not the criminal action may proceed.

Concept

  • Definition (Donato v. Luna): a prejudicial question is one that arises in a case, the resolution of which is a logical antecedent of the issue involved in that case, and the cognizance of which pertains to another tribunal. It is based on a fact distinct and separate from the crime but so intimately connected with it that it determines the guilt or innocence of the accused.
  • For it to suspend the criminal action, the civil case must involve facts intimately related to those on which the criminal prosecution is based, and the resolution of the issues in the civil case must necessarily determine the guilt or innocence of the accused. However the civil issue is resolved, it should be "determinative juris et de jure" of guilt or innocence.
  • Tolentino: a pre-judicial question is raised in a criminal case by the accused and is of such a nature that, if decided in his favor in a civil case, the supposed crime disappears. It must be determinative of the case, and jurisdiction to try it must be lodged in another tribunal. Hence the criminal case is suspended. Examples: (1) theft of a book where the accused claims he owns it and only lent it to the complainant, so ownership is pre-judicial; (2) bigamy, where the accused claims his first marriage is void (see the warning below).

Elements (memorize: Rule 111, Sec. 7)

  1. The previously instituted civil action involves an issue similar or intimately related to the issue raised in the subsequent criminal action; and
  2. the resolution of such issue determines whether or not the criminal action may proceed.

Older formulations (and Tolentino) add a third: (3) jurisdiction to try the question is lodged in another tribunal.

Procedure and effect

  • Where: (a) during preliminary investigation, in the office of the prosecutor or the court conducting the preliminary investigation; (b) once the case is filed in court for trial, in the same criminal action, at any time before the prosecution rests.
  • How: a "petition for suspension" (motion to suspend).
  • Effect: suspension only of the criminal proceedings, not dismissal and not acquittal.
  • It arises only between a civil and a criminal action. The civil action must have been previously instituted. A civil case filed after the criminal case does not qualify (Dreamwork Construction v. Janiola, 2009; Pimentel v. Pimentel, 2010).

Tolentino ("present article unnecessary"): Tolentino doubts the need for Art. 36. In the theft example, nothing prevents the accused from proving ownership in the criminal case itself, and in bigamy, nothing prevents him from proving the first marriage void there. Donato v. Luna (160 SCRA 441, April 15, 1988)

Donato was charged with bigamy for marrying Paz Abayan while his first marriage subsisted. Before that, Abayan had filed a civil action to annul her marriage to Donato on the ground that her consent was obtained through deceit (he concealed his prior marriage). Donato then moved to suspend the bigamy case on the ground of prejudicial question, later claiming his own consent was obtained through force and intimidation. Doctrine: No prejudicial question. (1) The issue of the nullity of the second marriage, raised by the second wife on the ground of deceit, is not determinative of Donato's guilt or innocence in bigamy. (2) Following Landicho v. Relova, an annulment case can be a prejudicial question to bigamy only if it is proved that the accused's consent to the second marriage was obtained by duress, violence and intimidation, to show that his act was involuntary and cannot be the basis of conviction. Donato did not show this. (3) The claim was a mere ploy to delay prosecution: he had lived with Abayan as husband and wife for at least five years before the second marriage (they even executed an affidavit of cohabitation), so his claim of force was belied, and it was the second wife, not he, who filed the civil action.

Landicho v. Relova (22 SCRA 731, 1968)

Landicho was charged with bigamy. His first wife sued to declare their marriage void, alleging she was forced into it. Landicho also filed a third-party complaint against his second wife, seeking to annul the second marriage on the ground that his own consent was obtained through force and intimidation. He moved to suspend the bigamy case. Doctrine: No prejudicial question. Parties to a marriage should not be permitted to judge for themselves its nullity, which must be submitted to the judgment of competent courts. So long as there is no such declaration, the presumption is that the marriage exists. "He who contracts a second marriage before the judicial declaration of nullity of the first marriage assumes the risk of being prosecuted for bigamy." It also recognized that annulment of the second marriage could be prejudicial only where the accused's own consent was obtained by force or intimidation.

Note: Landicho's "assumes the risk" line was carried into Art. 40, FC and later cases (Mercado v. Tan, 2000; Jarillo v. People, 2009; Capili v. People, 2013) to hold that the nullity of the first marriage is no defense unless judicially declared before the second marriage. Pulido v. People (2021) abandoned this line as to void ab initio marriages. Landicho remains good law for the point Uribe tests: annulment (voidable) of the first marriage, and annulment of the second at the instance of the second spouse, are not prejudicial.
Merced v. Diez (1960)

Merced filed an action to annul his second marriage on the ground that his consent was obtained through force, threats and intimidation by the second wife and her relatives. The second wife then filed a bigamy complaint against him. Doctrine: The annulment action is a prejudicial question. One element of bigamy is that the accused freely and voluntarily contracted the second marriage. If his consent was vitiated by force or intimidation, the act would be involuntary and he cannot be held guilty of bigamy. The validity of the second marriage on that ground is for the civil court, so the criminal case must be suspended. (Same holding: Zapanta v. Montesa, 1962.)

Tenebro v. CA (423 SCRA 272, February 18, 2004)

Tenebro, while married to Villareyes, married Ancajas. That second marriage was later declared void on the ground of psychological incapacity. He argued there was no bigamy because the second marriage was void. Doctrine: The declaration of nullity of the second marriage on the ground of psychological incapacity is not an indicator that it lacked the essential requisites for validity. All the essential and formal requisites were present. Although a judicial declaration of nullity under Art. 36, FC retroacts to the celebration insofar as the vinculum is concerned, the marriage is not without legal effects: children conceived or born before the judgment are legitimate. One of those consequences is incurring criminal liability for bigamy. To hold otherwise would render the penal laws on bigamy nugatory and let people deliberately flaw each marriage. "The nullity of a second marriage is not per se an argument for the avoidance of criminal liability for bigamy." So an Art. 36 petition on the second marriage is not a prejudicial question to bigamy.

Pulido v. People (G.R. No. 220149, July 27, 2021)

Pulido married Arcon, and while that marriage subsisted, married Baleda. Charged with bigamy, he argued that the first marriage was void ab initio for lack of a marriage license, and pending the case both marriages were judicially declared void. Doctrine (En Banc): (1) Art. 40, FC requires a judicial declaration of nullity of a previous void marriage only for purposes of remarriage. It was not intended to amend Art. 349, RPC. (2) A void ab initio marriage is void from the beginning. It is not "valid until declared void," which is the rule for voidable marriages. (3) A judicial declaration of nullity, even one obtained after the second marriage or after the filing of the bigamy case, retroacts to the celebration of the marriage. (4) Hence the accused may raise the void ab initio nature of the first marriage as a defense in the bigamy case, even without a prior judicial declaration, and may present evidence of its nullity there. Contrary rulings (e.g., Mercado v. Tan, Jarillo) were abandoned. (5) Bigamy also requires that the second marriage have all the essential requisites for validity, i.e., that it would have been valid were it not for the subsisting first marriage. So the accused may also show that the second marriage is void on a ground other than its being bigamous. Pulido was acquitted. The second marriage remains void under Art. 40 for civil purposes, and Art. 40 still applies to remarriage.

Beltran v. People (2000)

A husband charged with concubinage moved to suspend the case because of his pending petition to declare his marriage void under Art. 36, FC. Doctrine: No prejudicial question. A party cannot judge for himself the nullity of his marriage, and the Art. 40 judicial declaration requirement applies. Even if the marriage is later declared void, he is presumed married while cohabiting with his paramour. He who cohabits with another before a judicial declaration assumes the risk of prosecution for concubinage. (Decided before Pulido, but it concerns concubinage, where the marriage at the time of the act is what counts.)

Pimentel v. Pimentel (2010)

The husband was charged with frustrated parricide. Weeks later, the wife filed a petition to declare the marriage void under Art. 36, and the husband moved to suspend. Doctrine: No prejudicial question. (1) The civil action must be previously instituted, and here it was filed after the criminal case. (2) The issue is not determinative: relationship is an element of parricide, and at the time of the attack the parties were married. A later declaration of nullity would not change that for purposes of the crime.

Dreamwork Construction v. Janiola (2009)

Doctrine: Under Rule 111, Sec. 7 (2000 Rules), the civil action must be previously instituted. A civil case for rescission filed after the B.P. 22 informations is not a prejudicial question. The change is consistent with Art. 36, which leaves the matter to the Rules of Court, and it prevents delaying tactics.

People v. Carmenforte (2021, as reported in the B29 repository)

A final civil judgment upheld the genuineness of signatures on deeds of sale. The accused was charged with falsification for allegedly forging those signatures. Doctrine: Res judicata did not apply because the People was not a party to the civil case. But the civil finding was a prejudicial question that negated the element of falsification, and the dismissal of the criminal cases was affirmed. A prejudicial question exists when the civil issue is a logical antecedent of the criminal issue and determines whether the criminal case may proceed.

Note: The B29 case repository describes Pulido v. People as "an example of settling pre-judicial questions." That is imprecise. Pulido was decided on the merits of the bigamy defense: the accused may prove the nullity of a void marriage in the criminal case itself, and the retroactive judicial declaration negates an element. It did not hinge on a motion to suspend. Also, B29 suggests "pre-judicial" and "prejudicial" questions are different. They are the same concept: Art. 36 uses the hyphenated spelling, Rule 111 does not.

Quiz trap: The Statute of Frauds covers an agreement made in consideration of marriage, other than a mutual promise to marry. A mutual promise to marry may be proved orally (it is outside the Statute). A promise by a parent to give a dowry or to build a house in consideration of the marriage must be in writing. The rule and its basis

  • General rule: a mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong. No action for damages lies purely for the breach, and marriage cannot be compelled by specific performance, since consent to the actual marriage must be freely given (FC 2[2]).
  • History: the Code Commission's draft contained a chapter (ten articles) on "Promise of Marriage," which provided for material and moral damages for breach and determined who may sue and who may be liable. Congress deliberately eliminated the whole chapter. The Senate Special Committee cited the US experience that breach of promise suits are prone to abuse by "designing women and unscrupulous men" (the "heart balm" statutes being abolished in many states). It reaffirmed De Jesus v. Syquia (58 Phil. 866, 1933): "the action for breach of promise to marry has no standing in the civil law, apart from the right to recover money or property advanced by the plaintiff upon the faith of such promise." The Family Code did not change this.
  • Exceptions (where damages are recoverable): not because of the breach itself, but because of an independent wrong:
    1. Art. 21 / Art. 19: the breach is accompanied by acts palpably and unjustifiably contrary to good customs, e.g., walking out on a formally set and publicized wedding (Wassmer), or abusive, capricious withdrawal;
    2. Moral seduction / fraud: the promise was a deceptive device to obtain the woman's consent to sexual intercourse (Baksh; cf. Tanjanco for the limits);
    3. Recovery of money or property advanced on the faith of the promise (De Jesus v. Syquia; Domalagan, unjust enrichment under Art. 22); return of conditional gifts;
    4. Actual damages caused by fault, e.g., inducing the woman to resign her job (Garcia v. Del Rosario, 33 Phil. 189, quasi-delict); the expenses of pregnancy and childbirth and support of the child (Hermosisima).
Tolentino (contract to marry): When parties agree to marry in the future there is a contract to marry (the engagement), a purely executory contract. It differs from ordinary contracts because neither party's promise can be enforced by court action: consent to the marriage itself must be purely voluntary. No form or ceremony is needed. A promise to marry needs only to be made so that the injured party may reasonably believe it, judging from the antecedents, the persistence and repetition of the offer, and the like. A request to postpone the marriage for reasonable cause is not a repudiation. A party who develops a disease making it unsafe to marry may have the ceremony postponed. There is repudiation when one declares he will not go through with it, refuses to communicate, or puts himself in a position where he cannot perform (as by marrying another).
Damages: an action based purely on breach of the contract to marry will not lie. A young woman whose suitor changes his mind, absent seduction or abuse of right, has no cause of action, even if she suffers wounded feelings and mental anguish (elements of moral damages under Art. 2217), because there must first be a right of action. But if the action is based on tort or quasi-delict, or on Arts. 19, 21 or 22, there is a legal basis.
Seduction: Tolentino criticizes old cases (e.g., Batarra v. Marcos) for treating carnal intercourse and the contract to marry as identical. The sexual relation is apart from the contract and is rarely its consideration. If the promise was the means of accomplishing the seduction, the woman may recover not on the promise but on the tortious seduction, a breach of trust "as plain as any advantage gained by a trustee." The essence of the action is the wrong accomplished through the deceitful promise.
Abuse of right: even without seduction, under Art. 19 a party who repudiates capriciously may be liable, especially just before the wedding and after a long engagement. "Where a woman has been wantonly deserted after a long engagement ... the result to her feelings ... are all matters to be considered."
Unjust enrichment: Art. 22 is another basis, essentially the basis of Domalagan v. Bolifer. Gifts to a fiancée are generally conditional on the marriage and recoverable on breach by the donee, particularly the engagement ring and jewelry. Absolute gifts (e.g., Christmas presents) are not recoverable.
Oral agreements: Tolentino thinks writing is not necessary in an action for damages for breach, because the Statute of Frauds should apply only when the action is to enforce the contract, and it is unjust to let the party who received the consideration hide behind it.
Cabague v. Auxilio (92 Phil. 294, November 26, 1952)

Felipe Cabague and his son Geronimo sued Matias Auxilio and his daughter Socorro. The defendants had promised that Socorro would marry Geronimo if the Cabagues improved the Auxilios' house and spent for the wedding, which they did. Socorro then refused to marry Geronimo. The trial court dismissed under the Statute of Frauds. Doctrine: There were two kinds of agreement: (1) the agreement between Felipe Cabague and the defendants in consideration of the marriage, and (2) the agreement between the two lovers, a mutual promise to marry. For breach of the mutual promise, Geronimo may sue Socorro for damages, and evidence of that oral promise is admissible since it is excepted from the Statute of Frauds. But Felipe's action may not prosper, because it seeks to enforce an agreement in consideration of marriage, which must be in writing. As between Felipe and Matias, the action cannot be maintained on the theory of "mutual promise to marry," nor may it be regarded as an action by Felipe against Socorro on a mutual promise to marry.

Domalagan v. Bolifer (33 Phil. 471, February 8, 1916)

The plaintiff paid the defendant ₱516 in consideration of the defendant's promise that his daughter would marry the plaintiff's son. The daughter married another man. The plaintiff sued to recover the money, and the defendant invoked the Statute of Frauds. Doctrine: Verbal agreements made upon the consideration of marriage, other than a mutual promise to marry, are not invalid for not being in writing. The law only says such agreements cannot be proved in court if not in writing. Since the defendant did not object to the oral evidence, he waived the Statute (cf. Art. 1405, ratification by failure to object). The defendant was ordered to return the ₱516. Tolentino reads the case as essentially resting on unjust enrichment (now Art. 22).

Hermosisima v. CA (109 Phil. 629, 1960)

Soledad Cagigas, a school teacher, had intimate relations with Francisco Hermosisima, an apprentice pilot about ten years her junior, who promised to marry her. She became pregnant and gave birth, but he married another woman. The CA awarded moral damages for breach of promise. Doctrine: The SC deleted the moral damages. A mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong: Congress deliberately eliminated from the draft Code the provisions that would have made it so, and an action for breach of promise has no standing apart from the right to recover money or property advanced on the faith of the promise (De Jesus v. Syquia). Damages could be claimed if seduction were involved, but here, given that she was older than he by about ten years and a mature woman, it could not be said that he seduced her. The award of actual damages (pregnancy and delivery expenses) and support for the child was maintained.

Wassmer v. Velez (12 SCRA 648, December 26, 1964)

Beatriz Wassmer and Francisco Velez applied for a marriage license and set their wedding. Invitations were printed and distributed, the gown, trousseau and reception were prepared, and bridal showers were given. Two days before the wedding, Velez left a note: "Will have to postpone wedding — My mother opposes it." The next day he wired that nothing had changed and he would return. He never appeared. Doctrine: Mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong. But "to formally set a wedding and go through all the above-described preparation and publicity, only to walk out of it when the matrimony is about to be solemnized, is quite different. This is palpably and unjustifiably contrary to good customs for which defendant must be held answerable in damages in accordance with Article 21." Actual damages (wedding expenses), moral damages (Art. 2219[10]) and exemplary damages were awarded, though the Court reduced the moral and exemplary damages as excessive. The defense that the judgment was by default and that he was not given a chance to settle was rejected.

Estremos v. Ephan (CA, 83 O.G. 4022, No. 35)

Doctrine (per Uribe's doctrines outline): A mere breach of promise to marry is not an actionable wrong. Congress deliberately eliminated from the draft of the new Civil Code the provisions that would have made it so. The SC has, however, laid down exceptions, as in Wassmer v. Velez: under Art. 21, formally setting a wedding and going through the preparation and publicity, only to walk out when the marriage is about to be solemnized, is different from a mere breach. It is palpably and unjustifiably contrary to good customs, making the defendant answerable in damages under Art. 21.

Tanjanco v. CA (18 SCRA 994, December 17, 1966)

Tanjanco courted Araceli Santos, a woman of legal age, and promised to marry her. She consented to sexual relations and they had repeated intercourse for about a year. She became pregnant, had to resign from her job, and suffered mental anguish, and he refused to marry her. Doctrine: No cause of action under Art. 21. Seduction connotes essentially the idea of deceit, enticement, superior power or abuse of confidence on the part of the seducer, to which the woman yields against her will. "In an action by the woman, the enticement, persuasion or deception is the essence of the injury; and a mere proof of intercourse is insufficient to warrant a recovery." A woman of adult age who maintained intimate relations for a year, with repeated acts, shows a mutual passion incompatible with seduction. The Code Commission's example in Art. 21 refers to seduction, not mere sexual intercourse or breach of promise.

Baksh v. CA (G.R. No. 97326, February 19, 1993)

Gashem Shookat Baksh, a foreign medical student, courted Marilou Gonzales, a 22-year-old restaurant waitress, and promised to marry her. Relying on the promise, she lived with him and surrendered her virginity, and her parents agreed to the live-in because of the promised marriage. He later maltreated her and repudiated the promise. Doctrine: Where a man's promise to marry is in fact the proximate cause of the woman's acceptance of his love, and his representation that he will fulfill it is the proximate cause of her giving herself to him in sexual congress, proof that he had no intention of marrying her and that the promise was only a subtle scheme or deceptive device to entice her could justify damages under Art. 21, "not because of such promise to marry but because of the fraud and deceit behind it and the willful injury to her honor and reputation which followed thereafter." The injury must have been committed in a manner contrary to morals, good customs or public policy. She surrendered her virginity "not because of lust but because of moral seduction," the kind illustrated by the Code Commission's example. He could not be held liable for criminal seduction under RPC 337 or 338 because she was above 18. In pari delicto does not apply because her fault, if any, was not equal to his. She was the victim of his deceit and was not in equal fault. Moral damages were awarded.

Uribe (doctrines outline summary): "Prior decisions of this Court clearly suggest that Article 21 may be applied in a breach of promise to marry where the woman is a victim of moral seduction." The progression to memorize is: Hermosisima (no action for mere breach; no seduction where the woman is older) → Wassmer (walking out on a publicized wedding is contra bonos mores, so Art. 21 applies) → Tanjanco (mutual passion is not seduction, so no recovery) → Baksh (fraudulent promise as a device for moral seduction, so Art. 21 applies).
Garcia v. Del Rosario (33 Phil. 189)

The plaintiff, a school teacher, resigned her position at the defendant's instance, relying on his promise of marriage, which he then broke. Doctrine: The defendant was held liable for the damage caused by her loss of employment. Liability rests not on the breach of the contract to marry as such but on his fault in inducing her to resign (cf. Art. 2176). (Cited by Tolentino and in B29: the breach directly led to the injured party's loss of employment.)

Bunag, Jr. v. CA (1992)

After an abduction, the man and the woman applied for a marriage license, but he later withdrew his application and refused to marry her. The criminal case for abduction had been dismissed. Doctrine: While a mere breach of promise is not actionable, the circumstances, including the abduction and deflowering followed by the deceitful promise and its repudiation, made the man liable for moral and exemplary damages under Art. 21. Dismissal of the criminal case did not bar the civil liability.

Abanag v. Mabute (2011)

Doctrine: Voluntary intimacy between two unmarried, consenting adults, neither under any impediment to marry and with no deceit, is neither criminal nor so grossly immoral as to warrant administrative sanction. (Related: no "seduction" without deceit.)

B and F set their wedding date. Invitations were distributed, the gown and reception arranged, and bridal showers given. Two days before, F left a note saying his mother opposed the wedding, then wired that "nothing changed," but never showed up. B sues for damages. Which is correct? (a) B cannot recover since breach of promise is not actionable; (b) B may recover only expenses as damages for breach of contract; (c) B may recover actual, moral and exemplary damages under Art. 21; (d) B may compel F to marry her.

Answer: (c). Wassmer v. Velez. The award is based on Art. 21 (walking out of a formally set and publicized wedding is palpably contrary to good customs), not on the breach of contract as such. Marriage can never be compelled.

Statements: (1) "A promise to marry has obligatory force; hence its breach is per se an actionable wrong." (2) "A prospective bride or groom who capriciously breaks an engagement without reason, causing moral and material injury, may be held liable for damages, especially where the break occurs just before the wedding."

Answer: No. 1 is FALSE and No. 2 is TRUE (Tolentino; Arts. 19 and 21; Wassmer).

Felipe and his son Geronimo orally agreed with Matias and his daughter Socorro that Socorro would marry Geronimo if Felipe repaired Matias's house. Felipe spent ₱700. Socorro backed out. Felipe and Geronimo sue. Matias objects to oral evidence under the Statute of Frauds. Rule.

Answer: Cabague v. Auxilio. (1) Geronimo's claim against Socorro rests on a mutual promise to marry, which is excepted from Art. 1403(2)(c), so oral evidence is admissible and he may sue. (2) Felipe's claim rests on an agreement in consideration of marriage, which is unenforceable unless in writing, so with a timely objection his oral evidence is inadmissible. Had Matias failed to object, the defect would have been waived (Domalagan). Felipe may also explore recovery of what he spent under Art. 22 (unjust enrichment), per Tolentino.

X, 25, and Y, 28, both single, had a sexual relationship for two years. Y repeatedly promised to marry X but never set a date. Y then married another woman. X sues Y under Art. 21. Will it prosper?

Answer: As a rule, no (Tanjanco; Hermosisima). A mere breach of promise is not actionable. Voluntary intercourse between adults over a long period indicates mutual passion, not seduction, because there is no deceit, enticement, superior power or abuse of confidence. It would be different if X proves that Y never intended to marry her and used the promise only as a device to obtain her consent (Baksh), or that Y's withdrawal was attended by acts contrary to good customs (Wassmer). Support for any common child and actual expenses are separate matters.

More application drills (Uribe style)

True or False: "Every person who, contrary to law, wilfully or negligently causes damage to another, shall indemnify the latter for the same." / "Any person who wilfully or negligently causes loss or injury to another in a manner that is contrary to morals, good customs or public policy shall compensate the latter for the damage."

Answer: The first is TRUE (Art. 20 verbatim). The second is FALSE because Art. 21 requires a wilful act. "Or negligently" is not in Art. 21.

True or False: "The classical rule that he who uses a right injures no one is maintained in the Civil Code." / "Under Art. 19, the absence of good faith is essential to abuse of right."

Answer: The first is FALSE. The Code abandoned qui jure suo utitur neminem laedit, and only the legitimate exercise of a right (with prudence and good faith) is protected. The second is TRUE (Tolentino).

During a severe typhoon emergency, a wealthy family throws a lavish week-long fireworks party broadcast online. A neighbor, a private individual, asks the court to stop it. Will it prosper?

Answer: Not at the neighbor's instance. Art. 25 allows thoughtless extravagance for pleasure or display during acute public want or emergency to be stopped by court order only "at the instance of any government or private charitable institution." The neighbor may sue only if he has his own cause of action, e.g., under Art. 26 or as a nuisance.

A mayor, without just cause, refuses for six months to act on X's complete application for a business permit, which by ordinance must be issued upon compliance. X loses a supply contract. Remedy?

Answer: An action for damages and other relief against the mayor under Art. 27 for refusal or neglect, without just cause, to perform an official duty owed to X, causing material (and moral) loss. It is "without prejudice to any disciplinary administrative action." Good faith or a superior's order is no defense, though it may mitigate damages.

A was charged with serious physical injuries for mauling B. Without making any reservation, B filed a separate civil action for damages based on Art. 33. A moves to suspend the civil case until the criminal case is decided. Rule.

Answer: Deny. Under Art. 33 and Rule 111, Sec. 3, the civil action for physical injuries is entirely separate and distinct, proceeds independently of the criminal prosecution, and needs only a preponderance of evidence. Under the 2000 Rules no reservation is required. B may not, however, recover twice for the same act.

A, charged with homicide, was acquitted. The decision stated that the prosecution's evidence "creates doubt" as to A's participation, without an express statement that it was on reasonable doubt. Can the heirs still sue A civilly for the same act?

Answer: Yes. Under Art. 29, par. 2, absent an express declaration it "may be inferred from the text of the decision" whether the acquittal was on reasonable doubt, and here it was. The civil action requires only a preponderance of evidence, and upon A's motion the court may require the heirs to post a bond against a malicious suit. Had the court declared that A was not the author of the act or that the act did not exist, the civil action ex delicto would be barred (Rule 111, Sec. 2).

B complained to the prosecutor that A committed an offense for which the law grants no independent civil action. The prosecutor refused to file the case. B sued A for damages. A year later the prosecutor, on new evidence, filed an information. What happens to B's civil case?

Answer: Under Art. 35, par. 2, the civil action shall be suspended until the termination of the criminal proceedings. (Contrast: if the offense had an independent civil action, such as defamation, the civil case would continue.)

Last-minute checklist

  • Art. 19: "in the exercise of his rights AND in the performance of his duties": (1) act with justice, (2) give everyone his due, (3) observe honesty and good faith. Elements: legal right or duty, bad faith, sole intent to prejudice. Sanction via Art. 20 or 21. Absolute rights include a parent's refusal of consent to a minor's marriage.
  • Art. 20: "contrary to law, wilfully or negligently," "indemnify." General sanction for laws without their own sanction. Prudence, not charity: no liability for failing to save a drowning man.
  • Art. 21: "wilfully" only, "contrary to morals, good customs or public policy" (three, and NOT "law"), "compensate." Covers legal acts. Moral damages under 2219(10).
  • Art. 22: "without just or legal ground," "shall return." Accion in rem verso has four requisites: enrichment, loss, no just ground, no other action (subsidiary). Recovery is capped at the enrichment.
  • Art. 23: "Even when not due to fault or negligence ... shall be liable ... if benefited." Damage to property. Example: flood drives cattle onto a neighbor's highland. Liability up to the extent of the benefit.
  • Art. 24: moral dependence, ignorance, indigence, mental weakness, tender age, other handicap. "Courts must be vigilant." Covers contractual, property or other relations.
  • Art. 25: thoughtless extravagance in expenses for pleasure or display, during acute public want or emergency, stopped by court order "at the instance of any government or private charitable institution."
  • Art. 26: dignity, personality, privacy, peace of mind. (1) prying into privacy of residence; (2) meddling with private life or family relations; (3) intriguing to alienate from friends; (4) vexing or humiliating on account of religious beliefs, lowly station, place of birth, physical defect, other personal condition. "Following and similar acts," "though they may not constitute a criminal offense," "damages, prevention and other relief."
  • Art. 27: public servant "refuses or neglects, without just cause," "material or moral loss," "without prejudice to" administrative action. Nonfeasance only.
  • Art. 28: unfair competition in agricultural, commercial, industrial enterprises or labor through force, intimidation, deceit, machination, or other unjust, oppressive or highhanded method. Need not be a crime.
  • Art. 29: acquittal on reasonable doubt allows a civil action for the same act, by preponderance. Bond "upon motion of the defendant," "may." The court "shall so declare," or it may be inferred from the text. Acquittal because the act did not exist or the accused was not the author bars the civil action ex delicto (Rule 111, Sec. 2).
  • Art. 30: separate civil action ex delicto with no criminal proceedings during its pendency: preponderance.
  • Art. 31: obligation not arising from the felony: proceeds independently and regardless of the result (contract, quasi-delict).
  • Art. 32: public officer or private individual, "directly or indirectly," 19 rights, whether or not a crime, independent, preponderance. Moral damages "shall" be included; exemplary "may." Judges are liable only if the act violates the Penal Code or another penal statute. Malice and good faith are irrelevant (Lim v. Ponce de Leon; Vinzons-Chato). Aberca: superiors are liable, and suspension of the writ is no bar.
  • Art. 33: defamation, fraud, physical injuries: entirely separate and distinct, "only a preponderance." Physical injuries include death (Madeja v. Caro). Fraud includes estafa.
  • Art. 34: police refuses or fails to aid. Officer primarily liable, city or municipality subsidiarily (NOT solidarily). Independent, preponderance. No diligence-in-selection defense.
  • Art. 35: no independent civil action plus no reasonable ground found or prosecutor refuses: civil action by preponderance, bond on the defendant's motion. If an information is later filed, the CIVIL action is suspended.
  • Moral damages, Art. 2219(10): Arts. 21, 26, 27, 28, 29, 30, 32, 34, 35 (not 19, 20, 22–25, 31, 33).
  • Rule 111 (2000): only civil liability ex delicto is deemed instituted. Reservation must be made before the prosecution presents evidence. Independent actions (32, 33, 34, 2176) need no reservation. No double recovery. B.P. 22: no reservation allowed. Death of the accused pending the case extinguishes ex delicto liability only.
  • Art. 36: "Pre-judicial questions ... before any criminal prosecution may be instituted or may proceed ... governed by rules of court ... not in conflict with this Code."
  • PQ elements (Rule 111, Sec. 7): (a) previously instituted civil action with an issue similar or intimately related to the issue in the subsequent criminal action; (b) its resolution determines whether or not the criminal action may proceed. Traditional third requisite: jurisdiction is in another tribunal.
  • PQ procedure (Sec. 6): file with the prosecutor or the court conducting preliminary investigation, or, if already in court, in the same criminal action before the prosecution rests. Effect: suspension only, not acquittal (Uribe).
  • Bigamy MCQ: PQ answer = annulment of the SECOND marriage (accused's consent vitiated by force or intimidation: Merced v. Diez, Zapanta v. Montesa). Uribe personally disagrees but keys (c).
  • Not a PQ: legal separation; annulment of the first marriage (voidable = valid until annulled); annulment of the second filed by the second spouse for the accused's deceit (Donato v. Luna); nullity of the second marriage for being bigamous; Art. 36 nullity of the second marriage (Tenebro); civil case filed after the criminal case (Pimentel; Dreamwork).
  • Landicho v. Relova: parties cannot judge the nullity of their marriage themselves, and one who remarries before a judicial declaration "assumes the risk" of a bigamy prosecution. This was abandoned by Pulido as to void ab initio marriages.
  • Pulido v. People (2021): Art. 40 applies only for remarriage, not for bigamy. A void marriage is not valid until declared void. A judicial declaration retroacts. The accused may prove nullity of the first (void) marriage, or of the second on grounds other than bigamy, even without a prior declaration. Uribe: "wala nang Tenebro" if Pulido is maintained.
  • Morigo: no ceremony means no first marriage at all, so no bigamy and no judicial declaration needed.
  • Ritter: acquittal on reasonable doubt does not extinguish civil liability. Art. 21 applies even if no penal law punishes the act. Ardiente: water cut off without notice is an abuse of right, with three elements.
  • Breach of promise: mere breach is NOT actionable (Hermosisima; Estremos; De Jesus v. Syquia; Congress deleted the chapter). Exceptions: Art. 21 walkout on a publicized wedding (Wassmer); fraudulent promise as a means of moral seduction (Baksh, with no in pari delicto since fault is unequal); recovery of money or property advanced (Domalagan; Art. 22); lost job (Garcia v. Del Rosario). No seduction where there is mutual passion between adults (Tanjanco). The plaintiff must be in good faith (Guevarra v. Banach).
  • Art. 1403(2)(c): agreement in consideration of marriage, OTHER THAN a mutual promise to marry, must be in writing. Cabague: the son may sue the fiancée on the mutual promise, but the father may not sue on the oral agreement in consideration of marriage. Domalagan: failure to object to oral evidence = waiver, and the ₱516 had to be returned.
  • Marriage can never be compelled by specific performance. Donations propter nuptias are revocable if the marriage is not celebrated (FC 86[1]). Those in marriage settlements are void if the marriage does not take place (FC 81).
