Separation of Property During the Marriage and the Regime of Separation of Property

Title IV, Chapters 5–6, Family Code, Arts. 134–146 (syllabus X.F–G). Covers judicial separation of property, voluntary or for sufficient cause (Arts. 134–136), its effects (Arts. 137–140), revival of the former regime (Art. 141), court transfer of the administration of exclusive property (Art. 142), and the regime of separation of property, whether by settlement or by mandate of law (Arts. 143–146; Arts. 103, 130, 66, 63). Cases: Garcia v. Manzano; Lacson v. San Jose-Lacson.

When the spouses' property is separate: the four routes

3. Legal separation and reconciliationArt. 63(2) (dissolution and liquidation); Art. 66(2) (separation subsists after reconciliation unless the spouses agree to revive)Revival only by a sworn agreement filed in court (Art. 67)
4. Judicial separation of propertyArts. 134–138: voluntary (Art. 136) or for sufficient cause (Art. 135); also Arts. 101, 128 (abandonment)After dissolution, complete separation applies (Art. 138)
Uribe (2018 situationer): "There can be regime of separation of property if so agreed upon by parties in marriage settlements. Do you agree?" No, if the point is that the settlement is the only way. Separation also arises by mandate of law (Arts. 103, 130), after legal separation, and by judicial decree.
Tolentino (five sources of separation): Separation of property may be (1) agreed in a marriage settlement, (2) ordered by the court on one spouse's petition, (3) adopted by voluntary agreement of the spouses approved by the court, (4) a result of legal separation, or (5) compulsory, when a surviving spouse remarries without liquidating the property of the prior marriage (Arts. 103, 130). It is the only regime that may begin during the marriage rather than at the celebration.

Art. 134: separation during the marriage needs a judicial order

Art. 134, FC. In the absence of an express declaration in the marriage settlements, the separation of property between spouses during the marriage shall not take place except by judicial order. Such judicial separation of property may either be voluntary or for sufficient cause. (190a)
Tolentino (Art. 134): The article is "deficient" because it ignores the compulsory separation that the law itself imposes under Arts. 103 and 130. A contract of separation of property signed by the spouses during the marriage without court approval is non-existent, whatever its consideration (Quintana v. Lerma; Garcia v. Manzano), and separation in fact does not create a separation regime (Arts. 100, 127). The agreement may be submitted to the court, but the separation is effected by the court's decree approving it, not by the contract, and it takes effect only upon approval (Brugi; In re Bermas, 14 SCRA 327; Lacson v. San Jose-Lacson, 24 SCRA 837). Likewise, the mere existence of an Art. 135 ground does not separate the property: a spouse must petition, and the court's order effects the separation.
Quiz traps: (1) UP BOC 2012 MCQ: "Separation of property between spouses during the marriage may take place only: (a) by agreement of the spouses; (b) if one spouse has given ground for legal separation; (c) upon order of the court; (d) if one spouse has abandoned the other." C. Agreement alone is insufficient, and grounds and abandonment still require a court order. (2) 2005 Bar (Gabby and Mila): a private document dissolving the CPG and adopting separation mid-marriage is void. The CPG subsists. (3) 2018 situationer: "If a petition for separation of property was filed, for this petition to prosper, the petitioner must allege and thereafter prove a sufficient cause." No. Separation may be voluntary (Art. 136), with no cause needed. Creditors are protected because they must be listed and notified.

Art. 135: sufficient causes for judicial separation

Art. 135, FC. Any of the following shall be considered sufficient cause for judicial separation of property:
(1) That the spouse of the petitioner has been sentenced to a penalty which carries with it civil interdiction;
(2) That the spouse of the petitioner has been judicially declared an absentee;
(3) That loss of parental authority of the spouse of petitioner has been decreed by the court;
(4) That the spouse of the petitioner has abandoned the latter or failed to comply with his or her obligations to the family as provided for in Article 101;
(5) That the spouse granted the power of administration in the marriage settlements has abused that power; and
(6) That at the time of the petition, the spouses have been separated in fact for at least one year and reconciliation is highly improbable.
In the cases provided for in Numbers (1), (2), and (3), the presentation of the final judgment against the guilty or absent spouse shall be enough basis for the grant of the decree of judicial separation of property. (191a)

Breakdown

  • Judgment-based grounds (1)–(3): civil interdiction, judicial declaration of absence, loss of parental authority. The final judgment alone suffices; nothing else need be proved.
  • Fact-based grounds (4)–(6): abandonment or failure to comply with obligations to the family (Art. 101: marital, parental or property); abuse of the power of administration granted in the settlement; separation in fact for at least one year with reconciliation highly improbable.
  • Compare with legal separation (Art. 55(10)): abandonment without justifiable cause for more than one year. For judicial separation of property, any abandonment under Art. 101 suffices (the three-month presumption helps prove it).
Number traps: separation in fact "for at least one year" (Art. 135(6)); abandonment presumption after "three months" (Arts. 101, 128); legal separation for abandonment "for more than one year" (Art. 55(10)). His legal separation key: "Abandonment of petitioner by respondent for more than one year is a ground for legal separation" is False (it omits "without justifiable cause").
De la Cruz v. De la Cruz (22 SCRA 333, 1968)

(Also in the CPG section.) A husband who leaves the conjugal home but keeps managing the conjugal properties diligently and supports his family has not "abandoned" his wife in the sense that justifies separation of property. The remedies exist "to protect the conjugal partnership from waste and shield the wife from want."

Art. 136: voluntary separation of property

Art. 136, FC. The spouses may jointly file a verified petition with the court for the voluntary dissolution of the absolute community or the conjugal partnership of gains, and for the separation of their common properties.
All creditors of the absolute community or of the conjugal partnership of gains, as well as the personal creditors of the spouse, shall be listed in the petition and notified of the filing thereof. The court shall take measures to protect the creditors and other persons with pecuniary interest. (191a)
  • Joint, verified petition by both spouses. No cause needs to be alleged or proved.
  • All creditors (of the regime and the personal creditors of each spouse) must be listed and notified. The court takes protective measures.
  • Only one voluntary separation is allowed after a revival: once a voluntary separation is revived under Art. 141(7), "no voluntary separation of property may thereafter be granted."
Tolentino (grounds for approving a voluntary separation): The law does not state the reasons that justify approval; it leaves their sufficiency to the court's sound discretion. An agreement with no reason at all should be disapproved. Any ground that would lessen friction and misunderstanding over the property or its administration, or that is for the mutual benefit of the spouses and the family, justifies approval.
Garcia v. Manzano (103 Phil. 798)

"The husband and the wife may agree upon the dissolution of the conjugal partnership during the marriage, subject to judicial approval. All the creditors of the husband and of the wife, as well as of the conjugal partnership, shall be notified of any petition for judicial approval of the voluntary dissolution of the conjugal partnership, so that any such creditors may appear at the hearing to safeguard his interests. Upon approval of the petition for dissolution of the conjugal partnership, the court shall take such measures as may protect the creditors and other third persons." (Uribe's syllabus places it under "For sufficient cause, FC 135, cf. FC 55(10)"; its doctrine is on judicial approval and protection of creditors.)

Lacson v. San Jose-Lacson (24 SCRA 837, 1968)

The spouses, separated in fact, executed a compromise agreement on the separation of their property and the dissolution of their conjugal partnership, which the court approved. Held: "The law allows separation of property of the spouses and the dissolution of their conjugal partnership provided judicial sanction is secured beforehand." Here "the spouses obtained judicial imprimatur of their separation of property and the dissolution of their conjugal partnership," and no creditors would be prejudiced, so the separation of property was valid. (The same compromise also dealt with the custody of the children; the case is more often cited under parental authority for the rule that a child under seven should not be separated from the mother by mere agreement.)

Arts. 137–140: effects of judicial separation

Art. 137, FC. Once the separation of property has been decreed, the absolute community or the conjugal partnership of gains shall be liquidated in conformity with this Code.
During the pendency of the proceedings for separation of property, the absolute community or the conjugal partnership shall pay for the support of the spouses and their children. (192a)
Art. 138, FC. After dissolution of the absolute community or of the conjugal partnership, the provisions on complete separation of property shall apply. (191a)
Art. 139, FC. The petition for separation of property and the final judgment granting the same shall be recorded in the proper local civil registries and registries of property. (193a)
Art. 140, FC. The separation of property shall not prejudice the rights previously acquired by creditors. (194a)
  1. Liquidation under Art. 102 (ACP) or Art. 129 (CPG).
  2. Support pendente lite from the common property while the case is pending.
  3. Complete separation of property thereafter (Arts. 143–146).
  4. Recording of both the petition and the final judgment in the LCR and the registries of property.
  5. Creditors' prior rights are unaffected.
  6. A waiver of the ACP or CPG share is allowed only upon judicial separation, in a public instrument, recorded, and subject to the creditors' action to rescind (Arts. 89, 107).
Tolentino (effects of the decree): (1) the ACP or CPG is dissolved and liquidated; (2) each spouse thereafter owns exclusively his or her earnings and the fruits of his or her separate property; (3) the duty to support the children continues, each spouse contributing in proportion to his or her income or property; (4) the spouses' mutual duty of support continues. Contributions are based on the income from their property, not its value, and on market value only if the income is insufficient. A spouse who paid all the children's expenses because the other had no means cannot later recover a proportionate share when the other's means improve: he or she was paying his or her own legal obligation.

Art. 141: revival of the former regime

Art. 141, FC. The spouses may, in the same proceedings where separation of property was decreed, file a motion in court for a decree reviving the property regime, in the following instances:
(1) When the civil interdiction terminates;
(2) When the absentee spouse reappears;
(3) When the court, being satisfied that the spouse granted the power of administration in the marriage settlements will not again abuse that power, authorizes the resumption of said administration;
(4) When the spouse who has left the conjugal home without a decree of legal separation resumes common life with the other;
(5) When parental authority is judicially restored to the spouse previously deprived thereof;
(6) When the spouses who have separated in fact for at least one year, reconcile and resume common life; or
(7) When after voluntary dissolution of the absolute community of property or conjugal partnership has been judicially decreed upon the joint petition of the spouses, they agree to the revival of the former property regime. No voluntary separation of property may thereafter be granted.
The revival of the former property regime shall be governed by Article 67. (195a)

Each ground in Art. 141 mirrors a cause in Art. 135 (or the voluntary separation in Art. 136) that has ceased. The revival is by motion in the same proceeding, and the procedure follows Art. 67.

Tolentino (who files the motion): Revival is not automatic when the cause ends; the court must grant a motion. Tolentino says the motion must be filed by both spouses: Art. 141 does not give the right to only one, and Art. 67, which governs the revival, requires an agreement of both.
Art. 67, FC. The agreement to revive the former property regime referred to in the preceding Article shall be executed under oath and shall specify:
(1) The properties to be contributed anew to the restored regime;
(2) Those to be retained as separated properties of each spouse; and
(3) The names of all their known creditors, their addresses and the amounts owing to each.
The agreement of revival and the motion for its approval shall be filed with the court in the same proceeding for legal separation, with copies of both furnished to the creditors named therein. After due hearing, the court shall, in its order, take measure to protect the interest of creditors and such order shall be recorded in the proper registries of properties.
The recording of the ordering in the registries of property shall not prejudice any creditor not listed or not notified, unless the debtor-spouse has sufficient separate properties to satisfy the creditor's claim. (195a, 108a)
Quiz trap: the revival agreement must be under oath (not merely in writing) and filed in court. Compare the original settlement (a private writing is enough, Art. 77), the waiver of a share (a public instrument, Art. 89), and the transfer of administration of exclusive property (a public instrument, recorded, Art. 110).

Art. 142: court transfer of the administration of exclusive property

Art. 142, FC. The administration of all classes of exclusive property of either spouse may be transferred by the court to the other spouse:
(1) When one spouse becomes the guardian of the other;
(2) When one spouse is judicially declared an absentee;
(3) When one spouse is sentenced to a penalty which carries with it civil interdiction; or
(4) When one spouse becomes a fugitive from justice or is in hiding as an accused in a criminal case.
If the other spouse is not qualified by reason of incompetence, conflict of interest, or any other just cause, the court shall appoint a suitable person to be the administrator. (n)

Compare Art. 110 (2nd par.): the owner-spouse may voluntarily transfer administration of his or her exclusive property to the other by a recorded public instrument. Art. 142 is the judicial transfer.

Tolentino (Art. 142): Art. 196 NCC let the court transfer the administration of "all classes of property in the marriage"; Art. 142 covers only the exclusive property of either spouse. The section heading still speaks of "Administration of Common Property by One Spouse", which Tolentino treats as an error in phrasing. For common property, Arts. 96 and 124 let the capable spouse assume sole administration without court intervention; for exclusive property, the court makes the transfer. If the other spouse is unfit (incompetence, conflict of interest or other just cause), the court appoints a suitable person. The administrator is only a trustee: ownership does not pass, and he or she cannot alienate or encumber the property without court authority, and the court may impose conditions to protect the owner. When the cause ends, the owner may petition to end the administration, recover the property, and demand an accounting.

Arts. 143–146: the regime of separation of property

Tolentino (the separation regime): Under separation of property, the spouses' interests are completely independent; there is separation not only of assets but also of liabilities. Each spouse has full ownership, enjoyment and administration of his or her own property, its fruits, and the products of his or her work. Its advantage is simplicity (no complicated liquidation). Its disadvantages: it runs contrary to the community of life and interest that marriage creates, and it may cause constant friction over family expenses. Conventional vs. judicial separation: conventional separation (by settlement) takes effect at the celebration, is permanent and immutable, may be partial, and is governed first by the settlement; judicial separation (by decree) takes effect during the marriage, is provisional (it ends when its cause ends, Art. 141), covers the whole patrimony of both spouses with no exceptions, and is governed by Arts. 135–141.
Art. 143, FC. Should the future spouses agree in the marriage settlements that their property relations during marriage shall be governed by the regime of separation of property, the provisions of this Chapter shall be suppletory. (212a)
Art. 144, FC. Separation of property may refer to present or future property or both. It may be total or partial. In the latter case, the property not agreed upon as separate shall pertain to the absolute community. (213a)
Art. 145, FC. Each spouse shall own, dispose of, possess, administer and enjoy his or her own separate estate, without need of the consent of the other. To each spouse shall belong all earnings from his or her profession, business or industry and all fruits, natural, industrial or civil, due or received during the marriage from his or her separate property. (214a)
Art. 146, FC. Both spouses shall bear the family expenses in proportion to their income, or, in case of insufficiency or default thereof, to the current market value of their separate properties.
The liabilities of the spouses to creditors for family expenses shall, however, be solidary. (215a)
  • Scope (Art. 144): present property, future property, or both; total or partial.
  • Partial separation: what is not agreed upon as separate goes to the absolute community, not the CPG.
  • Tolentino (partial separation): partial separation means separation and the ACP (or the CPG, if agreed) exist side by side. If the settlement says nothing about the spouses' earnings from work during the marriage, those earnings go to the community or partnership; property acquired for value during the marriage is presumed common; accessions to property under separation belong to its owner. In total separation, nothing is common: fruits and earnings belong to each spouse.
  • Ownership and administration (Art. 145): each spouse owns, disposes of, possesses, administers and enjoys his or her own estate without the other's consent. Each keeps his or her own earnings and the fruits of his or her separate property.
  • Tolentino (Art. 145): (1) A wife under separation may transfer the management of her property to her husband, as Art. 110 allows in the CPG. This is not an amendment of the settlement; it is an agency, revocable at will (the Swiss Code makes a waiver of the right to take back management void). (2) Property belongs to the spouse who appears as buyer or grantee, without prejudice to creditors or heirs proving that he or she acted only for the other. In case of doubt, real property belongs to the spouse in whose name it is registered or was acquired, and personal property to the spouse in possession. Personal property possessed jointly may be attached by either spouse's creditor, leaving the other spouse to prove exclusive ownership; between the spouses or their heirs, it is presumed to belong to the spouse who owns or leases the building where it is found, and personal effects belong to their user. (3) Liabilities are separate: one spouse's creditors cannot levy on the other's property, except when the debtor acted as the other's authorized agent, or the other spouse benefited (liable to the extent of the unjust enrichment). (4) Each spouse may recover from the other any credit or advance even during the marriage, and damages caused by the other's fraud, fault or negligence; but neither may charge for personal services, advice or help, which are part of the duty of mutual help.
  • Family expenses (Art. 146): borne in proportion to income, or, if income is insufficient or lacking, to the current market value of their separate properties. As to creditors, the spouses are solidarily liable for family expenses.
  • Tolentino (Art. 146): for family expenses it does not matter which spouse contracted the debt; both are liable in proportion. If one spouse has income but no property and the other has property but no income, the proportion is based on the amount of the income and the value of the property, respectively. A spouse who paid all the family expenses because the other had neither property nor income cannot seek reimbursement, because the obligation was his or her own, and the same is true if a creditor enforced the solidary liability against one spouse.
Quiz traps: (1) partial separation → the rest goes to the absolute community (Art. 144), not "the conjugal partnership". (2) Family expenses are borne "in proportion to their income", not "equally", and the fallback is "current market value" of separate properties. (3) Liability to creditors for family expenses is solidary, even though the sharing between the spouses is proportional.
Uribe (Quiz 4 #13 discussion on the mandatory regime): under Arts. 103 and 130, liquidation is needed to deliver the legitimes to the heirs and to pay the debts of the deceased. If the surviving spouse remarries without doing so, a mandatory regime of complete separation of property governs the new marriage "in order to protect the rights of the heirs left by the deceased."

Checklist: Arts. 134–146

  1. Separation during the marriage: only by judicial order, unless the settlement expressly provides otherwise. It may be voluntary or for sufficient cause (Art. 134).
  2. Causes (Art. 135): civil interdiction, judicial absence, loss of parental authority (the final judgment is enough); abandonment or failure of obligations (Art. 101); abuse of administration granted in the settlement; separation in fact for at least one year with reconciliation highly improbable.
  3. Voluntary (Art. 136): joint verified petition; list and notify all creditors.
  4. Effects: liquidation; support pendente lite from the common property; complete separation thereafter; recording in the LCR and registries of property; creditors' prior rights preserved (Arts. 137–140).
  5. Revival (Art. 141): by motion in the same proceeding, on seven grounds; procedure under Art. 67 (sworn agreement listing properties and creditors). No further voluntary separation after reviving a voluntary one.
  6. Judicial transfer of the administration of exclusive property (Art. 142): guardian, absentee, civil interdiction, fugitive or accused in hiding.
  7. Regime of separation (Arts. 143–146): present or future, total or partial (residue → ACP); each spouse administers and disposes of his or her own property without consent; family expenses in proportion to income, then market value; solidary liability to creditors for family expenses.
