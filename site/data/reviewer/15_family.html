The Family

Title V, Chapter 1, Family Code, Arts. 149–151 (syllabus XI), with NCC 2035 and the rule on condition precedents in the Rules of Court. Covers the policy declaration (Art. 149), the closed list of family relations (Art. 150), and the earnest-efforts requirement for suits between family members (Art. 151). Cases: Alavado, Arroyo, Gayon, Wainwright-Versoza, Heirs of Favis and Salazar v. Felias (the syllabus lists Salazar here, but its doctrine is on the family home, so it is digested in the next section).

Art. 149: the family as a basic social institution

Art. 149, FC. The family, being the foundation of the nation, is a basic social institution which public policy cherishes and protects. Consequently, family relations are governed by law and no custom, practice or agreement destructive of the family shall be recognized or given effect. (216a, 218a)
  • Family relations are governed by law, not by the parties' will. Rights and duties within the family are generally inalienable, non-transferable, imprescriptible and not subject to waiver.
  • A custom, practice or agreement destructive of the family is not recognized. Examples: an agreement between spouses to live separately or to "free" each other to cohabit with others; a stipulation renouncing future support; a waiver of future legitime (NCC 905, 2035).
  • Cf. Const. Art. XV, Sec. 1 (the State recognizes the Filipino family as the foundation of the nation) and Art. II, Sec. 12.
Tolentino (Art. 149): the Spanish Civil Code had no declaration of policy; the Code Commission added principles that sustain family solidarity, as guidance for the courts and for their "wholesome influence". "The vitality and strength of the State depends upon the solidarity of its nucleus, the family." The family has two aspects. The internal aspect (the spouses' intimate relations, the career parents choose for a child, domestic practices) is natural and moral and is beyond the reach of law. The external aspect is regulated by law only where third persons or the public interest are involved, or to protect the wife and children from abuse (support, parental authority). Family law is marked by (1) increasing State intervention and (2) its obligatory character: its rules are of public order and cannot be set aside by agreement except in very exceptional cases, and family rights and duties cannot be performed through agents.
Alavado v. City Government of Tacloban (139 SCRA 230, 1985)

The claimant's status as the deceased Alavado's lawful wife (and so her right to what was due the family) was contested by the city. Held: the presumption that a man and a woman deporting themselves as husband and wife are legally married stands. "Public and open cohabitation as husband and wife, birth and baptismal certificates of children born unto them after the celebration of the questioned marriage, and a statement of such marriage in subsequent document were held to be competent evidence as proof of said marriage." Nothing in the records overthrew the presumption in favor of the claimant's marriage to the deceased. (The case illustrates the law's leaning toward the validity of marriage and the solidarity of the family, which Art. 149 protects.)

Arroyo, Jr. v. CA (203 SCRA 750, 1991)

Dr. Jorge Neri filed a complaint for adultery against his wife, Ruby Vera-Neri, and Eduardo Arroyo, who were convicted. The wife later executed an affidavit that she had been pardoned, and the husband filed a manifestation that he was no longer interested in prosecuting. Held: the conviction stands. Pardon must be given before the institution of the criminal action, and the husband's later desistance does not bind the State: "while Article 344 of the Revised Penal Code provides that the crime of adultery cannot be prosecuted without the offended spouse's complaint, once the complaint has been filed, the control of the case passes to the public prosecutor. Enforcement of our law on adultery is not exclusively, nor even principally, a matter of vindication of the private honor of the offended spouse … Such enforcement relates, more importantly, to protection of the basic social institutions of marriage and the family in the preservation of which the State has the strongest interest; the public policy here involved is of the most fundamental kind."

Quiz traps (Art. 149): "Family relations are governed by custom and by the agreement of the parties" → false: by law. "A custom destructive of the family is valid if long practiced in the locality" → false: no custom, practice or agreement destructive of the family is recognized. "Adultery is a private crime, so the offended spouse controls the case until judgment" → false (Arroyo): control passes to the public prosecutor once the complaint is filed.

Art. 150: who are members of the family

Art. 150, FC. Family relations include those: (1) Between husband and wife; (2) Between parents and children; (3) Among other ascendants and descendants; and (4) Among brothers and sisters, whether of the full or half-blood. (217a)
Count the items: there are four. Some student reviewers (including the B29 provisions file and some notes) print only three items and drop "(3) Among other ascendants and descendants". The official text, which Tolentino reproduces (p. 504), has four. So grandparents and grandchildren are family under Art. 150, and a suit between them requires earnest efforts.
  • The list is exclusive and strictly construed, because Art. 151 is an exception to the general right to sue (Gayon).
  • Brothers and sisters are included "whether of the full or half-blood", and the text does not distinguish legitimate from illegitimate.
  • Not included: relatives by affinity (brothers- and sisters-in-law, parents-in-law, stepparents and stepchildren), uncles and aunts, nephews and nieces, and cousins.
Tolentino (Art. 150): relationship may be natural (consanguinity, legitimate or illegitimate; and affinity), civil (adoption) or religious (from baptism or confirmation). For Art. 150, religious relationship and affinity are given no legal effect. Family relations in the broad sense are not limited to this list: civil relationship (adoption) and collateral consanguinity up to the fifth degree are recognized elsewhere in the law, e.g., in intestate succession.

Art. 151: earnest efforts toward a compromise

Art. 151, FC. No suit between members of the same family shall prosper unless it should appear from the verified complaint or petition that earnest efforts toward a compromise have been made, but that the same have failed. If it is shown that no such efforts were in fact made, the same case must be dismissed.
This rule shall not apply to cases which may not be the subject of compromise under the Civil Code. (222a)
Art. 2035, NCC. No compromise upon the following questions shall be valid: (1) The civil status of persons; (2) The validity of a marriage or a legal separation; (3) Any ground for legal separation; (4) Future support; (5) The jurisdiction of courts; (6) Future legitime.

Requisites and nature

  1. The suit is between members of the same family as enumerated in Art. 150, and all the parties are family; if a stranger is a co-party, Art. 151 does not apply.
  2. The subject is compromisable.
  3. The verified complaint or petition must allege that earnest efforts toward a compromise were made and failed.
  4. The requirement is a condition precedent, not a jurisdictional requirement. Non-compliance is a ground to dismiss (Rule 16, Sec. 1(j), 1997 Rules: "a condition precedent for filing the claim has not been complied with"; now an affirmative defense under Rule 8, Sec. 12 of the 2019 amendments). It is waived if not raised in a motion to dismiss or the answer, and the court cannot dismiss on that ground motu proprio (Heirs of Favis).
Gayon v. Gayon (36 SCRA 104, 1970)

Pedro Gayon sued the spouses Silvestre Gayon and Genoveva de Gayon over land. Silvestre had died before the complaint was filed, and the suit was in substance against his widow and heirs. The trial court dismissed for failure to allege earnest efforts under NCC 222 (now Art. 151), since the plaintiff and the deceased were brothers. Held: dismissal reversed. The defendants were Silvestre's surviving spouse and children, and "inasmuch as a sister-in-law, nephew or niece is not included in the enumeration contained in Article 217 [now Art. 150], … which should be construed strictly, it being an exception to the general rule, it follows that the same does not come within the purview of Art. 222, and plaintiff's failure to seek a compromise before filing the complaint does not bar the same."

Wainwright-Versoza v. Versoza (26 SCRA 78, 1968)

A wife (with her child) sued her husband for support. The husband moved to dismiss for failure to allege earnest efforts toward a compromise under NCC 222. Held: the action may proceed. "Since the present action also revolves on the right to future support and because compromise on future support is prescribed, then the conclusion is irresistible that an attempt at compromise of future support and failure thereof is not a condition precedent to the filing of the present suit. It need not be alleged in the complaint." (Art. 151, last paragraph, with NCC 2035(4).)

Heirs of Favis, Sr. v. Gonzales (G.R. No. 185922, January 15, 2014)

The children of Dr. Mariano Favis, Sr. by his first marriage sued his second wife, Juana Gonzales, their son Mariano, Jr., and Mariano Jr.'s wife and children, to annul a deed of donation Dr. Favis had executed in favor of those grandchildren. The RTC ruled for the plaintiffs; the CA motu proprio dismissed the complaint because it did not allege earnest efforts toward a compromise. Held: CA reversed. Failure to allege earnest efforts is not a jurisdictional defect; it is a condition precedent whose non-fulfillment is a ground for a motion to dismiss. "No motion to dismiss the complaint based on the failure to comply with a condition precedent was filed in the trial court and neither was such failure assigned as error in the appeal that respondent brought before the Court of Appeals. Therefore the rule on deemed waiver of the non-jurisdictional defense or objection is wholly applicable to respondent." Under Rule 9, Sec. 1, the court may dismiss on its own only for lack of jurisdiction over the subject matter, litis pendentia, res judicata or prescription.

Other cases often cited (not in Sir's syllabus)

Gonzales v. Lopez (160 SCRA 346, 1988), cited by Tolentino: Art. 151 does not apply where the defendants include strangers to the family. Hontiveros v. RTC (G.R. No. 125465, June 29, 1999): a suit by spouses against the husband's brother and the brother's wife is not covered, because the sisters-in-law are not "members of the same family"; the inclusion of a stranger takes the case out of the article. Mendoza v. CA (19 SCRA 756, 1967): an action involving a non-compromisable question (there, support) needs no allegation of earnest efforts.

Tolentino (Art. 151): the Code Commission's reason: "It is difficult to imagine a sadder and more tragic spectacle than a litigation between members of the same family"; a suit between close relatives "generates deeper bitterness than between strangers". The article does not apply where the defendants are strangers to the principal parties, is limited to the members listed in Art. 150 (Gayon), and does not cover non-compromisable questions. The Family Code omitted NCC 219 (mutual aid) and NCC 220 (in case of doubt, all presumptions favor family solidarity: the validity of marriage, the indissolubility of the marriage bonds, the legitimacy of children, the community of property, parental authority, and the defense of family members). Tolentino says these principles remain valid as rules of interpretation, since they are already embodied in jurisprudence. Mutual aid is never a shield for nepotism or for helping a relative commit a crime, although the Revised Penal Code recognizes family solidarity (defense of relatives, Art. 11(2); vindication, Art. 13(5); exemption of relatives as accessories, Art. 20, except those who profit).
Uribe (situationers, 2018 and CivRev 2025): (1) "A filed an action for a sum of money against X and Y, X being the father of A … X and Y claimed that this action should be dismissed [for lack of an allegation of earnest efforts]." Sir first stressed that the facts did not say Y was A's mother; if Y is the mother, the contention is tenable under Art. 151 (all parties are family). (2) "A filed an action for support against X and Y. A being the illegitimate child of X, and Y being the spouse of X. … In the SC, X raised as a defense that the action cannot prosper because A failed to allege [earnest efforts]." Answer: the defense is only a ground for a motion to dismiss and is not jurisdictional, so raising it for the first time in the SC is too late; besides, Y (A's stepmother) is a stranger under Art. 150, and future support cannot be compromised. Follow-up: "Assuming X filed an MTD, will the action prosper?" Yes: there is a stranger in the suit, so Art. 151 does not apply.
Quiz traps (Art. 151): "Failure to allege earnest efforts deprives the court of jurisdiction" → false (condition precedent only). "The court may dismiss motu proprio for failure to allege earnest efforts even if the defendant did not raise it" → false (Favis: waived). "A suit between brothers-in-law requires earnest efforts" → false (Gayon). "An action for support between spouses requires earnest efforts" → false (future support is non-compromisable). Watch "verified": the complaint or petition must be verified.

Practice: X sued his brother Y and Y's wife Z to recover a lot. The complaint did not allege earnest efforts. Y and Z moved to dismiss under Art. 151. Rule.

Deny. Z, Y's wife, is X's sister-in-law, a relative by affinity who is not among the members of the family listed in Art. 150, which is strictly construed (Gayon; Hontiveros). Since a stranger is a party, the suit is not one "between members of the same family", and the earnest-efforts requirement does not apply.

Practice: In a partition case between two sisters, neither the complaint nor the answer mentioned earnest efforts. After trial, the CA dismissed the case on its own for non-compliance with Art. 151. Correct?

No. The requirement is a condition precedent, not a jurisdictional requirement. Not having been raised in a motion to dismiss or in the answer, the objection is deemed waived (Rule 9, Sec. 1), and the court cannot dismiss motu proprio on that ground (Heirs of Favis v. Gonzales).
