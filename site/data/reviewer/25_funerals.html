Funerals

Civil Code, Arts. 305–310 (syllabus XXI). Who has the right and duty to bury, the deceased's wishes, retention and exhumation of remains, autopsies, disrespect for the dead, and tombstones. Cases: Eugenio v. Velez; from the class reviewer, Valino v. Adriano.

Art. 305, NCC. The duty and the right to make arrangements for the funeral of a relative shall be in accordance with the order established for support, under article 294 [now FC 199]. In case of descendants of the same degree, or of brothers and sisters, the oldest shall be preferred. In case of ascendants, the paternal shall have a better right.
Art. 306, NCC. Every funeral shall be in keeping with the social position of the deceased.
Art. 307, NCC. The funeral shall be in accordance with the expressed wishes of the deceased. In the absence of such expression, his religious beliefs or affiliation shall determine the funeral rites. In case of doubt, the form of the funeral shall be decided upon by the person obliged to make arrangements for the same, after consulting the other members of the family.
Art. 308, NCC. No human remains shall be retained, interred, disposed of or exhumed without the consent of the persons mentioned in articles 294 and 305.
Art. 309, NCC. Any person who shows disrespect to the dead, or wrongfully interferes with a funeral shall be liable to the family of the deceased for damages, material and moral.
Art. 310, NCC. The construction of a tombstone or mausoleum shall be deemed a part of the funeral expenses, and shall be chargeable to the conjugal partnership property, if the deceased is one of the spouses.

Who buries (Art. 305 with FC 199)

Eugenio, Sr. v. Velez (185 SCRA 425, 1990)

Vitaliana Vargas's full-blood brothers and sisters filed a petition for habeas corpus, alleging she was being unlawfully detained by Tomas Eugenio, Sr., with whom she lived. She had in fact already died, and Eugenio, claiming to be her common-law husband, refused to surrender the body for burial. The trial court treated the case as an action for custody of the corpse. Held: custody goes to the siblings. A writ of habeas corpus cannot issue for a dead body, but the court could proceed on the question of custody. "We hold that the provisions of the Civil Code, unless expressly providing to the contrary as in Article 144, when referring to a 'spouse' contemplate a lawfully wedded spouse. Petitioner vis-a-vis Vitaliana was not a lawfully-wedded spouse to her; in fact, he was not legally capacitated to marry her in her lifetime." With no spouse, descendants or ascendants, her full-blood siblings had the right to bury her.

Valino v. Adriano (G.R. No. 182894, April 22, 2014; class reviewer)

Atty. Adriano Adriano, long separated in fact from his wife Rosario, lived for many years with Fe Floro Valino, who cared for him in his illness and paid his medical and funeral expenses. When he died, his wife and children were in the U.S. and asked Valino to delay the burial; she buried him in her family's mausoleum anyway. The legal family sued for damages and the transfer of the remains. Held: for the legal family; the remains are to be exhumed and transferred. Under Art. 305 with FC 199, the right and duty to make funeral arrangements belong to the surviving spouse, then descendants, ascendants and siblings, "to the exclusion of common-law partners", however long or close the relationship. The deceased's wishes (Art. 307) govern only the rites and form of the funeral, and must be proved clearly and expressly, preferably in a will; in case of doubt, the law favors the legitimate family. Valino's claim that the deceased wanted to be buried with her was not proved.

Tolentino (Art. 305): the person preferred may waive the right, expressly or impliedly, and it then passes to the next in order; a spouse must assert the right promptly. A wife living separately when the husband died, or who neglects or refuses the duty, impliedly waives it; but waiver is not presumed and needs clear proof of a free and voluntary intent. The person entitled to custody of the corpse cannot exclude the deceased's friends and other relatives from the wake and funeral without just cause; that would be an abuse of right (NCC 19) or contrary to good customs (NCC 21). Family members can never be excluded.

The corpse: rights, wishes, limits

Tolentino (Arts. 306–310): Art. 306 has no sanction of its own, but others supply it: if the surviving spouse spends lavishly out of proportion to the deceased's position and small estate, the other heirs may object to charging all of it to the estate; if the spouse gives a miserable funeral to a prominent person with a large estate, the relatives may sue for disrespect to the dead under Art. 309. Actionable disrespect includes withholding the body from those entitled to it, an unauthorized autopsy (a physician who did one to fill in a death certificate, without consent, was held liable), mutilation, unlawful disturbance of a grave or removal of remains, an undertaker's refusal to disclose where the body is, and unauthorized use of a photograph of a malformed deceased child. Revised Administrative Code limits: burial only in authorized places, a death certificate is needed, and an unembalmed body must be buried within 48 hours (12 hours if the death was due to a dangerous communicable disease), unless legal investigation or the health authorities require otherwise.
Quiz traps (funerals): "A common-law spouse of many years has the first right to bury the deceased" → false (Eugenio; Valino). "Among ascendants of the same degree, the maternal side is preferred" → false: paternal (Art. 305). "Among descendants of the same degree, the youngest is preferred" → false: the oldest. "Habeas corpus lies to recover a dead body" → false (Eugenio; the court may still decide custody). "A tombstone is a personal expense of the surviving spouse" → false: funeral expense chargeable to the conjugal partnership (Art. 310). "The deceased's wishes on the funeral always prevail" → false: subject to law, and they cover only the form and rites.
