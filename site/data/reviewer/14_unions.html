Property Regimes of Unions Without Marriage

Title IV, Chapter 8, Family Code, Arts. 147–148 (syllabus X.H; review FC 35–38, 41, 44, 45, 50, 53). Covers the special co-ownership of parties capacitated to marry each other (Art. 147), the limited co-ownership of parties who are not (Art. 148), forfeiture, and the cases: Maxey, Salas v. Aguila, Juaniza v. Jose, Gomez v. Lipana, Vda. de Consuegra v. GSIS, Yap v. CA, Cariño v. Cariño, Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan, Ventura v. Abuda. Sir flagged this chapter for emphasis: "focus really on property regimes on unions without marriage … last year tinanong sa bar."

147 or 148? The threshold question

Arts. 147 and 148 apply only where there is no valid marriage: either (a) a man and a woman live together as husband and wife without marrying, or (b) they are parties to a void marriage. They do not apply to voidable marriages, which are valid until annulled, so the ACP or CPG governs and is liquidated under Arts. 50 and 43. There is never an ACP or CPG to "dissolve" in a void marriage.

Uribe's rule of thumb (CivRev 2025): "If one of them or both of them is or are married to other persons, a third person will be affected by the cohabitation or marriage (bigamous or adulterous in character): 148 will apply. All other grounds that will make a marriage void, as long as no third person will be affected, 147 will apply." Asked whether first cousins who married fall under 147 or 148, the class answered 147 (the marriage is void for public policy, but no third person is affected), and Sir's rule of thumb supports that.
Conflict with the text and with commentators. Art. 147 requires parties "capacitated to marry each other". Many commentators and bar reviewers treat any legal impediment between the parties themselves (e.g., first cousins under Art. 38(1), step-relations, or a party below 18) as falling under Art. 148, which covers "cohabitation not falling under the preceding Article". Cariño lists Art. 148's coverage as "bigamous marriages, adulterous relationships, relationships in a state of concubine, relationships where both man and woman are married to other persons, multiple alliances of the same married man". On Sir's exam, apply his rule of thumb and say why (no third person is affected). If the item is a bar-style MCQ, note that the textual test is capacity to marry each other.
Tolentino (coverage of Arts. 147 and 148): Art. 147 covers void marriages, whether the parties were in good or bad faith, and a man and a woman, both unmarried and without impediment to marry each other, who live together as husband and wife. Art. 148 covers (1) parties with no legal capacity to marry each other who live together without marrying, e.g., persons below 18, those whose marriage would be incestuous, and those whose marriage would be against public policy (Arts. 35–38); and (2) cohabitation where one or both parties are validly married to others. This supports the textual reading in the note above. Both articles require cohabitation: living together as husband and wife, shown by sexual relations together with a common household, holding out as spouses to third persons, correspondence and documents, mutual financial help, and raising children together. Occasional secret meetings for sex are not enough; a man who repeatedly visited a woman at night for five months, had intercourse and left did not live with her as husband and wife.
Tolentino (comparing Arts. 147 and 148): Similarities: (1) cohabitation as husband and wife; (2) no ACP or CPG is formed; (3) the common property is governed by co-ownership; (4) property that is not co-owned (bought with money each had before living together, or from the fruits of separate property) belongs to each exclusively; (5) bad faith forfeits a party's share. Differences: (1) capacity to marry each other vs. incapacity or impediment; (2) exclusive cohabitation vs. one or both may have a valid existing marriage; (3) wages and work-acquired property co-owned in equal shares vs. co-ownership only by actual joint contribution of money, property or industry, in proportion; (4) a presumption that property acquired during cohabitation came from joint efforts vs. no such presumption; (5) care of the family and household counts as a contribution vs. it does not; (6) Art. 147 presumes the acquisition by joint effort (and equal shares), while Art. 148 presumes only the equality of contributions. "Under Art. 148, if the actual contribution by one party is not proved, there will be no co-ownership and no presumption of equal shares."
Uribe (2018 situationer): the marriage of A and B was declared void. During the cohabitation, A saved ₱1M in BDO, and a condominium unit was bought from A's income and registered in his name. B inherited a parcel of land from her grandmother. Who owns what? "It depends on why the marriage was declared void" (147 vs. 148). Under 147, the savings from wages are shared equally, and the condo is presumed acquired by joint efforts and owned equally (B's care of the household counts). Under 148, both belong to A unless B proves actual contribution. Under either article, the inherited land is B's alone, because it was not acquired through work or industry. Follow-up: "What if they were minors but obtained the consent of the parents? 147 or 148?" Under the Family Code, a party below 18 cannot marry even with consent (Art. 35(1)), so the parties are not "capacitated", which points to 148.

Art. 147: the special co-ownership

Art. 147, FC. When a man and a woman who are capacitated to marry each other, live exclusively with each other as husband and wife without the benefit of marriage or under a void marriage, their wages and salaries shall be owned by them in equal shares and the property acquired by both of them through their work or industry shall be governed by the rules on co-ownership.
In the absence of proof to the contrary, properties acquired while they lived together shall be presumed to have been obtained by their joint efforts, work or industry, and shall be owned by them in equal shares. For purposes of this Article, a party who did not participate in the acquisition by the other party of any property shall be deemed to have contributed jointly in the acquisition thereof if the former's efforts consisted in the care and maintenance of the family and of the household.
Neither party can encumber or dispose by acts inter vivos of his or her share in the property acquired during cohabitation and owned in common, without the consent of the other, until after the termination of their cohabitation.
When only one of the parties to a void marriage is in good faith, the share of the party in bad faith in the co-ownership shall be forfeited in favor of their common children. In case of default of or waiver by any or all of the common children or their descendants, each vacant share shall belong to the respective surviving descendants. In the absence of descendants, such share shall belong to the innocent party. In all cases, the forfeiture shall take place upon termination of the cohabitation. (144a)

Requisites

  1. A man and a woman. (2015 Bar: Bert and Joe, both men, cannot invoke Art. 147, because they are not a man and a woman capacitated to marry each other under Arts. 1 and 2.)
  2. Capacitated to marry each other: no legal impediment between them.
  3. Living exclusively with each other.
  4. As husband and wife.
  5. Without the benefit of marriage, or under a void marriage (e.g., no license, Art. 36 psychological incapacity, absence of authority with both parties in bad faith).

Rules on property

  • Wages and salaries → equal shares, regardless of who earned them. Sir: "Regardless of actual monetary contribution? Yes … kahit yung asawa nagsusugal lang araw-araw at gabi-gabi? Yes, it does not matter."
  • Property acquired through work or industry → co-ownership. In the absence of proof to the contrary, it is presumed acquired by joint efforts and owned equally. A party whose efforts consisted in the care and maintenance of the family and household is deemed to have contributed jointly.
  • Not covered: property acquired by gratuitous title (inheritance, donation), property acquired before the cohabitation, and property acquired by chance (lottery winnings), because none of these came from "work or industry". They remain the acquiring party's own.
  • No disposition inter vivos of one's share in the common property without the other's consent until the cohabitation ends.
  • Tolentino: in ordinary co-ownership a co-owner may sell his or her undivided share without the others' consent; Art. 147 makes an exception while the cohabitation lasts. After one party dies, the survivor cannot validly sell the common property without the consent of the deceased partner's heirs, who may recover their half from the buyer. A disposition by will is valid, because death ends the relationship and the common property becomes subject to partition.
  • Tolentino (what is covered; shares): the co-ownership covers property acquired through work or industry, wages and salaries, and purchases made with them (substitution), but not the fruits of either party's own property. Under the Civil Code (Art. 144), shares followed each party's contribution, presumed equal absent proof, and a woman who helped maritally and contributed to the prosperity of the union shared at least in proportion to the value of her work. (The Family Code now treats care of the family and household as a joint contribution.)
  • Tolentino (debts and expenses): with no partnership between them, each party is personally liable for his or her own debts, except expenses to preserve the common property, which they bear proportionately (Arts. 485, 488 NCC), enforceable against each one's share. But where the woman buys household necessities and the man usually pays, a tacit agency arises and he is liable to sellers who relied on it. The cohabitants may validly form an ordinary partnership by following the law on partnerships. If they later marry each other, the Court of Appeals once held that what they acquired before becomes conjugal (Woodfine v. Woodfine), but Tolentino finds the contrary view (Ceniza v. Gella: each one's share in the co-ownership is exclusive property brought to the marriage) "more acceptable."
  • Forfeiture (void marriage, one party in good faith): the bad-faith party's share goes to the common children → if they default or waive, to their respective surviving descendants → if none, to the innocent party. Forfeiture takes effect upon termination of the cohabitation.
Uribe (CivRev 2025): "In 147, as far as salaries and wages are concerned, will this be shared proportionately?" No: equal shares under Art. 147. "However, as to the property that may be acquired during this cohabitation or marriage (void), the rules on co-ownership will apply. So if there is no proof, then equal share; if there is proof of contribution, it will follow. Except if the contribution is for the maintenance of the household or family; she or he can claim 50% of the property during cohabitation. This is not applicable to 148."
Maxey v. CA (129 SCRA 187, 1984; under NCC 144)

Melbourne Maxey and Regina Morales lived together as husband and wife, with children, in a union that began before the Civil Code. Properties acquired during the cohabitation were later sold by Maxey, and Regina's children sued to recover their mother's share. Before the Civil Code took effect on August 30, 1950, the courts recognized an informal civil partnership in which common-law spouses had an equal share in property acquired through their joint efforts. Article 144 codified this, "but with the modification that the property governed by the rules on co-ownership may be acquired by either or both of them through their work or industry. Even if it is only the man who works, the property acquired during the man and wife relationship belongs through a fifty-fifty sharing to the two of them." The "real contribution" of a woman includes not only her earnings "but also her contribution to the family's material and spiritual goods through caring for the children, administering the household, husbanding scarce resources, freeing her husband from household tasks, and otherwise performing the traditional duties of a housewife." Held: the properties acquired during the union were co-owned by the partners in equal shares.

Tolentino's summary of Maxey: the partners lived together from 1903 and married in 1919; after the woman's death, the survivor sold, in 1953, land acquired before their legal marriage. The children were entitled to recover one-half from the buyer, and a co-ownership arises "even if common-law wife does not work or is not gainfully employed."
Tolentino on Art. 147 vs. Arts. 43 and 50 in void marriages. Art. 147 sets up a special co-ownership in void marriages, implying no ACP or CPG; but Art. 50, with Art. 43(2), expressly recognizes an ACP or CPG in certain void marriages (those under Art. 40). "It is extremely difficult to offer a solution to this inconsistency." Courts should choose the more equitable rule in each case, and forfeitures should be strictly construed. Forfeiture under Art. 43(2) reaches the guilty party's share in the net profits (which may include fruits and all acquisitions), while forfeiture under Art. 147 reaches only the share in property acquired through work or industry; Tolentino prefers the latter because it deprives less. (Compare Valdes, which applies Art. 147 to Art. 36 nullity and reserves Arts. 50–52 for Arts. 40 and 45.)
Salas, Jr. v. Aguila (G.R. No. 202370, September 23, 2013)

The marriage of Juan Sevilla Salas, Jr. and Eden Aguila was declared void for psychological incapacity. Eden later found properties (the "Discovered Properties") registered in Salas's name and in the name of "Juan S. Salas, married to Rubina C. Salas", and asked for their partition. Held: the regime is co-ownership under Art. 147, not a conjugal partnership. "Under this property regime, property acquired during the marriage is prima facie presumed to have been obtained through the couple's joint efforts and governed by the rules on co-ownership." Salas did not rebut the presumption, and Rubina, who claimed the properties as her own, failed to prove she had bought them with her own money (the words "married to Rubina" are merely descriptive). The partition was sustained, "but on the basis of co-ownership and not on the regime of conjugal partnership of gains."

Valdes v. RTC, Quezon City (G.R. No. 122749, July 31, 1996; cited in the bar answers)

In a marriage declared void under Art. 36, the property relations are governed by Art. 147, and the properties are liquidated under the rules on co-ownership, not Arts. 102 or 129. Arts. 50–52 (liquidation, presumptive legitimes, recording) apply only to marriages declared void under Art. 40 and to annulled marriages. Diño v. Diño (G.R. No. 178044, January 19, 2011) applied this: for an Art. 36 void marriage under Art. 147, delivery of the presumptive legitime is not a precondition to the issuance of the decree. This is the basis of the 2018 bar answer that Shalimar is not entitled to presumptive legitime in the Sancho–Sandy liquidation.

2016 Bar: Bernard and Dorothy, both capacitated to marry, lived together as common-law spouses. Dorothy worked in Dubai and sent money to Bernard, who used it to buy a lot and later sold it without her consent. (a) Will her suit to nullify the sale prosper? (b) Same, if she was jobless and only cared for the family and household?

(a) Yes, unless the buyer bought in good faith and for value. Under Art. 147, neither party may dispose of his or her share in the property acquired during the cohabitation without the other's consent until the cohabitation ends. (b) Yes, still. Her care and maintenance of the family and household is deemed a joint contribution, so the lot is co-owned equally, and her consent remains necessary. (The UP BOC note gives credit to examinees who reasoned that Art. 147 does not apply because they were not living together as husband and wife while she was abroad.)

2023 Bar: Santiago and Lucia lived together as common-law spouses from 2000. They acquired several properties, mainly from Lucia's salon and water-refilling business. The purchase records were destroyed by Typhoon Ondoy. In 2023 Lucia sued, claiming all the properties. Is she correct?

No, assuming they were capacitated to marry each other. Under Art. 147, properties acquired during the cohabitation are presumed obtained by joint efforts and owned equally, and a party who cared for the household is deemed to have contributed (Paterno v. Paterno, 2020; Barrido v. Nonato, 2014). The burden of proving otherwise is on Lucia, and she has no records. (If they were not capacitated, Art. 148 would require proof of Santiago's actual contribution.)

2010 Bar: G and B married on July 3, 1989. In 2001, the marriage was declared void under Art. 36. They had (1) a house and lot bought by B on installment on August 3, 1988 (one-third down payment from B; one-third paid by G in 1990 from a cash gift her parents gave her at graduation in 1989; the rest from their joint income) and (2) an apartment unit donated to B by an uncle in 1987. Who owns them?

No ACP or CPG ever existed. Art. 147 governs (they were capacitated). If B acquired ownership of the house and lot on August 3, 1988, before the marriage, it is his, subject to reimbursing G for her payment and her half of the payments from joint income. If ownership was acquired during the cohabitation, it is co-owned: one-third is B's (his premarital down payment), one-third is G's (her gift money, which is not wages), and one-third is co-owned equally (joint income). Altogether they hold it in equal shares. The apartment is B's alone (acquired before the cohabitation, and by gratuitous title). If they had married in 1987, the result would be the same: NCC 144 is substantially identical, and Art. 147 applies retroactively under Art. 256 because no vested right is impaired.

1997 Bar: Luis and Rizza, both 26 and single, lived together for 20 years. Luis worked, and Rizza kept house. They have (a) ₱200,000 of Luis's savings from his salary; (b) a house and lot worth ₱500,000 bought with ₱200,000 from the sale of palay harvested from Luis's hacienda and ₱300,000 from the rentals of Rizza's building; (c) a car worth ₱100,000 donated to Rizza by her parents. Allocation?

(a) Salary: equal shares (Art. 147). (b) Acquired through the fruits of their separate properties, so co-owned in proportion to contributions: Luis two-fifths, Rizza three-fifths (UPLC answer). (c) Car: Rizza's alone (gratuitous title). Had they been validly married 20 years earlier (under the NCC), the CPG would apply, and the salary, the house and lot (bought from fruits) would be conjugal and divided equally, while the car would be Rizza's exclusive property.

Art. 148: co-ownership limited to actual joint contribution

Art. 148, FC. In cases of cohabitation not falling under the preceding Article, only the properties acquired by both of the parties through their actual joint contribution of money, property, or industry shall be owned by them in common in proportion to their respective contributions. In the absence of proof to the contrary, their contributions and corresponding shares are presumed to be equal. The same rule and presumption shall apply to joint deposits of money and evidences of credit.
If one of the parties is validly married to another, his or her share in the co-ownership shall accrue to the absolute community or conjugal partnership existing in such valid marriage. If the party who acted in bad faith is not validly married to another, his or her share shall be forfeited in the manner provided in the last paragraph of the preceding Article.
The foregoing rules on forfeiture shall likewise apply even if both parties are in bad faith. (144a)

Rules

  • Co-ownership arises only over property acquired through actual joint contribution of money, property or industry, owned in proportion to the contributions.
  • The presumption of equal shares arises only once the fact of actual joint contribution is shown, and it covers the extent of each contribution. There is no presumption that property was acquired jointly (Ventura; Go-Bangayan; Borromeo v. Descallar).
  • Care of the home, children and household, and "spiritual or moral inspiration", are not contributions (Cariño).
  • Wages and salaries belong to the earner (Cariño). If the earner is validly married to someone else, they belong to that marriage's ACP or CPG (2009 Bar).
  • Share of a party validly married to another → accrues to the ACP or CPG of the valid marriage. Share of a bad-faith party not validly married to another → forfeited as in Art. 147. The forfeiture rules apply even if both are in bad faith.
  • Tolentino (bad faith in adulterous cohabitation): the share of the party who is not married to another stays with him or her if he or she did not know of the other's marriage. If he or she knew when the cohabitation began, the share is forfeited to the common children or descendants. If there are none, Art. 147 would give it to the innocent party, but under Art. 148 there is no "innocent" party, since the other is married. The last paragraph (forfeiture "even if both parties are in bad faith") should apply, Tolentino says, only when there are common children or descendants. Without them, the bad-faith unmarried party's share should stay with him or her: "to forfeit it in favor of the married party would be rewarding infidelity."
  • Tolentino (adulterous relations and the legal spouse): Art. 147 does not cover adulterous cohabitation. The legal spouses' ACP or CPG continues, so no co-ownership can exist between a spouse and the paramour over that spouse's earnings or work; Art. 148's second paragraph allows co-ownership only of what the two acquired by actual joint contribution. In Layson v. Oliquino (CA, 47 O.G. 4216), a husband bought a lot and built a house with his earnings, titled in his mistress's name; they were conjugal property of the legal marriage, and the "gift" to the mistress could not prejudice the wife.
Juaniza v. Jose (89 SCRA 306, 1979)

Eugenio Jose, a married man, lived with Rosalia Arroyo. His jeepney, registered in his name, figured in a collision, and the victims sued both. Held: the co-ownership under NCC 144 "requires that the man and the woman living together must not in any way be incapacitated to contract marriage." Since Jose was legally married to Socorro Ramos, "Arroyo cannot be a co-owner of the jeepney. The jeepney belongs to the conjugal partnership of Jose and his legal wife." There was therefore no basis to hold Arroyo liable for damages as a co-owner. (This is the rule now codified in Art. 148.)

Gomez v. Lipana (33 SCRA 615, 1970)

The husband married a second wife (1935) while his first marriage subsisted. (1) A bigamous marriage, void under the Marriage Law, may be collaterally attacked in the intestate proceedings. (2) The party invoking the exceptions under Sec. 29 of the Marriage Law (absence of the first spouse) bears the burden of proving them. (3) The second wife could not invoke Art. 1417 of the Spanish Civil Code to forfeit the husband's share, because that partnership terminated on his death in 1958, when the Spanish Code was no longer in force. The Court divided the property acquired during the second union: the second wife's half was recognized as hers, and the husband's half pertained to the conjugal partnership of the first, subsisting marriage (as summarized in Consuegra).

Vda. de Consuegra v. GSIS (37 SCRA 315, 1971)

Jose Consuegra married Rosario Diaz and later, while the first marriage subsisted, married Basilia Berdin in good faith. The GSIS divided his retirement insurance proceeds equally between the first wife and the second wife with her children. Held: correct. Citing Gomez v. Lipana, the first marriage was never dissolved, so its conjugal partnership never ceased. As to the second wife, although the second marriage was void, "the only just and equitable solution in this case would be to recognize the right of the second wife to her share of one-half in the property acquired by her and her husband and consider the other half as pertaining to the conjugal partnership of the first marriage."

Yap v. CA (145 SCRA 229, 1986)

Maning Yap's first marriage, to Talina Bianong, was valid. His second marriage, to Nancy Yap, was void under the Marriage Law (Act 3613). Held: the second "wife" got no share in the estate of the husband; only the legal wife did. (A bigamous "spouse" is not an heir and has no share in property she did not help acquire.)

Cariño v. Cariño (351 SCRA 127, February 2, 2001)

SPO4 Santiago Cariño married Susan Nicdao (1969, with no marriage license) and then Susan Yee (1992) while the first marriage had not been judicially declared void. Both "wives" claimed his death benefits. (1) A judicial declaration of nullity of the prior marriage is required before remarriage (Art. 40), so the second marriage is also void. "Considering that the two marriages are void ab initio, the applicable property regime would not be absolute community or conjugal partnership of property, but rather, be governed by the provisions of Articles 147 and 148." (2) Under Art. 148, which covers "bigamous marriages, adulterous relationships, relationships in a state of concubine, relationships where both man and woman are married to other persons, multiple alliances of the same married man," the properties acquired through actual joint contribution belong to the co-ownership. "Wages and salaries earned by each party belong to him or her exclusively. Then too, contributions in the form of care of the home, children and household, or spiritual or moral inspiration, are excluded in this regime." Susan Yee, having made no contribution, had no share in the benefits. (3) The first marriage, void for lack of a license, falls under Art. 147, so the death benefits from his salary were owned equally by Nicdao and Santiago. His half passed to his legal heirs, his children with Nicdao, not to Nicdao herself, since she was not his legal spouse.

Go-Bangayan v. Bangayan (G.R. No. 201061, July 3, 2013)

Benjamin Bangayan, married to Azucena, lived with Sally Go and signed a purported marriage contract with her. The marriage was void (and, the Court found, inexistent, the supposed license being fictitious). Several properties were titled in the name of "Benjamin, married to Sally". Held: "the words 'married to' preceding the name of a spouse are merely descriptive of the civil status of the registered owner. Such words do not prove co-ownership. Without proof of actual contribution from either or both spouses, there can be no co-ownership under Article 148."

Ventura, Jr. v. Spouses Abuda (G.R. No. 202932, October 23, 2013)

Socorro married Esteban Abuda while her prior marriage subsisted, so the union fell under Art. 148. The petitioner, Edilberto, claimed shares in the properties through that union. Held: as Edilberto himself admitted, quoting Borromeo v. Descallar, "it is necessary for each of the partners to prove his or her actual contributions to the acquisition of property in order to be able to lay claim to any portion of it. Presumptions of co-ownership and equal contribution do not apply." Without proof of Socorro's actual contribution, no co-ownership arose.

2024 Bar (and CivRev 2025 slide): In 2021, Jesse and Celeste got married. After a year, Jesse took all their savings and abandoned Celeste for his paramour, Diana. Jesse asked Diana to quit her job. Diana hesitated because she had no money and was drowning in debt, but Jesse promised to provide for all her needs. They lived together, and the next day they bought a brand-new car registered exclusively in Diana's name. Celeste sued to recover the car, claiming it is part of her community property. Diana countered that she and Jesse are co-owners. Can Celeste recover the car?

Yes. Jesse is not capacitated to marry Diana (he is married to Celeste), so Art. 148 governs. Diana, jobless and in debt, could not have contributed to the purchase. Her claim of co-ownership fails, and registration in her name does not prove ownership. The money came from Jesse's and Celeste's savings, which are community property (Arts. 91, 93, 75). In any case, Jesse's share in any co-ownership with Diana would accrue to the ACP (Art. 148, 2nd par.). Sir in class: argue from the facts and the law, not by conclusion ("the money used was the salary only of the husband … Diana has no money and she is drowning in debts"). Would it matter if Diana cooked and kept house for Jesse? No. "Art. 148, contribution by way of maintenance of the household does not matter. It is only available if it would fall under 147."

UP BOC 2011 MCQ: Venecio and Ester lived as common-law spouses, both still married to others from whom they had been separated in fact for years. Each earned income from their own profession and enterprise. Nature of their incomes?

B: separate, since their property relations with their legal spouses are still subsisting. Each income belongs to the earner's ACP or CPG with his or her lawful spouse (Art. 148).

UP BOC 2009 TF: "If there is no marriage settlement, the salary of a 'spouse' in an adulterous marriage belongs to the conjugal partnership of gains."

False. The salary of a married partner belongs to the ACP or CPG of his or her lawful marriage. The paramour is deemed not to have contributed to earning it (Art. 148).

2000 / 1997 / 1992 Bar: In 1989, Rico (a widower, 40) cohabited with Cora (a widow, 30), and they bought rice land from their combined earnings. They separated, and Rico lived with Mabel (16), who kept house while Rico bought coconut land from his savings. They separated a year later, and Rico married Letty, who bought a mango orchard from her own earnings during the marriage. Who owns each, under what regime?

Rice land: Rico and Cora as co-owners (Art. 147; both capacitated). After Rico married Letty, his half became ACP property of Rico and Letty. Coconut land: Rico alone. Mabel, at 16, was not capacitated to marry, so Art. 148 applies, and her housekeeping is not a contribution. It later became ACP property of Rico and Letty (owned at the celebration). Mango orchard: ACP of Rico and Letty (acquired during the marriage; Arts. 75, 91).

2000 Bar: Tony (a bank vice-president) and Susan (an entertainer who stopped working and kept house) lived together for five years from 1989, both capacitated to marry, and Tony bought a house and lot. (a) Who is entitled to it? (b) Would it differ if Tony could not marry Susan because he was legally separated from his wife, Alice?

(a) Tony and Susan are co-owners in equal shares (Art. 147; her care of the household counts). (b) Yes. A legally separated spouse is still married, so Art. 148 applies, Susan's housekeeping is not a contribution, and she gets nothing. If Tony cohabited with Susan after the decree of legal separation, the house and lot is his exclusive property (the ACP or CPG with Alice having been dissolved). If before, it belongs to his community or partnership with Alice.

1991 Bar: In June 1985, James married Mary. In September 1988, he also married Ophelia, and they had two children and acquired a residential lot. Mary died in July 1989. In July 1990, James married Shirley. What regime governed James and Ophelia?

Art. 148. The marriage to Ophelia was bigamous and void, and James was not capacitated to marry her. Only property acquired through their actual joint contribution is co-owned in proportion to their contributions, and James's share accrued to his CPG with Mary while that marriage subsisted.

Checklist: Arts. 147–148

  1. No valid marriage, or a void marriage. Voidable marriages use the ACP or CPG.
  2. Art. 147: a man and a woman, capacitated to marry each other, living exclusively as husband and wife. Wages and salaries → equal. Property acquired through work or industry → co-owned, presumed joint and equal; care of the household counts. No disposition of one's share without consent until the cohabitation ends. Forfeiture of the bad-faith party's share → common children → descendants → innocent party, upon termination of the cohabitation.
  3. Art. 148: all other cohabitations. Co-ownership only over what was acquired by actual joint contribution, in proportion; equal shares are presumed only as to the amount once contribution is shown; household care does not count; wages belong to the earner; the share of a party validly married to another → that marriage's ACP or CPG; forfeiture rules apply even if both are in bad faith.
  4. Sir's rule of thumb: a third person affected (either party married to another) → 148; otherwise → 147.
  5. Gratuitous acquisitions and premarital property are never part of either co-ownership. "Married to" in a title is descriptive only (Go-Bangayan; Salas).
  6. Art. 147 liquidation of a void Art. 36 marriage follows co-ownership; no presumptive legitimes are required (Valdes; Diño).
