Emancipation; Care and Education of Children; Summary Judicial Proceedings

Family Code, Titles X and XI, Arts. 234–253 (syllabus XVII–XVIII), as amended by R.A. 6809, with NCC 356–363 on the care and education of children. Emancipation at 18 and what still follows the child to 21; and the summary procedure the Family Code uses for spousal consent, parental-authority incidents, presumptive death and other matters.

Emancipation (Arts. 234–237, as amended by R.A. 6809)

Art. 234, FC (as amended by R.A. 6809, approved December 13, 1989). Emancipation takes place by the attainment of majority. Unless otherwise provided, majority commences at the age of eighteen years.
Art. 236, FC (as amended by R.A. 6809). Emancipation shall terminate parental authority over the person and property of the child who shall then be qualified and responsible for all acts of civil life, save the exceptions established by existing laws in special cases.
Contracting marriage shall require parental consent until the age of twenty-one.
Nothing in this Code shall be construed to derogate from the duty or responsibility of parents and guardians for children and wards below twenty-one years of age mentioned in the second and third paragraphs of Article 2180 of the Civil Code.
Arts. 235 and 237, FC. Repealed by R.A. 6809.
Uribe (CivRev Quiz 2, item 3): "Any contracting party between the age of twenty-one and twenty-five shall be obliged to ask their parents or guardian for consent." False: advice, not consent (Art. 15). Consent is required for parties between 18 and 21 (Art. 14). Sir then asked: if a 19-year-old who was previously married marries again, is parental consent needed? No: Art. 14 requires consent only from parties "not having been emancipated by a previous marriage". "Will you maintain that [consent] is for the specific person or for the FACT that their child is getting married? If unemancipated, then parental consent is needed; therefore a 19-year-old previously married does not need consent … because the child had already got married. This is a better view." (The same point was keyed in the CivRev midterm: a 19-year-old widow did not need parental consent.)
Tolentino (Arts. 234–237): R.A. 6809 protects existing wills, bequests, donations, insurance policies and similar instruments favorable to minors from prejudicial retroactivity. Requiring parental consent for the marriage of an emancipated 18-to-21-year-old is "an anomalous situation": he may enter any contract but not marry without consent. Keeping the parents' liability for torts of children below 21 is "more anomalous": "upon emancipation … parental authority ceases, and yet responsibility for his torts continues until he reaches 21 years of age. This is a case of responsibility without authority." If both parents are dead and the child is released from guardianship at 18, no one else answers; the child is personally liable. On marriage: a person below 18 whose marriage is declared void reverts to parental authority as a legal consequence of the marriage's nonexistence, despite the repeal of Art. 237; but a voidable marriage annulled on any ground involves someone already over 18 and emancipated by age, so there is nothing to revert to.

1993 Bar: Julio, 18 and living with his parents, shot and killed Jake at a party with his father's .38-calibre revolver, taken from an unlocked drawer in his father's bedroom. Jake's parents sued Julio's parents for damages based on quasi-delict. They moved to dismiss, saying Julio was already of age. (a) Should the motion be granted? (b) What is the parents' liability?

(a) No. Art. 236, 3rd par., as amended by R.A. 6809: "Nothing in this Code shall be construed to derogate from the duty or responsibility of parents and guardians for children and wards below twenty-one years of age mentioned in the second and third paragraphs of Article 2180." Julio was below 21 and living in their company. (b) Liability for quasi-delict: indemnity for death, loss of earning capacity, and moral damages; they may escape only by proving the diligence of a good father of a family, which the unlocked gun drawer belies.

Art. 236 vs. the 2010 Rozanno answer: the suggested answer to the 2010 Bar (see Parental Authority) said that at 21 Rozanno alone is liable and Arts. 218, 219 and 221 no longer apply. That is consistent: Arts. 218–221 cover unemancipated minors; Art. 236's 21-year line preserves only the NCC 2180 liability of parents and guardians for children below 21. Between 18 and 21, the school's special parental authority is gone but the parents' NCC 2180 liability remains.

Care and education of children (NCC 356–363)

Art. 356, NCC. Every child: (1) Is entitled to parental care; (2) Shall receive at least elementary education; (3) Shall be given moral and civic training by the parents or guardian; (4) Has a right to live in an atmosphere conducive to his physical, moral and intellectual development.
Art. 357, NCC. Every child shall: (1) Obey and honor his parents or guardian; (2) Respect his grandparents, old relatives, and persons holding substitute parental authority; (3) Exert his utmost for his education and training; (4) Cooperate with the family in all matters that make for the good of the same.
Art. 363, NCC. In all questions on the care, custody, education and property of children, the latter's welfare shall be paramount. No mother shall be separated from her child under seven years of age, unless the court finds compelling reasons for such measure.

Summary judicial proceedings (Arts. 238–253)

Art. 238, FC. Until modified by the Supreme Court, the procedural rules provided for in this Title shall apply as regards separation in fact between husband and wife, abandonment by one of the other, and incidents involving parental authority. (n)
Art. 239, FC. When a husband and wife are separated in fact, or one has abandoned the other and one of them seeks judicial authorization for a transaction where the consent of the other spouse is required by law but such consent is withheld or cannot be obtained, a verified petition may be filed in court alleging the foregoing facts.
The petition shall attach the proposed deed, if any, embodying the transaction, and, if none, shall describe in detail the said transaction and state the reason why the required consent thereto cannot be secured. In any case, the final deed duly executed by the parties shall be submitted to and approved by the court. (n)
Arts. 240–248, FC (summary). Claims for damages by either spouse, except costs, only in a separate action (240). Jurisdiction, upon proof of notice to the other spouse, in the family court or the RTC where either spouse resides (241). The court notifies the other spouse to show cause on or before the initial conference; notice with a copy of the petition, served at the last known address (242). The preliminary conference is conducted by the judge personally, without counsel; counsel may assist afterwards (243). Non-appearance: the court inquires and requires appearance if possible (244); if still not secured, it may proceed ex parte, protecting the absent spouse's interests (245). If unresolved at the initial conference, a summary hearing on affidavits, documents or oral testimony (246). The judgment is immediately final and executory (247). The same rules govern a petition to administer or encumber the separate property of the abandoning spouse for the family's support (248).
Arts. 249–253, FC (summary). Petitions under Arts. 223, 225 and 235 involving parental authority shall be verified (249) and filed where the child resides (250); the court notifies the parents or those exercising parental authority (251); Chapter 2 rules apply (252). "The foregoing rules in Chapters 2 and 3 hereof shall likewise govern summary proceedings filed under Articles 41, 51, 69, 73, 96, 124 and 217, insofar as they are applicable." (253)
Tolentino (Arts. 238–253): the rules are procedural and the Supreme Court may modify any of them under its rule-making power. Tolentino prints Art. 238 in a broader form, applying the Title "in all cases provided for in this Code requiring summary court proceedings", to be "decided in an expeditious manner, without regard to technical rules"; the official text limits it to separation in fact, abandonment, and parental-authority incidents, with Art. 253 extending it to the listed articles.
Consent first, court second (class reviewer, under Arts. 100 and 127): judicial authorization cannot take the place of the separated spouse's consent if that spouse is willing to give it; it is subsidiary, available only when consent is withheld or cannot be obtained.
Republic v. Bermudez-Lorino (G.R. No. 160258, January 19, 2005; not in your sources)

A wife obtained a declaration of the presumptive death of her husband under Art. 41; the Republic appealed to the CA by notice of appeal. Held: the appeal should not have been entertained. "In Summary Judicial Proceedings under the Family Code, there is no reglementary period within which to perfect an appeal, precisely because judgments rendered thereunder, by express provision of Article 247, Family Code, … are 'immediately final and executory'." The remedy is a petition for certiorari under Rule 65. (Reaffirmed in Republic v. Granada, G.R. No. 187512, June 13, 2012.)

Quiz traps (summary proceedings): "The parties must be assisted by counsel at the preliminary conference" → false: without counsel; counsel may assist afterwards (Art. 243). "Damages may be claimed in the summary petition" → false: separate action, except costs (Art. 240). "The judgment may be appealed within 15 days" → false: immediately final and executory (Art. 247); certiorari. "If the other spouse does not appear, the petition is dismissed" → false: the court may proceed ex parte (Art. 245). "Venue is where the petitioner resides" → incomplete: where either spouse resides (Art. 241); parental-authority petitions, where the child resides (Art. 250).
