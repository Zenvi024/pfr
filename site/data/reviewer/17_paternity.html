Paternity and Filiation I: Status and Impugning Legitimacy

Title VI, Family Code, Arts. 163–171 and 174–176 (syllabus XIII.A–D). Covers the kinds of filiation (Art. 163); who are legitimate (Art. 164, read with Arts. 42–43, 54 and 53, voidable marriages, adoption and legitimation); illegitimate children (Art. 165); the rights of legitimate and illegitimate children (Arts. 174, 176); and the action to impugn legitimacy: grounds (Art. 166), the mother's declaration (Art. 167), subsequent marriages (Arts. 168–169), periods (Art. 170) and who may sue (Art. 171). Sir flagged this chapter: "paternity and filiation (focus here and adoption); the rest are almost codal." Proof of filiation and legitimation are in the next section.

Kinds of filiation (Art. 163); vocabulary

Art. 163, FC. The filiation of children may be by nature or by adoption. Natural filiation may be legitimate or illegitimate. (n)
Uribe (Quiz 5, CivRev 2025): an MCQ defined "the legal bond or relationship of being a person's offspring" and offered legitimacy, paternity and maternity; the answer was E, none of the above: filiation. Paternity is the father's relationship, maternity the mother's, legitimacy a status. On impugning, Sir noted that the law sometimes says "impugn the legitimacy" and sometimes "impugn the filiation" (Art. 171); both are acceptable, but what is really attacked is the husband's paternity.
Tolentino (Title VI intro): paternity and filiation are the relation between parents and children, arising from nature (generation) or from a fiction of law (adoption). Generation in wedlock produces legitimate children; generation outside it, illegitimate children. The kinds of children are: legitimate, legitimated, illegitimate and adopted.

Who are legitimate (Art. 164 and related articles)

Art. 164, FC. Children conceived or born during the marriage of the parents are legitimate.
Children conceived as a result of artificial insemination of the wife with the sperm of the husband or that of a donor or both are likewise legitimate children of the husband and his wife, provided, that both of them authorized or ratified such insemination in a written instrument executed and signed by them before the birth of the child. The instrument shall be recorded in the civil registry together with the birth certificate of the child. (55a, 258a)
  1. Conceived during the marriage, even if born after it ends (e.g., the husband dies while the wife is pregnant). Cf. the disputable presumption of legitimacy in Rule 131 of the Rules of Court.
  2. Born during the marriage, even if conceived before it (the bride was already pregnant at the wedding).
  3. Artificial insemination with the husband's sperm, a donor's, or both, if both spouses authorized or ratified it in a written instrument executed and signed by them before the birth; the instrument is recorded with the birth certificate.
  4. Art. 43(1): children of a subsequent marriage under Art. 41 (presumptive death) conceived before its termination by the reappearance of the absent spouse.
  5. Art. 54: children conceived or born before the judgment of nullity under Art. 36 (psychological incapacity) becomes final, and children of a subsequent marriage that is void under Art. 53 (remarriage without complying with Art. 52).
  6. Voidable marriages: valid until annulled, so children conceived or born before the decree of annulment becomes final are legitimate (Art. 54).
  7. Adopted children: legitimate children of the adopter by legal fiction.
  8. Legitimated children: enjoy the same rights as legitimate children (Art. 179).
Art. 54, FC. Children conceived or born before the judgment of annulment or absolute nullity of the marriage under Article 36 has become final and executory shall be considered legitimate. Children conceived or born of the subsequent marriage under Article 53 shall likewise be legitimate.
Uribe (Quiz 1 and Quiz 5): "Children born after the dissolution of the marriage of the parents may be legitimate" → true (Art. 164: conceived during the marriage). Sir: think of the husband's death while the wife is pregnant; do not cite void marriages, because "there is nothing to dissolve if it is void". On a legitimacy question, never answer "legitimated": "legitimation happens AFTER, hindi upon birth. It presupposes na illegitimate ang bata." On artificial insemination, Sir treats the written authorization or ratification as a requirement for the child to be legitimate: a student who answered that the child is legitimate even without it ("because of the presumption of legitimacy") was made to correct himself. The instrument must be written and signed before the birth; it need not be published or notarized.
Uribe (Quiz 5, item 1; recitation, Sep. 27, 2025): "To be a legitimate child, he/she must be conceived or born during the marriage of the parents" → false: legitimate children also include children of Art. 36 and Art. 53 marriages (Art. 54), adopted children, and legitimated children (whose status changes later). In recitation: "If the child was born AFTER the declaration of nullity of the marriage, the child is an illegitimate child" → not necessarily (Art. 54). But Sir warned that the Art. 54 exception covers only Art. 36 and Art. 53 marriages: "In all other grounds of nullity, dapat illegitimate child siya." A marriage void for lack of a license produces illegitimate children even if they were conceived before the judgment. And do not argue "best interest of the child" to make an illegitimate child legitimate: "unfair yun sa ibang mga anak … mababawasan yung share nung iba." Older quizzes keyed the MCQ "For a child to be legitimate, he must be: … (d) conceived or born during the marriage" as (d), the best of the choices given.
Uribe (situationers): (1) "If spouses A and B (sperm of A, egg of B) were inseminated in C [a third woman]. Would this child be a legitimate child of A and B?" No. "The FC does not recognize the concept of surrogacy." Art. 164 speaks of insemination of the wife. (2) "A and B are spouses. During the lifetime of A, B was in cohabitation with C … the child X was born after the death of A, and during the subsequent marriage of B and C. Was X legitimated?" No. "X was already legitimate because he was born within a valid marriage. Legitimation presupposes that the child was born illegitimate." (3) Bar question: a husband proved by DNA that the child was not his, but filed only for annulment. Status of the child? Legitimate, because no action to impugn was brought.
Tolentino (Art. 164): a legitimate child is one born in lawful wedlock or within a competent time after it; it is enough that the child was born after the celebration even if begotten before it (Rex v. Luffe). The Family Code's "conceived or born" covers both a child conceived before and born during the marriage and one conceived during and born after its dissolution. On artificial insemination: dual consent is required, whether the semen is the husband's or a donor's. The Code does not require the husband to be impotent, which Tolentino calls the "better position" (a potent but sterile husband is covered). Without the husband's consent, the child is illegitimate, and the wife's submission to donor insemination has been treated as adultery abroad (Orford v. Orford, Ontario: the essence of adultery is "introducing into the family of the husband a false strain of blood").
Conflict: is the written instrument essential? Tolentino reads the written instrument executed before the birth, and its recording, as evidence only: consent may be proved by other admissible evidence, as a marriage may be proved without the marriage certificate. And if the husband was living with the wife and was not impotent, a child of secret donor insemination is legitimate unless the husband impugns it under Art. 166(2) or (3); doubt is resolved in favor of legitimacy. The text says "provided, that both of them authorized or ratified such insemination in a written instrument executed and signed by them before the birth", and Sir treats it as a requirement (Quiz 5). For Sir's quizzes, follow the text: no written consent signed before the birth, no legitimacy by artificial insemination (Quiz 5, item 2: "Children conceived as a result of artificial insemination of the wife with the sperm of the husband are legitimate children of the husband and his wife" → false, because it omits the proviso). The 2006 bar answer (UPLC) sides with Tolentino: a child of donor insemination born during the marriage is presumed the husband's legitimate child even without written consent, and the lack of consent is only a ground to impugn. Another bar reviewer answers the opposite. Watch also the 2025 MCQ that changed "written instrument" to "public instrument": the answer became "none of the above" ("Written lang, di naman kailangan published").

Cases on legitimate children

Perido v. Perido (63 SCRA 97, 1975)

Lucio Perido had children by his first wife and, after her death, by Marcelina Baliguat. The heirs of the first marriage attacked the legitimacy of Marcelina's children by claiming that Lucio and Marcelina were never married, relying on Lucio's civil status in certificates of title issued in 1923 and on a witness who had not attended the ceremony. Held: the children are legitimate. "A person who was not at the marriage ceremony cannot testify as an eyewitness that the marriage did not take place especially if the testimony is uncorroborated. In the absence of proof that marriage did not take place a man and a woman living together as husband and wife are presumed married." The presumption, "especially where legitimacy of the issue is involved", may be overcome only by cogent proof from those alleging illegitimacy.

De la Puerta v. CA (181 SCRA 861, 1990; printed in the syllabus as "De la Purrta, 161 SCRA 661")

Carmelita, a child of Vicente de la Puerta by a woman not his wife, claimed a share in the estate of Vicente's mother, Dominga Revuelta, by representing her father, who had predeceased Dominga. Held: she cannot inherit. "As a spurious child of Vicente, Carmelita is barred from inheriting from Dominga because of Article 992 of the Civil Code, which lays down the barrier between the legitimate and illegitimate families." (Note: Aquino v. Aquino, G.R. Nos. 208912 and 209018, December 7, 2021, later allowed nonmarital children to inherit from grandparents by right of representation in some situations; Sir cited Aquino in class.)

Tan v. Trocio (A.C. No. 2115, 191 SCRA 764, 1990)

In a disbarment case, the complainant alleged that the respondent lawyer had sexually abused her and that her son Jewel, born in 1972 during her marriage to Tan Le Pok, was the respondent's child. Her evidence: testimony of the respondent's "unusual closeness" to the boy (playing with him, giving him toys) and photographs said to show their physical likeness. Held: complaint dismissed. "Jewel Tan was born in 1972, during wedlock of Complainant and her husband and the presumption should be in favor of legitimacy unless physical access between the couple was impossible. From the evidence on hand, that presumption has not been overcome by adequate and convincing proof. In fact, Jewel was registered in his birth certificate as the legitimate child of the Complainant and her husband." Pictures showing physical likeness are "inconclusive to prove paternity". (Listed twice in the syllabus: under legitimate children and under physical impossibility.)

Illegitimate children (Art. 165)

Art. 165, FC. Children conceived and born outside a valid marriage are illegitimate, unless otherwise provided in this Code. (n)
  • Conceived and born outside a valid marriage. Compare Art. 164's "conceived or born during".
  • "Unless otherwise provided": Arts. 54 (void under 36 and 53) and 43(1) give legitimacy despite a defective marriage; legitimation (Art. 177) and adoption change the status later.
  • Under the NCC there were (1) natural children (parents could have married each other at conception), (2) natural children by legal fiction (mostly children of void marriages), and (3) other illegitimate or "spurious" children (adulterous, incestuous, sacrilegious, manceres). The Family Code has one class: illegitimate.
Tolentino (Arts. 165 and Ch. 3): the Family Code abolished the classes; the only remaining difference, which is not formally named, is between (1) children of parents who were not disqualified by any impediment to marry each other when the child was conceived (formerly "natural", and legitimable), and (2) all other illegitimate children (not legitimable). The distinction matters only for legitimation. There are no more "sacrilegious" children (religious profession is not an impediment), and no law classifies children as illegitimate merely because the mother is a prostitute.
Mangulabnan v. IAC (185 SCRA 760, 1990)

A minor, Alfie, sued his alleged father, a married man, for support. Held: support may be granted. "An illegitimate child like the minor Alfie in this case whose father … is married and had no legal capacity to contract marriage at the time of his conception is not a natural child but an illegitimate child or spurious child in which case recognition is not required before support may be granted. There must be a declaration of the status of the child from which the right to support is derived and before support can be ordered. Such a declaration may be provisional, that is, by affidavits."

Rights of legitimate and illegitimate children (Arts. 174, 176)

Art. 174, FC. Legitimate children shall have the right: (1) To bear the surnames of the father and the mother, in conformity with the provisions of the Civil Code on Surnames; (2) To receive support from their parents, their ascendants, and in proper cases, their brothers and sisters, in conformity with the provisions of this Code on Support; and (3) To be entitled to the legitime and other successional rights granted to them by the Civil Code. (264a)
Art. 176, FC (as amended by R.A. No. 9255). Illegitimate children shall use the surname and shall be under the parental authority of their mother, and shall be entitled to support in conformity with this Code. However, illegitimate children may use the surname of their father if their filiation has been expressly recognized by the father through the record of birth appearing in the civil register, or when an admission in a public document or private handwritten instrument is made by the father. Provided, the father has the right to institute an action before the regular courts to prove non-filiation during his lifetime. The legitime of each illegitimate child shall consist of one-half of the legitime of a legitimate child. (287a)
Uribe (Quiz 5): a statement framing the illegitimate child's legitime as half of what the legitimate children get collectively was false: "the legitime of each illegitimate child shall consist of one-half of the legitime of a legitimate child". With two or more legitimate children, take one legitimate child's share and halve it.
Moore v. Republic (8 SCRA 282, 1963)

Elaine Moore, an American, married Joseph Velarde and had a son; the marriage was dissolved by a California court, and she married Don Moore. She petitioned for her son to use the surname "Moore", his stepfather's. Held: denied. Philippine law does not authorize a legitimate child to use the surname of a person who is not his father. "If a child born out of a lawful wedlock be allowed to bear the surname of the second husband of the mother, should the first husband die or be separated by a decree of divorce, there may result a confusion to his real paternity. In the long run the change may redound to the prejudice of the child in the community."

Naldoza v. Republic (112 SCRA 568, 1982; Tolentino and Uribe's Doctrines print 658)

Zosima Naldoza's husband left her when she confronted him about an earlier marriage, and he was said to have swindled several people; their sons were teased at school. She petitioned to change the boys' surname from Divinagracia to Naldoza. Held: denied. "To allow them, at their mother's behest, to bear only their mother's surname (which they are entitled to use together with their father's surname) and to discard altogether their father's surname, thus removing the prima-facie evidence of their paternal provenance or ancestry, is a serious matter in which, ordinarily, the minors and their father should be consulted. The mother's desire should not be the sole consideration." The change "might create the impression that the minors are illegitimate since they would carry the maternal surname only. That would be inconsistent with their legitimate status as indicated in their birth records." A minor who later fully appreciates the circumstances may himself apply.

Osmeña de Valencia v. Rodriguez (84 Phil. 222, 1949; Tolentino cites 47 O.G. 180)

The legitimate children of Pio E. Valencia sued to stop his illegitimate children from using the surname "Valencia". Held: they cannot. "We concede that the plaintiffs may use the surname of their father as a matter of right by reason of the mere fact that they are legitimate children; but we cannot agree to the view that article [264] of the Civil Code, without more, grants monopolistic proprietary control to legitimate children over the surname of their father. In other words, said article has marked a right of which legitimate children may not be deprived, but it cannot be interpreted as a prohibition against the use by others of what may happen to be the surname of their father. If plaintiff's theory were correct, they can stop countless inhabitants from bearing the surname 'Valencia.'"

In re Cancellation of the Certificates of Live Birth of Yohan Grant and Avee Kynna Noelle Barcelote Tinitigan (Barcelote v. Republic) (G.R. No. 222095, August 7, 2017)

Jonna Barcelote had two children with Ricky Tinitigan, a married man. Without her knowledge, he had their births late-registered with his surname. She sought to cancel the certificates. Held: cancelled. Under Art. 176 as amended by R.A. 9255, illegitimate children use the mother's surname, and they "may" use the father's only upon his express recognition; "may" is permissive. The late registration by the father alone was not valid: "It is mandatory that the mother of an illegitimate child signs the birth certificate of her child in all cases, irrespective of whether the father recognizes the child as his or not," because she has parental authority and custody. (Compare Grande v. Antonio, G.R. No. 206248, February 18, 2014: the father cannot compel the use of his surname; the choice belongs to the child, through the mother while the child is a minor.)

Tolentino (Arts. 174, 176): the right to the father's surname "creates no monopoly" (Osmeña de Valencia). Legitimate children may not adopt the surname of the mother's second husband; minors may not, on the mother's petition, drop the father's surname (Naldoza); but a child of age may choose the mother's surname. The rights in Art. 174 cannot be renounced: future support cannot be waived, and NCC 905 and 1347 bar renunciation of future legitime and contracts on future inheritance. On illegitimate children: the law recognizes the same rights as for legitimate children, differing only in degree, because "the transgressions of social conventions committed by the parents should not be visited upon the children" (Code Commission). If there are valid reasons not to leave an illegitimate child under the mother's authority, the court may appoint a guardian.
Tolentino's text of Art. 176 is outdated. His 1990 commentary quotes the original Art. 176, under which illegitimate children always use the mother's surname. R.A. No. 9255 (2004) added the proviso letting them use the father's surname upon his express recognition. Quote the amended text above.
Quiz traps (rights): "Illegitimate children shall use the father's surname if he recognized them" → false: they may (Barcelote; Grande). "Legitimate children have exclusive rights to their father's surname and may stop his illegitimate children from using it" → false (Osmeña de Valencia). "An illegitimate child's legitime is ½ of the legitime of the legitimate children" → false: ½ of the legitime of a (one) legitimate child. "Recognition is required before an illegitimate child may claim support" → false: a provisional declaration of status suffices (Mangulabnan).

Action to impugn legitimacy: grounds (Art. 166)

Art. 166, FC. Legitimacy of a child may be impugned only on the following grounds:
(1) That it was physically impossible for the husband to have sexual intercourse with his wife within the first 120 days of the 300 days which immediately preceded the birth of the child because of:
(a) the physical incapacity of the husband to have sexual intercourse with his wife;
(b) the fact that the husband and wife were living separately in such a way that sexual intercourse was not possible; or
(c) serious illness of the husband, which absolutely prevented sexual intercourse;
(2) That it is proved that for biological or other scientific reasons, the child could not have been that of the husband, except in the instance provided in the second paragraph of Article 164; or
(3) That in case of children conceived through artificial insemination, the written authorization or ratification of either parent was obtained through mistake, fraud, violence, intimidation, or undue influence. (255a)
Uribe (Quiz 5): "Why 120 days and not 150 or 100?" Sir's answer: "If impossible ang 120 days out of 300 preceding the birth, so 180 days nalang natitira, which is 6 months. If impossible yun at 6 months nalang natitira at buhay naman ang bata, di siya anak ng husband. Otherwise, kung 6 months lang siya napanganak, di siya mabubuhay kahit i-incubate siya. Kaya yung 180 sa 168, tungkol yan doon." In the same quiz, the item asking for the period of impossibility had answer E, "none of the above": the first 120 days of the 300 days immediately preceding the birth (not "120 days immediately preceding the birth").
Andal v. Macaraig (89 Phil. 165, 1951; printed in the syllabus as 69 Phil.)

Mariano Andal, represented by his mother Maria Dueñas, claimed land that had been donated to his deceased father, Emiliano. The defense was that Mariano was not Emiliano's son: Emiliano had serious tuberculosis around the time of conception, and Maria was then living with his brother. Held: Mariano is Emiliano's legitimate son. "Although the husband was already suffering from tuberculosis and his condition then was so serious that he could hardly move and get up from his bed, his feet were swollen and his voice hoarse, yet that is no evidence of impotency, nor does it prevent carnal intercourse. There are cases where persons suffering from this sickness can do the carnal act even in the most crucial stage because they are more inclined to sexual intercourse." The wife's adultery, if any, does not make the child illegitimate (Art. 167).

Macadangdang v. CA (100 SCRA 73, 1980)

Elizabeth Mejias, married to Crispin Anahaw, had an affair with Antonio Macadangdang, a married man, and bore a child. She sued Macadangdang for recognition and support as the father. Held: dismissed. "During the initial 120 days of the 300 which preceded the birth of the child, there was no concrete or substantial proof that was presented to establish physical impossibility of access between Elizabeth and Crispin. Elizabeth and Crispin continued to live in the same province, therefore there is still the possibility of access to one another." Moral impossibility (the spouses' estrangement) is not enough. "When circumstances are equal, the law is inclined to follow the paternity by the husband rather than the paternity by the paramour, thus giving the child the benefit of legitimacy." Only the husband, and his heirs in proper cases, may impugn.

Jao v. CA (152 SCRA 359, 1987)

The minor Janice Marie Jao, through her mother, sued Perico Jao for recognition and support. The parties agreed to blood grouping tests at the NBI, which excluded Perico as a possible father. Held: the action fails. "Blood grouping test can establish conclusively that the man is not the father of the child but not necessarily that a man is the father of a particular child. Blood tests are conclusive as to non-paternity and inconclusive to paternity." (DNA testing, under the Rule on DNA Evidence and cases like Herrera v. Alba, can now establish paternity positively.)

Amurao v. CA (168 SCRA 734, 1988)

In an action for support of a child, the trial court's order of September 26, 1977 recorded the petitioner's alleged admission of paternity and undertaking to give support, which he later disputed. Held: "Whether or not the petitioner made an admission of paternity under the terms of the trial court's order dated September 26, 1977, thereby binding himself to give support to his child, the private respondent herein, is a finding of fact which this Court may not review under Rule 45 of the Rules of Court." (The sources do not explain why the syllabus lists it under scientific grounds; the takeaway is that an admission of paternity on record binds the father.)

Tolentino (Art. 166): the presumption rests on the assumption of sexual union in marriage, especially during the period of conception. The husband must show, beyond reasonable doubt, that there was no access that could have enabled him to be the father; intercourse is presumed where personal access is not disproved. Only physical impossibility counts; moral impossibility (the wife's open adultery, bitter hatred) cannot defeat the presumption and can only corroborate (Macadangdang). The impossibility must be shown only for the part of the 120 days that falls during the marriage: for a child born 185 days after the wedding, the husband need prove impossibility during the first five days of the marriage only. Impotence (inability to copulate) is a ground; sterility (inability to procreate) is not, because short of absent testicles, absent spermatozoa or a blocked vas deferens there is no absolute sterility (normal counts are 100–150 million per cc; even a "sterile" 30 million may fertilize). Living separately must make access impossible: in different countries or distant provinces, never together during the period, or the husband in prison (unless he obtained access by corrupt violation of prison rules or conjugal visits). Legally separated spouses who reconcile, even briefly, fall back under the presumption. Serious illness must exclude intercourse, e.g., a sacroiliac injury in a plaster cast. On scientific grounds, Tolentino had criticized the NCC for ignoring science. A child with Caucasian features born to Malay parents with no Caucasian ancestry may defeat the presumption, but courts must take extreme care because of "alternating generations". A blood-group exclusion is treated in Europe as indisputable proof of non-paternity, and the Supreme Court has accepted it (Jao). For vitiated consent to artificial insemination, the vice must be such that the husband would not otherwise have consented.

The mother's declaration (Art. 167)

Art. 167, FC. The child shall be considered legitimate although the mother may have declared against its legitimacy or may have been sentenced as an adulteress. (256a)
Chua Keng Giap v. IAC (158 SCRA 18, 1988; printed in the syllabus as "PAC, 18 SCRA 18")

Chua Keng Giap claimed to be the son of Sy Kao, and through her an heir of her late husband. Sy Kao had consistently denied for many years that he was her son. Held: his claim of filiation fails. "Who better than Sy Kao herself would know if Chua Keng Giap was really her son? More than anyone else, it was Sy Kao who could say — as indeed she has said these many years — that Chua Keng Giap was not begotten of her womb."

Reconciling Chua Keng Giap with Art. 167. Art. 167 disregards a mother's declaration against the legitimacy of a child born in wedlock (i.e., that her husband is not the father). Chua Keng Giap is about maternity: the mother denied that the claimant was her child at all, and her word on that was given decisive weight. If the question says the mother declared that her husband is not the father, cite Art. 167; if it says she denies having given birth to the claimant, cite Chua Keng Giap (and Tolentino: "The mother is in the best position to know whether a person is really her son").
Tolentino (Art. 167): the article is a guaranty for children against the passions of their parents. An adulterous wife's forced confession may be true as to the offense but not as to paternity; a vengeful wife may lie; a woman with two partners in the same period cannot tell who the father is. While the husband had access, the wife's adultery does not overthrow the presumption, nor does the husband's denial of intercourse. But once adultery during the period of conception is proved, the presumption ceases to be conclusive, and other proof becomes admissible: absence, sickness, strained relations, blood tests, ethnic improbability ("Is not the illegitimacy of the child written on its own skin?" Demolombe; Lee Sing v. Collector of Customs, 59 Phil. 147). The article applies even to the child of a raped wife, who is legitimate if the husband was cohabiting with her. NCC 257 (a prima facie presumption of illegitimacy for ethnic reasons) and NCC 258 (a conclusive presumption of legitimacy for a child conceived before the marriage if the husband knew of the pregnancy, consented to his surname, or recognized the child) were both dropped by the Family Code; the husband's remedy is now Art. 166(2), with the burden on him.
Quiz traps (Art. 167): "A child is illegitimate if the mother declares under oath that her husband is not the father" → false. "A child is illegitimate if the mother was convicted of adultery for an affair during the period of conception" → false. Both are verbatim Art. 167 situations. "Only the mother can impugn" → false: she cannot impugn at all.

Children of a subsequent marriage (Arts. 168–169)

Art. 168, FC. If the marriage is terminated and the mother contracted another marriage within three hundred days after such termination of the former marriage, these rules shall govern in the absence of proof to the contrary:
(1) A child born before one hundred eighty days after the solemnization of the subsequent marriage is considered to have been conceived during the former marriage, provided it be born within three hundred days after the termination of the former marriage;
(2) A child born after one hundred eighty days following the celebration of the subsequent marriage is considered to have been conceived during such marriage, even though it be born within the three hundred days after the termination of the former marriage. (259a)
Art. 169, FC. The legitimacy or illegitimacy of a child born after three hundred days following the termination of the marriage shall be proved by whoever alleges such legitimacy or illegitimacy. (261a)
Uribe (Quiz 5): facts like "A died on January 1, 2024; B married C; the child was born on November 15, 2024." Students cited Art. 168's 180-day rule or Art. 169. Sir: Art. 169 does not apply (there is a subsequent marriage), and the 180-day computation is beside the point. What matters is that the birth is more than 300 days (over 10 months) after the termination: "A cannot be the father of the child. Alangan naman makabuntis pa ang patay." Art. 168(1) needs birth within 300 days after the termination, which is absent, so the child belongs to the second marriage, during which he was born. In another item (a child conceived during the first marriage and born during the second), Sir stressed that once the child was conceived during the first marriage, he does not become the second husband's child "inside the womb" just because he was born during the second marriage.
Tolentino (Arts. 168–169): Art. 168 (from NCC 259, and Argentine and Mexican sources) applies when a woman remarries within 300 days after her husband's death or, in an annulment, after the spouses' separation, and a child is born within those 300 days. In an annulment, count from the actual separation, not the final judgment, since the case may outlast the separation by years. The cut-off is the 180th day after the second wedding. Both rules are only presumptions, rebuttable under Art. 166, and only the husband presumed to be the father (or his heirs, in proper cases) may rebut them. "The child himself cannot choose his own filiation"; if the presumption is overthrown, the child cannot elect the paternity of the husband who defeated it. If the second marriage was bigamous, a child born more than 300 days after the separation and more than 180 days after the second wedding is legitimate in the second marriage (Arts. 168 and 54); one born within the 300 days is presumed the first husband's, unless he proves physical impossibility, in which case the child is the second husband's if born after the first 180 days. For a widow's illicit relations, a child born within 300 days of the husband's death and later acknowledged by the mother and her lover, who then marry, is still the first husband's legitimate child: it had that status at birth, and only children of parents who could marry at conception can be legitimated. Art. 169 (from NCC 261) replaced Spanish CC 111, under which the husband or his heirs could refuse to recognize such a child, creating a presumption of illegitimacy.
Tolentino vs. the text on Art. 169. Art. 169 creates no presumption: whoever alleges legitimacy or illegitimacy must prove it. Tolentino would treat the child, if nobody proves anything, as the mother's illegitimate child unless she or the child proves legitimacy ("there can be no person without status"; presuming illegitimacy "is realistic" and compels the mother or child to establish a better status). On a quiz, the answer is the text: neither presumption; burden on whoever alleges.

Periods and parties (Arts. 170–171)

Art. 170, FC. The action to impugn the legitimacy of the child shall be brought within one year from the knowledge of the birth or its recording in the civil register, if the husband or, in a proper case, any of his heirs, should reside in the city or municipality where the birth took place or was recorded.
If the husband or, in his default, all of his heirs do not reside at the place of birth as defined in the first paragraph or where it was recorded, the period shall be two years if they should reside in the Philippines; and three years if abroad. If the birth of the child has been concealed from or was unknown to the husband or his heirs, the period shall be counted from the discovery or knowledge of the birth of the child or of the fact of registration of said birth, whichever is earlier. (263a)
Art. 171, FC. The heirs of the husband may impugn the filiation of the child within the period prescribed in the preceding article only in the following cases:
(1) If the husband should die before the expiration of the period fixed for bringing his action;
(2) If he should die after the filing of the complaint without having desisted therefrom; or
(3) If the child was born after the death of the husband. (262a)
Liyao, Jr. v. Tanhoti-Liyao (G.R. No. 138961, March 7, 2002, 378 SCRA 563)

William Liyao, Jr., through his mother Corazon Garcia, sued for compulsory recognition as the illegitimate son of the late William Liyao, Sr. Corazon was still married to Ramon Yulo, though long separated from him in fact, when William Jr. was born; William Sr. had treated the boy as his son. The legal wife and legitimate children opposed. Held: the action fails. William Jr. is presumed the legitimate child of Ramon Yulo, and only the husband (or his heirs in the cases provided by law) may impugn that, in a direct action. "The child himself cannot choose his own filiation. If the husband, presumed to be the father does not impugn the legitimacy of the child, then the status of the child is fixed, and the latter cannot choose to be the child of his mother's alleged paramour." A child born within a valid marriage is presumed legitimate "even though the mother may have declared against its legitimacy or may have been sentenced as an adulteress."

Yap v. Yap (G.R. No. 222259, October 17, 2022)

A petition to establish filiation with an alleged biological father (who had died) was dismissed below because the petitioner was born during the mother's marriage to another man, and only that husband or his heirs may impugn legitimacy. Held: the dismissal was wrong; the case was remanded for trial, where filiation may be proved, including by DNA evidence. "To hastily dismiss a petition to establish filiation … merely because Articles 170 and 171 only allow the husband or his heirs to impugn the child's legitimate status unjustifiably limits the instances when a child's filiation with his/her biological father may be established. … it was never the intent of the legislators … to elevate the presumption of legitimacy to a position higher than a proven fact. Albeit every reasonable presumption must be made in favor of legitimacy, upholding such presumption despite the presence of a contrary scientifically-proven fact becomes unreasonable — if not totally absurd." Arts. 164, 166, 170 and 171 "contemplate a situation where a husband (or his heirs) denies as his own a child of his wife".

Uribe on Liyao and Yap (CivRev 2025; 2018 situationer): "Who can impugn: eto ang naging tanong last year." Before Yap, the Court consistently ruled that only the husband (the putative father) can impugn during his lifetime, and his heirs after his death because their shares are affected. The reason given is the husband's reputation. Sir disagrees: "I do not agree just because reputation … if totoo namang anak siya ni [another man] edi pwedeng i-prove na hindi talaga siya anak ng tatay niya … truth should prevail." "Nagbabago talaga ang mundo: in Yap v. Yap, even the child himself can impugn the paternity of the husband of his mother. Which I think is a good ruling." On Liyao in 2018: "Sir does not agree. I will always side with the truth and not the reputation."
Conflict: can the child impugn? The text (Arts. 170–171), Tolentino ("only the husband can contest"; "the child himself cannot choose his own filiation") and Liyao say no. A 2024 bar Q&A still answers no, citing Liyao, with a "see also" to Yap. Sir reads Yap as allowing the child to establish filiation with the biological father despite the presumption. For Sir: state the traditional rule (Arts. 170–171, Liyao), then say that Yap (2022) relaxed it where filiation with the biological father is scientifically proven, and that Sir agrees with Yap.
Lim v. IAC (166 SCRA 451, 1988)

The nephews and nieces of the childless Esperanza Cabatbat claimed her estate. Violeta Cabatbat Lim claimed to be Esperanza's child and invoked NCC 263 (now Art. 170): the period to impugn legitimacy had long expired. Held: the period does not apply. "This legal provision refers to an action to impugn legitimacy. It is inapplicable to this case because this is not an action to impugn the legitimacy of a child, but an action of the private respondents to claim their inheritance as legal heirs of their childless deceased aunt. They do not claim that petitioner Violeta Cabatbat Lim is an illegitimate child of the deceased, but that she is not the decedent's child at all." Violeta was not an adopted, acknowledged natural, or legal-fiction child, so she was not an heir. (Same rule for a simulated birth: Benitez-Badua v. CA, 229 SCRA 468, 1994.)

Tolentino (Arts. 170–171): the presumption of legitimacy is not merely evidentiary; it fixes the child's civil status, which cannot be attacked collaterally, e.g., as a defense to a support suit, but only in a direct action by the proper parties within the period (cf. Mexican Civil Code 335). When the period lapses, the status becomes fixed and unassailable, whether the presumption was prima facie or conclusive; the aim is to end uncertainty and force early action while the evidence of the conception period is fresh. Only the husband may impugn: he faces the scandal and decides whether to conceal or expose the wife's infidelity. His heirs may do so only in the Art. 171 cases, acting in substitution; they cannot sue if he did not use the right or desisted, lest they subordinate his sentiments to their patrimonial interest. "Heirs" includes all kinds, testamentary or legal, compulsory or voluntary. The period runs from knowledge of the birth or its registration, whichever is earlier. Where the husband dies before his period lapses, Tolentino applies the heir's own period (he sues in his own right). If the husband dies while his action is pending, the action, being personal, may be dismissed, and the heirs sue anew in their own right. If the impugning succeeds, the husband's paternity is rejected and the child is the mother's illegitimate (adulterous) child; the real father need not be identified. A child born within 180 days after the wedding becomes the mother's illegitimate child, conceived while she was single.
Tolentino vs. the text on Art. 171(2). The article lets the heirs impugn "if he should die after the filing of the complaint without having desisted therefrom", which most readers take as the heirs continuing the husband's action. Tolentino treats the action as personal, so it may be dismissed on his death, and the heirs must file their own action within their own period. On a codal quiz, answer with the text: the heirs may impugn in that case.
Uribe (Quiz 5) on the effect of a successful action: students said the child becomes "an illegitimate child of the spouses". Sir: non sequitur. Impugning establishes that the child is not the husband's child; it does not make him an illegitimate child of the husband or "of the spouses". "Di siya anak ng husband." (Tolentino and the codal structure: the child is then the mother's illegitimate child.)
Quiz traps (Arts. 166–171): "The period is one year if the husband lives anywhere in the Philippines" → false: one year only if he (or any heir) lives in the city or municipality of birth or recording; two years elsewhere in the Philippines. "The period always runs from the birth" → false: from knowledge of the birth or its recording; if concealed, from discovery, whichever is earlier. "The husband's legitimate children may impugn the legitimacy of their half-brother while their father is alive" → false. "Sterility of the husband is a ground" → false: impotence (physical incapacity), not sterility. "Serious illness of the husband is a ground" → incomplete: it must have absolutely prevented intercourse (Andal). "Estrangement (they slept in separate rooms of the same house) is enough" → false: physical, not moral, impossibility (Macadangdang). "Legitimacy may be raised as a defense in a support case" → false: only in a direct action.

Practice: H and W live in Quezon City. W gave birth in Cebu City on March 1, 2025; the birth was recorded there on March 10, 2025. H learned of the birth on February 1, 2026. Until when may H impugn the child's legitimacy?

H does not reside in the city where the birth took place or was recorded, but he lives in the Philippines, so the period is two years (Art. 170, 2nd par.). It runs from his knowledge of the birth or from the recording, whichever is earlier; the recording (March 10, 2025) is earlier, so the period ends on March 10, 2027. (If the birth was concealed from him, the period runs from his discovery of the birth or of its registration, whichever is earlier.)

Practice: W, whose husband H had been working in Saudi Arabia for two straight years without returning, gave birth to a child. H did nothing and died three months after learning of the birth. May H's brother, his sole heir, impugn the child's legitimacy? On what ground?

Yes, if H died before his own period expired (Art. 171(1)); H lived abroad, so his period was three years. The ground is Art. 166(1)(b): the spouses lived apart in such a way that intercourse was physically impossible during the first 120 of the 300 days before the birth. The brother sues within the Art. 170 period; Tolentino would apply the period based on the brother's own residence, since he sues in his own right.
