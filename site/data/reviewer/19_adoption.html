Adoption

Syllabus XIV. The syllabus still cites the Family Code (Arts. 183–193) and the Domestic Adoption Act (R.A. 8552, Secs. 7–22); both are now repealed by the Domestic Administrative Adoption and Alternative Child Care Act (R.A. 11642, approved January 6, 2022), and simulated births are rectified under R.A. 11222 (2019). Sir's rule: the law in force when the petition was filed governs, so know all three. Cases: Lazatin, Cervantes, Republic v. CA (1996), Republic v. CA & Bobiles, Lim, Santos v. Aranzanso, Daoang, Nieto, Duncan, Cang, Bobanovic, Pardo de Tavera, Edwin Villa; from the class reviewer and the group report, Landingin, Toledano, Bartolome, Stephanie Garcia, Lahom, Tamargo, Sayson, Bagcat-Gullas.

Which law governs

Uribe (CivRev, adoption discussion): asked whether students still need to study the Domestic Adoption Act since R.A. 11642 repealed it, Sir said yes: "If the petition was filed during the effectivity of those laws, those laws will apply whether or not that petition should be granted. Fair lang naman kasi kung qualified ka at that time of the filing, pag nabago na ang batas edi di na pwede di na igrant ang petition? Parang di naman tama yun." Same idea as the property regime of a marriage solemnized in 1985. "It is possible in a case, the adoption took place in 1982 kaso ngayon lang nagkaproblem kasi sa mamanahin. The law governing is the law at the time the adoption is filed, so hindi totoo na aaralin lang ang batas ngayon." Also: under R.A. 8552, "18 pwede na but under 11642 hindi na pwede kasi 25 na."
Old answer keys vs. R.A. 11642: a past Uribe quiz keyed "Domestic adoption is essentially judicial" as true; under R.A. 11642 it is now administrative (an Order of Adoption from the NACC, appealable to the Court of Appeals). Inter-country adoption remains under R.A. 8043, with the NACC replacing the ICAB.
Republic v. CA and Bobiles (G.R. No. 92326, January 24, 1992)

Zenaida Bobiles filed alone, under P.D. 603 (which allowed it), a petition to adopt Jason Condat. The Family Code took effect while the case was pending and required spouses to adopt jointly. Held: the petition was properly granted. The law in force at the filing governs, and the petitioner acquired a vested right that the FC could not impair. In any case, "the affidavit of consent attached by the husband showed that he actually joined his wife in adopting Jayson. His declarations and subsequent confirmatory testimony in open court was sufficient to make him a co-petitioner. Future of an innocent child must not be compromised by arbitrary insistence of rigid adherence to procedural rules on the form of the pleadings." (Applied in Republic v. Miller, G.R. No. 125932, April 21, 1999: a German couple who filed under P.D. 603 kept their right to adopt after the FC disqualified aliens; 2001 Bar.)

Lazatin v. Campos (92 SCRA 250, 1979)

Renato Lazatin sought to intervene in the settlement of the estate of Margarita de Asis, claiming to be her adopted son, and offered evidence of acts and declarations of the deceased treating him as such, but no decree. Held: no adoption shown. "Only an adoption made through the court, or in pursuance with the procedure laid down under Rule 99 of the Rules of Court is valid in this jurisdiction. Withal, the attempts of petitioner to prove his adoption by acts and declarations of the deceased do not discharge the mandatory presentation of the judicial decree of adoption." (Today, the Order of Adoption issued by the NACC under R.A. 11642.)

Who may adopt (R.A. 11642, Sec. 21)

Sec. 21, R.A. 11642. Who May Adopt. – The following may adopt:
(a) Any Filipino citizen at least twenty-five (25) years of age, who is in possession of full civil capacity and legal rights; has not been convicted of any crime involving moral turpitude; is of good moral character and can model the same; is emotionally and psychologically capable of caring for children; at least sixteen (16) years older than the adoptee; and who is in a position to support and care for adopted children in keeping with the means of the family: Provided, That the requirement of sixteen (16)-years difference between the age of the adopter and the adoptee may be waived when the adopter is the biological parent of the adoptee, or is the spouse of the adoptee's parent;
(b) The legal guardian with respect to the ward after the termination of the guardianship and clearance of financial accountabilities;
(c) The legal guardians with respect to the foster child;
(d) Philippine government officials and employees deployed or stationed abroad: Provided, That they are able to bring the child with them; and
(e) Foreign nationals who are permanent or habitual residents of the Philippines for at least five (5) years possessing the same qualifications as above stated for Filipino nationals prior to filing of the petition: Provided, That they come from a country with diplomatic relations with the Republic of the Philippines and that the laws of the adopter's country will acknowledge the Certificate of Adoption as valid, acknowledge the child as a legal child of the adopters, and allow entry of the child into such country as an adoptee: Provided, further, That requirements of residency may be waived for the following: (1) A former Filipino citizen, habitually residing in the Philippines, who seeks to adopt a relative within fourth (4th) civil degree of consanguinity or affinity; or (2) One who seeks to adopt the legitimate child of the Filipino spouse; or (3) One who is married to a Filipino citizen and seeks to adopt jointly with the spouse a relative within the fourth (4th) degree of consanguinity or affinity of the Filipino spouse.
Spouses shall jointly adopt, except in the following cases: (a) If one spouse seeks to adopt the legitimate child of the other; or (b) If one spouse seeks to adopt own illegitimate child: Provided, That the other spouse has signified consent thereto; or (c) If the spouses are legally separated from each other.
Uribe (Quiz, with class discussion): "The adopter must be at least sixteen years older than the adoptee." False: the gap may be waived when the adopter is the biological parent or the spouse of the adoptee's parent. Another item: a statement that the adopter need only be "of legal age" is false under R.A. 11642: "kung sinabi mong at least of legal age, pwede na 19 or 18. But when the law requires 25, kung 24 palang siya, di pa siya pwedeng mag-adopt." On the MC "Following are necessary for a Filipino to be able to adopt, except: (a) of legal age …", Sir's basis was (a), because R.A. 11642 requires 25. (In the same item, "not been convicted of a crime with penalty of more than 6 years" is not the statutory test either; the law says a crime involving moral turpitude.) On aliens: "before, the requirement is 3 years residency … pareho pa rin ang instances where that requirement will be waived" (former Filipino adopting a relative within the 4th degree, etc.).
Uribe (CivRev Quiz 2, item 7): "Husband and wife must jointly adopt if one seeks to adopt his own illegitimate child." False: "The law provides that consent is only needed and not adopt jointly. Consent ≠ Joint Adoption." In class Sir pressed: "When will the spouses have joint authority when only one of them is the petitioner?" Answer: when one spouse adopts the legitimate child of the other. "Aside from that scenario, any other scenario where a petition for adoption will prosper even if the spouses did not jointly file? … If the spouses are legally separated. … Alamin niyo ang exemptions." Samplex MC: "In case of adoption, husband and wife shall exercise joint parental authority in the following cases, except: (a) if husband and wife jointly adopt; (b) one spouse adopts the [legitimate] child of the other; (c) one spouse seeks to adopt his own illegitimate child …" → (c): "the adopting spouse needs the consent of the other, but this doesn't mean that they must jointly adopt. Consent does not lead to parental authority."
In re Adoption of Michelle P. Lim and Michael Jude P. Lim (G.R. Nos. 168992–93, May 21, 2009)

Monina and Primo Lim, childless, were entrusted with two infants of unknown parents and registered them as their own. After Primo died, Monina married Angel Olario, an American, and, availing herself of the amnesty in R.A. 8552, Sec. 22, filed petitions alone to adopt Michelle (then 25) and Michael (18), with Olario's affidavit of consent. Held: denied. "Husband and wife shall jointly adopt except in 3 instances", none present. "The use of the word 'shall' signifies that joint adoption of husband and wife is mandatory. This is in consonance with the concept of joint parental authority since the child to be adopted is elevated to the level of a legitimate child." Olario's consent does not suffice: as an alien he must himself meet Sec. 7(b), and the residency and certification requirements cannot be waived. That the adoptees were already emancipated does not matter: "Parental authority is merely just one of the effects of legal adoption."

Republic v. Toledano (G.R. No. 94147, June 8, 1994; class reviewer)

Alvin Clouse, an American, and his wife Evelyn, a former Filipino who had become an American citizen, jointly petitioned to adopt Evelyn's minor brother. Held: denied. Alvin was not qualified (not a former Filipino, not adopting his Filipino spouse's legitimate child); Evelyn alone was, but she could not adopt alone because spouses must adopt jointly. "As the child to be adopted is elevated to the level of a legitimate child, it is but natural to require the spouses to adopt jointly." (Same answer in the 2000, 2003 and 2005 Bar questions on Sarah and Tom, Lina, and Eva and Paul.)

Nieto v. Magat (136 SCRA 533, 1985)

Filipino spouses temporarily residing in Guam (the husband was employed there) petitioned to adopt a minor; the trial court dismissed it on the ground that they were non-residents. Held: they may adopt. "The fact that the petitioners reside temporarily in Guam does not disqualify them from adopting the minor child." Arts. 27 and 28 of P.D. 603 did not disqualify Filipinos temporarily abroad.

Daoang v. Municipal Judge of San Nicolas (159 SCRA 369, 1988)

The spouses Agonoy petitioned to adopt two minors; relatives opposed because the spouses had a legitimate daughter (since deceased) and grandchildren, invoking the old NCC 335 disqualification of persons with legitimate descendants. Held: the adoption may proceed. "Under the law now in force, having legitimate, legitimated, acknowledged natural children, or children by legal fiction, is no longer a ground for disqualification to adopt. Adoption used to be for the benefit of the adoptor … The present tendency, however, is geared more towards the promotion of the welfare of the child and the enhancement of his opportunities for a useful and happy life."

Bobanovic v. Montes (142 SCRA 485, 1986)

Foreign spouses who had been granted the adoption of the infant Adam Christopher could not take him abroad because the MSSD refused a travel clearance, insisting that only the MSSD could make the case study (the court had relied on its own social worker's report). Held: the MSSD must issue the clearance. It did not appeal the decree and never claimed the social worker's report was wrong; "absent any proof that prejudice would be caused to the Adam Christopher if he joins his adoptive parents, the MSSD should be faulted for declining to issue the travel clearance."

Pardo de Tavera v. Cacdac (167 SCRA 636, 1988)

An American couple, the Gordons, adopted a Filipino child on the strength of a case study by the court's social worker; the MSSD objected that the case study was its exclusive prerogative. Held: the adoption stands. Before E.O. 91, the MSSD had no exclusive authority to make the case study; the court found its social worker's report based on "very honest insight and opinion based on personal interviews and home study painstakingly made", and the MSSD never alleged it was faulty. "The objective of trial custody had been substantially achieved."

Tolentino (FC Arts. 183–186): adoption statutes are liberally construed to carry out their beneficent purpose; the proceedings are in rem. Under the FC, aliens were generally disqualified, except a former Filipino adopting a relative by consanguinity, one adopting the legitimate child of his Filipino spouse, and one married to a Filipino adopting jointly a relative of the Filipino spouse. Tolentino read the old Code as allowing one spouse to adopt alone with the other's consent (to prevent dissension), and the consent as unnecessary after legal separation; the FC and later laws made joint adoption mandatory, keeping legal separation as an exception.

Who may be adopted (R.A. 11642, Sec. 22)

Sec. 22, R.A. 11642. Who May Be Adopted. – The following may be adopted:
(a) Any child who has been issued a CDCLAA;
(b) The legitimate child of one spouse by the other spouse;
(c) An illegitimate child by a qualified adopter to improve status of legitimacy;
(d) A Filipino of legal age if, prior to the adoption, said person has been consistently considered and treated by the adopters as their own child for a period of at least three (3) years;
(e) A foster child;
(f) A child whose adoption has been previously rescinded;
(g) A child whose biological or adoptive parents have died: Provided, That no proceedings shall be initiated within six (6) months from the time of death of said parents; or
(h) A relative of the adopter.
In re Adoption of Edwin Villa (21 SCRA 399, 1967)

The spouses Luis Santos, Jr. and Edipola Villa Santos petitioned to adopt Edwin Villa, Edipola's younger brother, whom they had cared for since infancy. The Solicitor General opposed, arguing that adoption between siblings would create confusing relationships. Held: granted. "The fact alone that petitioner wife and minor are siblings should not prevent the adoption. There is no express provision in law that disallows relatives by blood or affinity from adopting one another."

Samplex (Uribe quizzes; Civ Rev Samplex #46): "Only minors may be adopted." False: "Illegitimate child may be adopted by parent regardless of age" (FC 183, 2nd par., and 187(1); now Sec. 22(c), (d)).

Whose consent is needed (Sec. 23)

Sec. 23, R.A. 11642. Whose Consent is Necessary to the Adoption. – After being properly counseled and informed of the right to give or withhold approval of the adoption, the written consent of the following to the adoption are hereby required:
(a) The adoptee, if ten (10) years of age or over;
(b) The biological parents of the child, if known, or the legal guardian, or the proper government instrumentality which has legal custody of the child, except in the case of a Filipino of legal age if, prior to the adoption, said person has been consistently considered and treated as their own child by the adopters for at least three (3) years;
(c) The legitimate and adopted children, ten (10) years of age or over, of the adopters, if any;
(d) The illegitimate children, ten (10) years of age or over, of the adopter if living with said adopter or over whom the adopter exercises parental authority and the latter's spouse, if any; and
(e) The spouse, if any, of the person adopting or to be adopted.
Provided, That children under ten (10) years of age shall be counseled and consulted, but shall not be required to execute within consent.
Uribe (Quiz, with class discussion): "The consent of the following is required to the adoption, except: … (d) parents of the adopter" → (d), not provided in the law. Sir then asked why: "Pag walang anak at namatay, at least intestate succession, sino ang magmamana? … parents. Bakit di kailangan ang consent ng parents? … As a matter of policy, baka kailangan ng consent ng parents. But the point is more important in Domestic Administrative Adoption: sa letter of the law, magmamana na ang adoptee sa parents of adopter" (Sec. 41 extends legitimate filiation to them), "pero walang kailangan ng consent sa parents tapos magmamana na sa kanila?" A variant: "The consent of the following is not required except: (a) adoptee if 7 years or over; (b) siblings of adoptee, if known; (c) spouse of adoptee …; (d) parents of adopter" → (c): the adoptee's spouse must consent (an adult may be adopted, so he may be married); 10, not 7, is the age.
Cang v. CA (296 SCRA 128, September 25, 1998)

Herbert Cang and Anna Marie Clavano separated; he went to the U.S., divorced her there, and became an American. Anna Marie's brother Ronald and his wife petitioned to adopt the couple's three children, with Anna Marie's consent, alleging that Herbert had abandoned them. The lower courts granted it. Held: reversed. "The written consent of the natural parent is indispensable for the validity of the decree of adoption. Nevertheless, the requirement of written consent can be dispensed with if the parent has abandoned the child or that such parent is insane or hopelessly intemperate. The court may acquire jurisdiction over the case even without the written consent of the parents or one of the parents provided that the petition for adoption alleges facts sufficient to warrant exemption from compliance therewith." But there was no abandonment: Herbert had sent money, gifts and letters and kept in touch. Abandonment means "neglect or refusal to perform the natural and legal obligations of care and support which parents owe their children"; mere physical estrangement is not enough.

Duncan v. CFI of Rizal (69 SCRA 298, 1976)

An unwed mother gave her three-day-old child, through a lawyer (Atty. Corazon Velasco), to the Duncan spouses, asking that her identity be kept secret; she never saw the child again. The Duncans petitioned to adopt with the lawyer's consent as de facto guardian. Held: granted. "The parental consent required by law in adoption proceedings refers to parents who have not abandoned their child. Thus, where the natural and unwed mother turned over her three-day old child to another person and from that date on to the time of the adoption proceedings in court, she is considered to have abandoned her child and her consent to the adoption is no longer necessary."

Santos v. Aranzanso (16 SCRA 344, 1966)

The adoption of Paulina and Aurora Santos by Simplicio Santos and Juliana Reyes was attacked collaterally in the settlement of Juliana's estate, by her relatives, for lack of the natural parents' consent. Held: the decree stands. "Consent by the parents to the adoption is not an absolute requisite. If the natural parents have abandoned their children, consent by the guardian ad litem suffices." The decree cannot be attacked collaterally.

Landingin v. Republic (G.R. No. 164948, June 27, 2006; class reviewer)

Diwata Landingin, a U.S. citizen of Filipino parentage, petitioned to adopt her three minor nephews and niece, children of her deceased brother; their mother had gone to Italy and remarried. Only a social worker's report said the mother consented. Held: denied. "Written consent of the biological parent/s is necessary—indispensable—for the adoption to proceed." Abandonment was not proved: the mother kept in touch and sent money. Also, "it is the financial capacity of the adopter that should be considered, not the biological parent", and Landingin, relying on her children's help, had not shown she could support the adoptees.

Bagcat-Gullas v. Gullas (class reviewer)

The spouses Gullas adopted a minor, Jo Anne, whose mother had abandoned her. Jose Gullas's adult legitimate children later attacked the decree: they had not been impleaded or served summons, and the "affidavit of consent" in the record was not genuine. Held: the decree is void. "The written consent of the adopter's legitimate children aged ten or older is a mandatory jurisdictional requirement in adoption proceedings, and failure to implead and serve summons upon them renders the judgment void." Lack of jurisdiction defeats immutability of judgments.

Tolentino (FC Art. 188): parental consent is essential to confer jurisdiction; the parent's testimony in court, beyond the written consent, is not required. Where a parent survives, grandparents with actual custody cannot consent. Consent cannot be required from an insane parent, one under civil interdiction or declared absent, or one judicially deprived of parental authority. Until the court approves the adoption, the parents still have parental authority and may withdraw consent with court approval.

Effects of adoption (Secs. 41–43)

Sec. 41, R.A. 11642. Legitimacy. – The adoptee shall be considered the legitimate child of the adopter for all intents and purposes and as such is entitled to all the rights and obligations provided by law to legitimate children born to them without discrimination of any kind. To this end, the adoptee is entitled to love, guidance, and support in keeping with the means of the family. The legitimate filiation that is created between the adopter and adoptee shall be extended to the adopter's parents, adopter's legitimate siblings, and legitimate descendants.
The adopter is also given the right to choose the name by which the child is to be known, consistent with the best interest of the child.
Sec. 42, R.A. 11642. Parental Authority. – Upon issuances of the Order of Adoption, adoption shall cease as alternative care and becomes parental care. Adoptive parents shall now have full parental authority over the child. Except in cases where the biological parent is the spouse of the adopter, all legal ties between the biological parents and the adoptee shall be severed and the same shall then be vested on the adopters.
In case spouses jointly adopt or one spouse adopts the legitimate child of the other, joint parental authority shall be exercised by the spouses.
Sec. 43, R.A. 11642. Succession. – In testate and intestate succession, the adopters and the adoptee shall have reciprocal rights of succession without distinction from legitimate filiations. However, if the adoptees and their biological parents have left a will, the law on testamentary succession shall govern.
Art. 190, FC (repealed; still asked). Legal or intestate succession to the estate of the adopted shall be governed by the following rules: (1) Legitimate and illegitimate children and descendants and the surviving spouse of the adopted shall inherit from the adopted, in accordance with the ordinary rules of legal or intestate succession; (2) When the parents, legitimate or illegitimate, or the legitimate ascendants of the adopted concur with the adopter, they shall divide the entire estate, one-half to be inherited by the parents or ascendants and the other half, by the adopters; (3) When the surviving spouse or the illegitimate children of the adopted concur with the adopters, they shall divide the entire estate in equal shares, one-half to be inherited by the spouse or the illegitimate children of the adopted and the other half, by the adopters; (4) When the adopters concur with the illegitimate children and the surviving spouse of the adopted, they shall divide the entire estate in equal shares, one-third to be inherited by the illegitimate children, one-third by the surviving spouse, and one-third by the adopters; (5) When only the adopters survive, they shall inherit the entire estate; and (6) When only collateral blood relatives of the adopted survive, then the ordinary rules of legal or intestate succession shall apply.
Samplex (Civ Rev Samplex #47; Uribe Quiz #4, T/F 7): the verbatim Art. 190(1) and 190(3) statements were keyed true ("This is a verbatim provision"). Under R.A. 8552 and 11642, Art. 190 no longer exists; Sir still tests the text.
Cervantes v. Fajardo (In re Habeas Corpus of Angelie Anne Cervantes) (169 SCRA 575, 1989)

Conrado Fajardo and Gina Carreon, common-law partners, gave their daughter Angelie Anne to the Cervantes spouses, who adopted her with the natural parents' consent. Later the natural mother took the child back and demanded money. Held: custody to the adopters. "A decree of adoption has the effect, among others, of dissolving the authority vested in natural parents over the adopted child, except where the adopting parent is the spouse of the natural parent of the adopted, in which case, parental authority over the adopted shall be exercised jointly by both spouses. The adopting parents have the right to the care and custody of the adopted child and exercise parental authority and responsibility over him." Moreover, "in all controversies regarding the custody of minors, the foremost consideration is the moral, physical and social welfare of the child", and the natural mother's common-law situation weighed against her.

Bartolome v. SSS (G.R. No. 192531, November 12, 2014; class reviewer)

John Colcol, adopted as a child by his great-grandfather Cornelio (who died in 1987, when John was four), died in a work accident, childless and single. His biological mother, Bernardina, with whom he had lived since Cornelio's death and whom he had named as his beneficiary, claimed death benefits as a "dependent parent". The ECC denied it, holding that the adoption severed her status. Held: she is entitled. "Parents" in the Labor Code is not limited to legitimate parents. Applying by analogy the rule that parental authority of the biological parents is restored when an adoption is rescinded during minority, the Court held that "the biological parents retain their rights of succession to the estate of their child who was the subject of adoption … [which] include the exercise of parental authority, in the event of the untimely passing of their minor offspring's adoptive parent."

In re Adoption of Stephanie Nathy Astorga Garcia (G.R. No. 148311, March 31, 2005)

Honorato Catindig adopted his illegitimate daughter, Stephanie, and asked that her middle name be changed from "Astorga" to "Garcia", her mother's surname, and her surname to "Catindig". Held: allowed. "There is no law prohibiting an illegitimate child adopted by her natural father … to use, as middle name her mother's surname"; what is not prohibited is allowed. Being now a legitimate child, she has the right to bear the surnames of both her father and her mother; it preserves her maternal lineage and her right to inherit from her mother, and follows Filipino custom. (1996, 2006, 2012 Bar.)

Republic v. CA and Caranto (255 SCRA 99, March 15, 1996)

The Carantos petitioned to adopt a minor and, in the same petition, to correct his first name in his birth record from "Midael" to "Michael". Notice was published only of the adoption. Held: the adoption is valid, but the correction is void. The published notice "only the prayer for adoption of the minor was stated. Nothing was mentioned that in addition the correction of his name in the civil registry was also being sought. The local civil registrar was thus deprived of notice and, consequently, of the opportunity to be heard." Failure to implead the civil registrar as an indispensable party and to publish the petition for correction made that part "null and void for lack of jurisdiction both as to party and as to the subject matter." (See Civil Register: Rule 108; today R.A. 9048 for a clerical change of first name.)

2007 Bar: Ramon married Dessa in 1970. Before the marriage, Ramon had an illegitimate child, Anna. Ramon and Dessa adopted Cherry (1971) and Michelle (1972). Dessa died in 1973 giving birth to Larry. Anna had an illegitimate child, Lia. Cherry adopted Shelly. Larry had twins, Hans and Gretel, with his girlfriend Fiona. In 2005 Anna, Larry and Cherry died in an accident; in 2007 Ramon died. Who may inherit from Ramon?

Michelle (adopted, inherits as a legitimate child) and Lia (by representation of Anna; Art. 992 does not bar her because Anna herself was illegitimate). Not Shelly: under the law then in force, adoption created a personal relation only between Cherry and Shelly, so Shelly cannot represent Cherry in Ramon's estate. Not Hans and Gretel: illegitimate children of a legitimate child are barred by Art. 992. Under R.A. 11642, the legitimate filiation of an adoptee extends to the adopter's parents, so a Shelly adopted under it would arguably be Ramon's legitimate granddaughter and could represent Cherry (a point to raise, not settled).

2009 Bar: Rafael, a bachelor, petitioned to adopt Dolly, a one-year-old foundling. He died while the case was pending. (a) Should it be dismissed? (b) What if Dolly died instead?

(a) Under R.A. 8552, Sec. 13, if the petitioner dies after the petition has been submitted for decision, the court may still issue the decree, which retroacts to the date of filing; otherwise the case is terminated. (b) The adoptee's death terminates the proceedings.

2010 Bar: Rex and Lea have two children, 14 and 8. Rex fathered a child, now 10, with another woman; Lea obtained a decree of legal separation. Rex wants to adopt his illegitimate child. Whose consent is needed? What if there had been no legal separation?

The 14-year-old legitimate child, the 10-year-old adoptee, and the adoptee's biological mother. The 8-year-old is only counseled and consulted. Lea's consent is not needed after legal separation. Without legal separation, Rex may still adopt alone, his own illegitimate child being an exception to joint adoption, but with Lea's consent as well.

Rescission (Secs. 47–53; FC 191–193; R.A. 8552, Secs. 19–20)

Sec. 47, R.A. 11642. Grounds for Rescission of Administrative Adoption. – The adoption may be rescinded only upon the petition of the adoptee with the NACC, or with the assistance of the SWDO if the adoptee is a minor, or if the adoptee is eighteen (18) years of age or over but who is incapacitated or by his or her guardian on any of the following grounds committed by the adopter(s): (a) Repeated physical or verbal maltreatment by the adopter despite having undergone counseling; (b) Attempt on the life of the adoptee; (c) Abandonment and failure to comply with parental obligations.
Adoption, being in the best interest of the child, shall not be subject to rescission by the adopter. However, the adopter may disinherit the adoptee for causes provided in Article 919 of the Civil Code of the Philippines.
Lahom v. Sibulo (G.R. No. 143989, July 14, 2003)

Dr. Diosdado Lahom and his wife Isabelita adopted Isabelita's nephew, Jose Melvin Sibulo, in 1972. In 1999, after R.A. 8552 took effect, Isabelita petitioned to rescind the adoption: Jose Melvin refused to use the surname Lahom, Diosdado had wanted to revoke the adoption before he died, and Jose Melvin neglected her. Held: dismissed. R.A. 8552 deleted the adopter's right to rescind. Even assuming a right to rescind had vested under the law in force when she adopted, it was "an action that should be exercised within the period allowed by" the Rules (five years from the minor's majority), which had long lapsed. "While R.A. No. 8552 has unqualifiedly withdrawn from an adopter a consequential right to rescind the adoption decree even in cases where the adoption might clearly turn out to be undesirable, it remains, nevertheless, the bounden duty of the Court to apply the law…. [T]he adopter may, for valid reasons, cause the forfeiture of certain benefits otherwise accruing to an undeserving child", e.g., by disinheritance or by excluding him from the free portion.

Tolentino (FC 191–193): a decree of adoption cannot be attacked collaterally in a settlement proceeding; a separate action is required. Grounds for the adopted differed by age: a minor or incapacitated adoptee could rescind on the grounds for loss or suspension of parental authority (Arts. 229–232); an adult, on the grounds for disinheriting an ascendant (NCC 920). On rescission while the adopted is a minor, the court reinstates the parental authority of the natural parents unless they are disqualified, or appoints a guardian; the adopted loses the adopter's surname.

Bar (1994–1995, adapted): Carol consented to the adoption of her illegitimate daughter Bing by Norma and Manuel in 1990. In 1991 she learned they ran a call-girl ring from their house; Bing wanted to return to her. Who has the better right to custody, and what legal actions may be taken?

Carol, because the child's welfare is paramount and Bing's stay in a house used for prostitution is harmful (though if Bing is already of age, custody is moot). The remedies: an action to deprive the adopters of parental authority (Art. 231(2): corrupting example), or rescission of the adoption, which under the law then in force was available to the adoptee on the same grounds (FC 191). Under R.A. 11642 the adoptee would petition the NACC on the grounds of Sec. 47, and corrupting the child would be argued as maltreatment or failure to comply with parental obligations.

Rectification of simulated birth (R.A. 11222; R.A. 8552, Sec. 22)

Sec. 4, R.A. 11222. Rectification of Simulated Birth Record. – Notwithstanding any provision of law to the contrary, a person or persons who, prior to the effectivity of this Act, simulated the birth of a child, and those who cooperated in the execution of such simulation, shall not be criminally, civilly, or administratively liable for such act: Provided, That the simulation of birth was made for the best interest of the child and that the child has been consistently considered and treated by such person or persons as her, his, or their own daughter or son: Provided, further, That such person or persons has or have filed a petition for adoption with an application for the rectification of the simulated birth record within ten (10) years from the effectivity of this Act: Provided, finally, That all the benefits of this Act shall also apply to adult adoptees.
Sec. 5, R.A. 11222. Administrative Adoption and Rectification. – A person or persons who simulated the birth of a child under the conditions provided under Section 4 of this Act may avail of administrative proceedings for the adoption and rectification of the simulated birth record of such child: Provided, That the child has been living with the person for at least three (3) years before the effectivity of this Act: Provided, further, That a certificate declaring the child legally available for adoption (CDCLAA) is issued by the DSWD in favor of such child.
Simulation of birth is "the tampering of the civil registry to make it appear in the record of birth that a child was born to a person who is not such child's biological mother, causing the loss of the true identity and status of such child" (R.A. 11222, Sec. 3). Before these laws, a simulated birth certificate could be cancelled at the instance of an interested relative (Babiera v. Catotal, G.R. No. 138493, June 15, 2000: a sister may sue to cancel a birth certificate falsely showing their mother, aged 54 at the alleged birth, as the mother of another), and simulated "parents" were exposed to prosecution under RPC 347. R.A. 8552, Sec. 22 gave a five-year amnesty (used in Lim); R.A. 11222 replaced it.
Quiz traps (adoption): "A Filipino of legal age may adopt" → false under R.A. 11642 (25). "The adopter must in all cases be at least 16 years older" → false (waivable). "Only minors may be adopted" → false. "Spouses must adopt jointly when one adopts his own illegitimate child" → false (consent only). "The adopter may rescind the adoption for causes for disinheritance" → false since R.A. 8552 (disinherit only). "The adopter's parents must consent" → false. "An adoption decree retroacts to make the adopters liable for the child's torts before they had custody" → false (Tamargo). "A foster child or ward may not be adopted by his guardian" → false (Sec. 21(b), (c); Sec. 22(e)). "Proceedings to adopt an orphan may begin a month after the parents' death" → false: six months (Sec. 22(g)).
